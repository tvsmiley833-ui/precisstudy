Unit 1: History & Research Methods

Wilhelm Wundt
Founded the first psychology laboratory in Leipzig, Germany in 1879, marking psychology's birth as a formal science.
Structuralism
Early school of psychology (Titchener) that used introspection to break consciousness into its basic elements.
Functionalism
William James's approach focusing on how mental processes help organisms adapt and survive.
Independent Variable (IV)
The factor a researcher manipulates in an experiment to observe its effect.
Dependent Variable (DV)
The outcome variable that is measured in an experiment, expected to change due to the IV.
Random Assignment
Assigning participants to experimental conditions by chance, controlling for confounding variables.
Operational Definition
A precise, measurable description of how a variable is defined and measured in a study.
Correlation Coefficient
A statistic from -1.00 to +1.00 indicating the strength and direction of a relationship between variables.
Double-Blind Procedure
A study design where neither participants nor researchers know who received the treatment, reducing bias.
Informed Consent
An ethical requirement that participants agree to take part in research after learning what it involves.

Unit 2: Biological Bases

Neuron
The basic building block of the nervous system; transmits electrical and chemical signals.
Synapse
The tiny gap between neurons where neurotransmitters are released to relay a signal.
Action Potential
An all-or-none electrical impulse that travels down a neuron's axon when threshold is reached.
Dopamine
A neurotransmitter involved in reward, motivation, and movement; linked to Parkinson's and schizophrenia.
Amygdala
A limbic system structure central to processing fear and aggressive emotional responses.
Hippocampus
A limbic structure essential for forming new explicit (conscious) long-term memories.
Broca's Area
A frontal lobe region controlling speech production; damage causes halting, effortful speech.
Wernicke's Area
A temporal lobe region governing language comprehension; damage causes fluent, nonsensical speech.
Corpus Callosum
The band of neural fibers connecting the brain's left and right hemispheres.
Endocrine System
A network of glands that releases hormones into the bloodstream to influence body and behavior.

Unit 3: Sensation & Perception

Sensation
The bottom-up process by which sensory receptors detect physical stimulus energy.
Perception
The top-down process of organizing and interpreting sensory information into meaningful experience.
Absolute Threshold
The minimum stimulus intensity needed to detect a stimulus 50% of the time.
Weber's Law
The principle that a just noticeable difference is a constant proportion of the original stimulus.
Trichromatic Theory
The theory that three types of cones (red, green, blue) allow us to perceive color.
Opponent-Process Theory
The theory that color vision relies on paired opposing colors, explaining afterimages.
Place Theory
Theory of hearing stating different locations on the basilar membrane detect different (mainly high) pitches.
Gate-Control Theory
The theory that the spinal cord contains a neurological gate that can block or allow pain signals to the brain.
Gestalt Principles
Rules like proximity, similarity, and closure describing how the brain organizes sensory input into wholes.
Inattentional Blindness
Failing to notice a fully visible object because attention is focused elsewhere.

Unit 4: Learning

Classical Conditioning
Learning process in which a neutral stimulus becomes associated with an unconditioned stimulus to elicit a conditioned response.
Operant Conditioning
Learning process in which behavior is strengthened by reinforcement or weakened by punishment.
Extinction
The weakening of a learned response when the CS is no longer paired with the UCS, or a behavior is no longer reinforced.
Negative Reinforcement
Strengthening a behavior by removing an unpleasant/aversive stimulus after the behavior occurs.
Variable-Ratio Schedule
A reinforcement schedule delivering reward after an unpredictable number of responses; produces high, extinction-resistant rates.
Shaping
Gradually guiding behavior toward a target by reinforcing successive approximations.
Latent Learning
Learning that occurs without reinforcement and is not demonstrated until there is an incentive to do so.
Observational Learning
Learning by watching and imitating a model's behavior, as shown in Bandura's Bobo doll study.
Learned Helplessness
Passive resignation resulting from repeated exposure to uncontrollable negative events.
Biological Preparedness
The idea that organisms are evolutionarily predisposed to learn certain associations, like taste aversions, more easily.

Unit 5: Cognition & Memory

Working Memory
An active system for temporarily holding and manipulating information, including a central executive and other components (Baddeley).
Encoding
The process of getting information into the memory system.
Chunking
Grouping information into larger, meaningful units to increase memory capacity.
Levels of Processing
The idea that deeper, semantic processing of information leads to better long-term retention than shallow processing.
Episodic Memory
Explicit memory for personally experienced events tied to a specific time and place.
Procedural Memory
Implicit memory for learned skills and habits, such as riding a bicycle.
Misinformation Effect
Elizabeth Loftus's finding that exposure to misleading information after an event can distort memory of that event.
Serial Position Effect
The tendency to recall the first (primacy) and last (recency) items in a list better than middle items.
Availability Heuristic
Judging the likelihood of an event based on how easily examples come to mind.
Confirmation Bias
The tendency to search for and favor information that confirms one's preexisting beliefs.

Unit 6: Motivation, Emotion & Development

Drive-Reduction Theory
The theory that physiological needs create an aroused drive state motivating behavior to restore homeostasis.
Yerkes-Dodson Law
The principle that performance is optimal at a moderate level of arousal, varying with task difficulty.
James-Lange Theory
Theory of emotion proposing that physiological arousal occurs first, and the emotion follows from interpreting that arousal.
Schachter-Singer Two-Factor Theory
Theory proposing emotion results from both physiological arousal and a cognitive label applied to that arousal.
General Adaptation Syndrome
Hans Selye's model of the body's stress response in three stages: alarm, resistance, and exhaustion.
Object Permanence
The understanding, developed in Piaget's sensorimotor stage, that objects continue to exist even when out of sight.
Conservation
The understanding, mastered in Piaget's concrete operational stage, that quantity stays the same despite changes in shape or appearance.
Zone of Proximal Development
Vygotsky's concept for the range of tasks a child can perform with guidance but not yet independently.
Strange Situation
Ainsworth's research procedure used to identify infant attachment styles: secure, avoidant, and anxious/ambivalent.
Authoritative Parenting
A parenting style combining high warmth with high structure/expectations, linked to the best child outcomes.

Unit 7: Personality & Testing

Id
The unconscious personality structure that seeks immediate gratification, operating on the pleasure principle.
Ego
The largely conscious personality structure that mediates between the id and reality, operating on the reality principle.
Superego
The internalized moral component of personality that produces feelings of guilt or pride.
Defense Mechanisms
Unconscious ego strategies, like repression and projection, that reduce anxiety from unacceptable thoughts or impulses.
Unconditional Positive Regard
Carl Rogers's concept of accepting and valuing a person regardless of their behavior, fostering healthy self-concept.
Big Five Traits
The OCEAN model: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism.
Reciprocal Determinism
Bandura's concept that behavior, personal/cognitive factors, and environment continuously influence one another.
Self-Efficacy
One's belief in their own capability to succeed at a specific task or challenge.
Projective Test
A personality assessment, like the Rorschach inkblot test, using ambiguous stimuli to reveal unconscious content.
Reliability vs. Validity
Reliability is a test's consistency; validity is whether a test measures what it claims to measure.

Unit 8: Disorders & Social Psych

DSM-5
The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, the standard classification system for psychological disorders.
Diathesis-Stress Model
The theory that disorders develop when a biological predisposition interacts with environmental stress.
Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder
A disorder involving intrusive unwanted thoughts (obsessions) and repetitive behaviors performed to reduce anxiety (compulsions).
Major Depressive Disorder
A mood disorder involving persistent sadness and loss of interest or pleasure for at least two weeks.
Bipolar I Disorder
A mood disorder involving at least one full manic episode, often alternating with depressive episodes.
Positive Symptoms (Schizophrenia)
Added abnormal experiences in schizophrenia, such as hallucinations and delusions.
Negative Symptoms (Schizophrenia)
Diminished or absent normal functioning in schizophrenia, such as flat affect and social withdrawal.
Antisocial Personality Disorder
A personality disorder marked by persistent disregard for and violation of others' rights, often without remorse.
Milgram's Obedience Study
Classic research showing a majority of participants would obey an authority figure and deliver what they believed were dangerous shocks.
Bystander Effect
The finding that individuals are less likely to help a victim when other bystanders are present, due to diffusion of responsibility.

Unit 1: History & Research Methods

Perspectives in Psychology
Modern psychology explains behavior through several complementary lenses rather than one master theory.
History of Psychology
Psychology emerged as a science in the late 1800s and branched into competing schools of thought.
Research Methods
Psychologists choose research designs based on whether they want to describe, correlate, or establish cause and effect.
Experimental Design & Bias Control
Well-designed experiments guard against bias so that results reflect the true effect of the manipulated variable.
Statistics in Psychology
Psychologists use descriptive and inferential statistics to summarize data and judge whether findings are meaningful.
Ethics in Psychological Research
Ethical guidelines protect the rights and welfare of both human and animal research participants.
Key fact
Wilhelm Wundt's 1879 Leipzig lab marks the formal founding of psychology as a science.
Key fact
Only true experiments with random assignment can establish cause-and-effect relationships.
Key fact
Correlation does not equal causation — a third variable may explain an observed relationship.
Key fact
Informed consent, debriefing, and IRB approval are the cornerstones of ethical human research.

Unit 2: Biological Bases

Neurons & Neural Communication
Neurons are the basic building blocks of the nervous system, communicating via electrical and chemical signals.
The Nervous System
The nervous system is divided into the central and peripheral systems, each with specialized subdivisions.
The Brain's Structures
Different brain structures are specialized for different functions, from basic survival to complex thought.
The Cerebral Cortex
The cerebral cortex is the brain's outer layer responsible for higher-order thinking, divided into four lobes.
Brain Plasticity & Research Techniques
The brain can reorganize itself, and scientists use various tools to study its structure and function.
Genetics, Evolution & the Endocrine System
Behavior is shaped by heredity, evolutionary pressures, and hormones circulating through the bloodstream.
Key fact
Neural signaling is electrical within a neuron and chemical (neurotransmitters) between neurons.
Key fact
The brain's four cortical lobes each specialize: frontal (planning/motor), parietal (touch/space), occipital (vision), temporal (hearing/language).
Key fact
The sympathetic nervous system arouses the body for action; the parasympathetic calms it back down.
Key fact
fMRI shows brain activity (function); MRI/CT shows brain structure (anatomy) only.

Unit 3: Sensation & Perception

Basic Sensory Concepts
Sensation is the process of detecting stimuli, while perception is how the brain organizes and interprets that raw information.
Vision
The visual system converts light waves into neural signals that the brain interprets as color, form, and depth.
Hearing (Audition)
Hearing converts sound waves into neural signals that let us perceive pitch, loudness, and location.
Other Senses
Touch, taste, smell, and body-position senses provide crucial information about the body and environment.
Perceptual Organization
Gestalt psychologists showed that the brain actively organizes sensory input into meaningful wholes.
Attention & Perceptual Phenomena
Attention filters the vast amount of sensory information into what we consciously notice and process.
Key fact
Sensation is the raw detection of stimuli (bottom-up); perception is interpreting/organizing that data (top-down).
Key fact
Rods handle black-and-white/night vision; cones handle color and detail in bright light.
Key fact
Place theory explains high-pitched sound perception; frequency theory explains low-pitched sound perception.
Key fact
Gestalt grouping principles (proximity, similarity, closure) show the brain actively organizes sensory input into wholes.

Unit 4: Learning

Classical Conditioning
Classical conditioning, discovered by Ivan Pavlov, is a form of learning in which a neutral stimulus comes to trigger a response through association.
Operant Conditioning
Operant conditioning, developed by B.F. Skinner, explains how consequences shape voluntary behavior.
Schedules of Reinforcement
The pattern and timing of reinforcement affects how quickly a behavior is learned and how resistant it is to extinction.
Cognitive Influences on Learning
Learning is not always purely mechanical; cognitive factors like expectations, insight, and observation also shape behavior.
Observational Learning
Albert Bandura demonstrated that much learning occurs by watching and imitating others, without direct reinforcement.
Biological Constraints & Real-World Applications
Learning is shaped by an organism's biology, and conditioning principles have wide practical use.
Key fact
In classical conditioning, the organism learns an association between two stimuli (passive/involuntary response).
Key fact
In operant conditioning, the organism learns an association between a behavior and its consequence (active/voluntary behavior).
Key fact
Variable-ratio schedules produce the highest, most extinction-resistant response rates (e.g., slot machines).
Key fact
Observational learning (Bandura) shows behavior can be learned by watching a model, without direct reinforcement of the observer.

Unit 5: Cognition & Memory

Memory Models
Psychologists model memory as a multi-stage system that encodes, stores, and retrieves information.
Encoding Strategies
How information is encoded strongly affects how well it is later remembered.
Long-Term Memory Types
Long-term memory is not a single system, but is divided into distinct types with different brain bases.
Retrieval & Forgetting
Retrieval success depends on retrieval cues, while forgetting can occur at any stage of memory processing.
Memory Construction & Distortion
Memory is reconstructive, not a perfect recording, making it vulnerable to errors and distortion.
Thinking, Language & Problem Solving
Cognition involves how we form concepts, solve problems, make decisions, and use language.
Key fact
The Atkinson-Shiffrin model divides memory into sensory, short-term, and long-term stores.
Key fact
Deeper, meaningful (semantic) processing produces better long-term recall than shallow processing.
Key fact
The misinformation effect (Loftus) shows memory can be distorted by information encountered after an event.
Key fact
Heuristics are fast mental shortcuts that can produce systematic errors, unlike slower but reliable algorithms.

Unit 6: Motivation, Emotion & Development

Theories of Motivation
Several major theories explain why organisms are driven to act, from biological needs to psychological growth.
Hunger & Other Biological Motives
Hunger illustrates how biological, psychological, and social factors interact to drive motivated behavior.
Emotion Theories
Psychologists debate the order in which physiological arousal, cognitive appraisal, and subjective feeling occur during an emotional experience.
Stress & Health
Stress is the body's response to challenging or threatening events, with both physiological and psychological effects.
Developmental Psychology: Physical & Cognitive
Development follows predictable physical and cognitive patterns from infancy through adulthood.
Developmental Psychology: Social & Moral
Social and emotional development is shaped by early attachment, identity formation, and moral reasoning.
Key fact
The Yerkes-Dodson law shows optimal performance occurs at moderate arousal, varying with task difficulty.
Key fact
James-Lange says arousal precedes emotion; Cannon-Bard says they occur simultaneously; Schachter-Singer says both arousal and cognitive labeling are needed.
Key fact
Piaget's four stages (sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational, formal operational) describe qualitatively different ways of thinking at each age.
Key fact
Harlow's studies showed contact comfort, not just food, is essential to infant attachment.

Unit 7: Personality & Testing

Psychodynamic Theory
Sigmund Freud's psychodynamic theory proposes that personality is shaped by unconscious conflicts and childhood experiences.
Humanistic Theory
Humanistic psychologists emphasize personal growth, free will, and the drive toward self-actualization.
Trait Theory
Trait theorists describe personality using stable dimensions along which individuals differ, rather than dynamic processes.
Social-Cognitive Theory
Albert Bandura's social-cognitive perspective emphasizes the interaction between behavior, cognition, and environment.
Personality Assessment
Psychologists use a variety of tools to measure and study personality, each with distinct strengths and weaknesses.
Intelligence & Testing
Intelligence testing has a long, sometimes controversial history connected closely to personality assessment methods.
Key fact
Freud's structure of personality includes the id (pleasure principle), ego (reality principle), and superego (moral standards).
Key fact
The Big Five traits (OCEAN) are the most scientifically supported dimensional model of personality today.
Key fact
Bandura's reciprocal determinism holds that behavior, cognition, and environment continuously influence one another.
Key fact
Projective tests (like the Rorschach) have lower reliability/validity than objective self-report inventories (like the MMPI).

Unit 8: Disorders & Social Psych

Defining & Classifying Disorders
Psychological disorders are diagnosed using standardized criteria that identify dysfunction, distress, and deviation from norms.
Anxiety, OCD & Trauma-Related Disorders
This group of disorders involves distressing, persistent anxiety, intrusive thoughts, or reactions to trauma.
Mood Disorders
Mood disorders involve significant and prolonged disturbances in emotional state.
Schizophrenia & Other Psychotic Disorders
Schizophrenia is a severe disorder marked by a break from reality, involving disturbances in thought, perception, and behavior.
Personality & Dissociative Disorders
These disorders involve enduring, inflexible patterns of behavior or disruptions in identity, memory, and consciousness.
Treatment & Social Psychology
Treatment approaches vary widely, and social psychology explains how the presence and behavior of others shape our own actions.
Key fact
The DSM-5 provides the standardized diagnostic criteria clinicians use to classify psychological disorders.
Key fact
Schizophrenia's positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions) are additions to experience; negative symptoms (flat affect, withdrawal) are absences of normal functioning.
Key fact
Milgram's obedience studies showed most participants obeyed authority to administer what they believed were dangerous shocks.
Key fact
The diathesis-stress model explains disorders as arising from a biological predisposition combined with environmental stress.

Unit 1: History & Research Methods

Watch out
Correlation shows a relationship, not causation — a lurking third variable may be responsible, not X causing Y.
Watch out
Case studies provide rich detail about one individual, not generalizable data about a whole population.
Watch out
A large sample size does not fix a biased (non-random) sample — representativeness matters more than size.
Watch out
Statistical significance means a result is unlikely due to chance, not that the effect is large or practically important.

Unit 2: Biological Bases

Watch out
Broca's area controls speech production, not comprehension — that's Wernicke's area, not the reverse.
Watch out
Agonists enhance a neurotransmitter's effect; antagonists block it — don't reverse these terms.
Watch out
The action potential is all-or-none — it doesn't get 'stronger' with a more intense stimulus, only more frequent.
Watch out
The endocrine system uses hormones through the bloodstream (slow, long-lasting) — it does not directly fire electrical impulses like neurons.

Unit 3: Sensation & Perception

Watch out
Sensation and perception are not the same thing — sensation detects, perception interprets; don't use them interchangeably.
Watch out
Subliminal messages presented below threshold have not been shown to reliably or powerfully change behavior — that's a common myth, not fact.
Watch out
Weber's Law is about a constant proportion (percentage), not a constant fixed amount, needed for a noticeable difference.
Watch out
Inattentional blindness (missing something because you weren't looking) is different from change blindness (missing a change during a disruption).

Unit 4: Learning

Watch out
Negative reinforcement strengthens a behavior by removing something unpleasant — it is not the same as punishment, which weakens behavior.
Watch out
In classical conditioning, the CS never causes the UCR directly before conditioning — the response to the CS is only the CR, learned through association.
Watch out
Extinction is not 'forgetting' — the association can return via spontaneous recovery, showing it was only suppressed, not erased.
Watch out
Continuous reinforcement leads to fast extinction, while partial reinforcement leads to greater resistance to extinction — don't reverse these.

Unit 5: Cognition & Memory

Watch out
Short-term/working memory has a limited capacity of about 7±2 items — it is not unlimited like long-term memory.
Watch out
Recognition is typically easier than recall — being asked to recognize an answer among choices is not the same task as free recall.
Watch out
Flashbulb memories feel vivid and certain, but research shows they are not perfectly accurate — vividness does not equal accuracy.
Watch out
The availability heuristic and representativeness heuristic are different shortcuts — one is about ease of recall, the other about resemblance to a prototype.

Unit 6: Motivation, Emotion & Development

Watch out
James-Lange theory says the physical response causes the emotion, not the reverse — you feel fear because you're trembling, not the other way around.
Watch out
Object permanence develops in the sensorimotor stage; conservation develops later, in the concrete operational stage — don't confuse the two milestones.
Watch out
Authoritative parenting (high warmth AND high structure) is not the same as authoritarian parenting (high structure, low warmth) — these terms sound alike but describe very different styles.
Watch out
The General Adaptation Syndrome's stages are alarm, resistance, then exhaustion — exhaustion happens last, after the body's resources are depleted.

Unit 7: Personality & Testing

Watch out
The id seeks immediate gratification (pleasure principle); the ego deals with reality — don't mix up which structure does what.
Watch out
Reliability (consistency) and validity (measuring what it claims to) are not the same thing — a test can be reliable without being valid.
Watch out
Self-actualization is the top of Maslow's hierarchy, not a Freudian concept — don't attribute humanistic ideas to psychodynamic theory.
Watch out
Internal locus of control means believing YOU control outcomes; external locus of control means believing outside forces do — the names describe where control is believed to reside, not what type of person one is.

Unit 8: Disorders & Social Psych

Watch out
Antisocial personality disorder is not the same as being merely shy or introverted — it involves a persistent disregard for others' rights, often with manipulation or lack of remorse.
Watch out
Bipolar disorder involves manic episodes, not simply mood swings from good days to bad days — mania is a distinct, extreme, and impairing state.
Watch out
Hallucinations (false perceptions) and delusions (false beliefs) are both positive symptoms of schizophrenia — 'positive' here means added experiences, not 'good.'
Watch out
The bystander effect predicts LESS helping as more people are present, due to diffusion of responsibility — more witnesses does not mean more help.
