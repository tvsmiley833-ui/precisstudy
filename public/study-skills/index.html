Unit 1: How Memory Works3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Encoding, Storage and Retrieval
Memory has three stages, and studying can fail at any of them.
  • Encoding: getting information into memory. Paying attention and thinking about meaning helps
  • Storage: keeping information over time; sleep helps consolidate new memories
  • Retrieval: getting information back out when you need it, which is what tests measure
  • Working memory holds only a few items at once, so cramming too much at a time overloads it
  • Long-term memory is effectively unlimited, but information must be connected to be found again
The Forgetting Curve
Without review, we forget quickly, but each review slows forgetting.
  • Hermann Ebbinghaus (1885) found memory for new material drops steeply within the first day or two
  • Reviewing at spaced intervals flattens the curve, so knowledge lasts longer
  • Feeling like you 'know it' right after reading is often misleading: familiarity isn't the same as recall
  • Forgetting isn't failure: retrieving something you've partly forgotten strengthens memory more
  • Plan reviews before a test rather than one long session the night before
Making Memories Stick
Meaningful, connected information is easier to store and retrieve.
  • Elaboration: explain ideas in your own words and connect them to what you already know
  • Dual coding: combine words with visuals such as diagrams, timelines and sketches
  • Concrete examples make abstract ideas easier to remember
  • Mnemonics (acronyms, memory palaces) help for lists and sequences
  • Sleep: an all-nighter hurts both encoding the next day and consolidation of what you studied
Familiarity from rereading feels like learning but often isn't recall.
All-nighters trade sleep, which memory depends on, for extra study time.
Mnemonics help with lists but don't replace understanding concepts.
Unit 2: Active Recall & Spaced Repetition3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Retrieval Practice
Testing yourself is one of the most effective ways to learn, not just to measure learning.
  • The testing effect: practicing recall produces better long-term retention than restudying (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006)
  • Methods: flashcards, practice problems, blank-page recall, answering end-of-chapter questions without looking
  • Always check answers afterward: feedback corrects errors
  • A 2013 review by Dunlosky and colleagues rated practice testing as a high-utility technique
  • Low-stakes quizzes work; you don't need a grade for the effect
Spaced Repetition
Spreading study over time beats massing it into one session.
  • The spacing effect: the same total study time produces better long-term memory when spread out
  • Cramming can work for tomorrow's test but is forgotten quickly
  • Expanding intervals: review after a day, then a few days, then a week
  • Flashcard systems (Leitner boxes, spaced-repetition apps) automate the schedule
  • Distributed practice was also rated high-utility in the Dunlosky review
Interleaving
Mixing problem types trains you to choose the right method.
  • Blocked practice does many problems of one type in a row; interleaved practice mixes types
  • Interleaving feels harder but improves the ability to pick the right strategy on a test
  • It works especially well in math and science where problem types look similar
  • Combine with spacing: revisit old topics mixed in with new ones
  • 'Desirable difficulties' feel harder during practice but improve long-term learning
Cramming isn't useless for tomorrow, but it fails for the final exam and beyond.
Interleaving feels less productive during practice. That's normal.
Flashcards only help if you try to recall the answer before flipping.
Unit 3: Note-Taking Systems3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Why Notes Matter
Good notes help encoding during class and give you material for retrieval practice later.
  • Notes should capture main ideas, examples and connections, not every word
  • Writing in your own words forces processing, which improves encoding
  • Studies comparing laptop and handwritten notes suggest verbatim transcription is less effective than summarizing (Mueller & Oppenheimer, 2014), though later replications found smaller effects
  • Review notes within 24 hours to fill gaps while memory is fresh
  • Notes are raw material: turn them into questions to test yourself
The Cornell Method
A page layout built for review and self-testing.
  • Divide the page: a narrow cue column on the left, a wide notes column on the right, a summary area at the bottom
  • During class, take notes in the right column
  • After class, write questions or keywords in the cue column
  • Cover the notes and answer the cue questions from memory
  • Write a 2–3 sentence summary at the bottom of each page
Other Formats
Match the format to the material.
  • Outline method: hierarchical headings and subpoints; good for well-organized lectures
  • Mind maps: a central idea with branches; good for seeing connections
  • Charting/table method: columns for comparing items (e.g. causes, effects and dates of wars)
  • Sketchnotes combine drawings and words (dual coding)
  • Use abbreviations and symbols consistently to keep up with lectures
Copying slides word for word feels productive but involves little thinking.
Beautiful notes aren't the goal. Notes you test yourself from are.
Don't rely on one format for everything; comparisons fit charts better.
Unit 4: Time Management3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Planning
Seeing all your commitments in one place prevents last-minute crunches.
  • Use one planner or calendar for classes, tests, work, activities and deadlines
  • Break big projects into small tasks with their own due dates
  • Plan backward from due dates: what needs to be done by when?
  • Estimate time realistically: most people underestimate (the planning fallacy)
  • Schedule study blocks like appointments rather than hoping to find time
Prioritizing
Not every task deserves the same attention.
  • Eisenhower matrix: sort tasks by urgent/not urgent and important/not important
  • Do important and urgent tasks first; schedule important but not urgent ones before they become crises
  • The 'most important task' approach: pick 1–3 key tasks each day
  • Say no or delay low-importance commitments when overloaded
  • Review your plan weekly and adjust
Beating Procrastination
Procrastination is usually about avoiding an unpleasant feeling, not laziness.
  • Make starting easy: commit to just five minutes
  • Break vague tasks ('study bio') into concrete actions ('answer 10 practice questions on cell transport')
  • The Pomodoro Technique: 25 minutes of focused work, then a 5-minute break
  • Implementation intentions: 'If it is 4 p.m., then I will start my history outline'
  • Reward progress and track streaks to build momentum
Being busy isn't the same as being productive. Check that tasks are important.
A to-do list with vague items ('study') is easy to avoid.
Multitasking usually slows you down and increases errors.
Unit 5: Reading Textbooks Efficiently3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Before Reading
A quick preview gives you a map, so details have somewhere to go.
  • Survey the chapter: title, headings, bold terms, graphics, summary and review questions
  • Turn headings into questions ('What causes inflation?')
  • Activate prior knowledge: what do you already know about this topic?
  • Decide your purpose: an overview, specific answers, or deep understanding
  • SQ3R: Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review
While Reading
Active reading means thinking, not just moving your eyes.
  • Read one section at a time, then look away and recite the main idea
  • Annotate sparingly: short margin notes in your own words beat heavy highlighting
  • Study figures and tables; they often carry key ideas
  • Monitor understanding: if a paragraph made no sense, reread or look up a term
  • Highlighting everything is a low-utility strategy on its own (Dunlosky et al., 2013)
After Reading
Consolidate before moving on.
  • Answer the questions you wrote from the headings without looking
  • Summarize the chapter in a few sentences or a concept map
  • Make flashcards for key terms and processes
  • Connect the chapter to class notes and earlier chapters
  • Schedule a spaced review of the chapter
Reading every word once doesn't mean you learned it.
Highlighting feels active but often isn't. Pair it with recall.
Skipping graphs and tables skips important content.
Unit 6: Test-Taking Strategies3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Preparing
Test performance starts days before the test.
  • Find out the format (multiple choice, short answer, essay) and practice in that format
  • Take full practice tests under timed conditions
  • Review mistakes and understand why each wrong answer was wrong
  • Sleep the night before; retrieval is worse when you're sleep-deprived
  • Pack materials the night before to reduce morning stress
During the Test
Manage time and work strategically.
  • Skim the whole test first and budget time by point value
  • Answer easier questions first, marking hard ones to return to
  • Multiple choice: predict the answer before looking at choices, then eliminate wrong options
  • Watch for qualifiers like 'always', 'never', 'except' and 'not'
  • Essays: make a quick outline, state a clear thesis, and support it with specific evidence
Test Anxiety and Review
Some nervousness is normal, and there are proven ways to manage it.
  • Slow breathing (longer exhale than inhale) calms the body's stress response
  • Expressive writing about worries for 10 minutes before a test improved scores for anxious students in one study (Ramirez & Beilock, 2011)
  • Reframe arousal as excitement ('I'm ready') rather than fear
  • After a test, do an error analysis: content gap, careless mistake or misread question?
  • Changing an answer after careful reconsideration is more often right than wrong, despite the myth
'Always stick with your first answer' is a myth; reconsidered changes more often help than hurt.
Don't spend 10 minutes on a 1-point question.
Rereading notes the morning of the test is weaker than a quick self-quiz.
Unit 7: Focus & Distraction Management3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Why Attention Is Limited
Your brain can't truly focus on two demanding tasks at once.
  • 'Multitasking' is really rapid task-switching, which costs time and accuracy
  • After an interruption it can take a while to fully refocus
  • Notifications pull attention even when you don't respond
  • Studies have found that having a phone visible can reduce available attention, though effect sizes vary
  • Deep, uninterrupted focus produces more learning per hour than distracted study
Designing Your Environment
It's easier to change your surroundings than rely on willpower.
  • Put your phone in another room or use app blockers during study blocks
  • Close unneeded browser tabs and turn off notifications
  • Keep a consistent study spot associated with work
  • Have materials ready before you start to avoid 'quick' trips that turn into breaks
  • Instrumental music may help some people; lyrics tend to compete with reading
Managing Energy
Focus depends on your body as much as your intentions.
  • Schedule hard tasks when you're most alert
  • Take real breaks: move, stretch or get outside instead of scrolling
  • Regular sleep and exercise improve attention and mood
  • Stay hydrated and eat regular meals
  • Mindfulness practice can strengthen your ability to notice and redirect wandering attention
Feeling good at multitasking doesn't mean you are.
Scrolling isn't a restful break. It often extends into lost time.
Willpower alone fails; design the environment.
Unit 8: Goal Setting & Habits3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Setting Useful Goals
Clear goals direct effort and make progress visible.
  • SMART goals: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound
  • 'Raise my chemistry grade from 78% to 85% by the end of the quarter' beats 'do better in chemistry'
  • Process goals (study 30 minutes daily) are under your control; outcome goals (get an A) aren't fully
  • Break long-term goals into weekly targets
  • Track progress so you can adjust early
Building Habits
Habits make good study behavior automatic, so it doesn't depend on motivation.
  • Habit loop: cue → routine → reward
  • Habit stacking: attach a new habit to an existing one ('after dinner, I review flashcards')
  • Start small so the habit is easy to repeat
  • Research suggests habits take weeks to months to become automatic, and it varies by person and behavior
  • Missing one day doesn't break a habit; getting back on track quickly matters most
Mindset and Motivation
How you interpret setbacks affects what you do next.
  • Growth mindset (Dweck): believing abilities can improve with effort and strategy
  • Effective effort means changing strategies, not just working longer
  • Self-efficacy (Bandura): belief you can succeed, built through small wins
  • Intrinsic motivation (interest, mastery) tends to last longer than rewards alone
  • Reflect regularly: what worked, what didn't, what will I change?
'Just try harder' isn't a growth mindset. Change strategies.
The '21 days to form a habit' figure isn't supported by research; it varies widely.
Outcome goals alone can feel discouraging; pair them with process goals.
