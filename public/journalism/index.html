Unit 1: What Makes News5 concepts▾
News Values
Editors decide what becomes news by weighing a shared set of news values against each other; a story rarely needs all of them, but the strongest stories carry several at once.
  • Timeliness: recent events beat old ones — 'news' literally means new
  • Proximity: events closer to the audience (geographically or culturally) feel more relevant
  • Impact/consequence: how many people are affected and how seriously
  • Prominence: well-known people or institutions make ordinary events newsworthy
  • Conflict: disagreement, competition, or controversy draws attention
  • Human interest: emotionally engaging stories about ordinary people
Oddity and Novelty
Unusual or unexpected events are newsworthy precisely because they break from routine — 'dog bites man' is not news, but 'man bites dog' is.
  • Novelty covers firsts, records, and rare occurrences
  • Oddity relies on violating audience expectations
  • Overused novelty angles can trivialize serious topics
  • Editors must balance oddity against genuine significance
  • Novelty alone rarely justifies front-page placement without other values
The News Cycle
The news cycle is the ongoing process by which stories are reported, updated, and eventually replaced by newer developments.
  • 24-hour digital cycles have replaced the old once-daily print cycle
  • Breaking news gets updated continuously as facts emerge
  • Follow-up stories add context, reaction, and consequence over days
  • Social media can compress the cycle to minutes
  • Speed pressure increases the risk of reporting errors
Hard News vs. Soft News
Hard news covers timely, significant events (crime, politics, disasters); soft news covers lifestyle, entertainment, and human-interest topics with less time pressure.
  • Hard news is typically written in inverted-pyramid style
  • Soft news allows more narrative and creative structure
  • Hard news usually runs quickly; soft news can run anytime
  • Both require the same standards of accuracy and sourcing
  • The line between them blurs in feature-style hard-news coverage
Gatekeeping and Agenda-Setting
Editors act as gatekeepers, deciding which stories reach the public; by choosing what to cover, the press also shapes what audiences think about (agenda-setting), even if not what to think.
  • Gatekeeping happens at every stage: pitch, assignment, edit, and publish
  • Agenda-setting theory holds that media prominence signals importance to the public
  • Social media has distributed gatekeeping beyond traditional editors
  • News judgment reflects a newsroom's values and audience
  • Bias can enter through story selection, not just wording
Confusing 'interesting to me' with 'newsworthy' — newsworthiness depends on audience relevance, not personal curiosity
Treating oddity as sufficient on its own; unusual is not automatically important
Assuming proximity only means physical distance — cultural and community proximity count too
Forgetting that agenda-setting theory is about influencing what people think about, not what they think
Unit 2: News Writing & Inverted Pyramid6 concepts▾
The Inverted Pyramid
The inverted pyramid structures a news story from most to least important information, so editors can cut from the bottom and readers get the key facts immediately.
  • Most newsworthy facts appear first, least important last
  • Allows editors to trim length from the bottom without losing essentials
  • Suits skimming readers who may not finish the article
  • Contrasts with chronological or narrative storytelling structures
  • Originated partly from unreliable telegraph transmission during the Civil War era
The Lead (Lede)
The lead is the opening sentence or paragraph that summarizes the story's most essential information and hooks the reader.
  • A summary lead answers the most important of the Five Ws and H
  • Hard-news leads are typically one sentence, 25-35 words
  • Feature leads (delayed/anecdotal) can defer the main point for effect
  • A strong lead avoids burying the news under minor details
  • Leads should avoid starting with a quote or a question in hard news
The Five Ws and H
Who, What, When, Where, Why, and How are the essential questions every basic news story must answer.
  • Who: the people or organizations involved
  • What: the event or action that occurred
  • When and Where: the time and location
  • Why: the cause or motivation behind the event
  • How: the manner in which it happened
  • Not every W needs to be in the lead — prioritize the most newsworthy ones
Nut Graf and Body Structure
The nut graf (nutshell paragraph) follows the lead to explain why the story matters, then the body elaborates with supporting facts and quotes in descending importance.
  • Nut graf gives context and significance the lead couldn't fit
  • Body paragraphs should each carry one clear idea
  • Supporting quotes should reinforce, not just repeat, the facts
  • Background/context info sits lower since it's less time-sensitive
  • Transitions guide the reader logically between related facts
AP Style and News Writing Mechanics
AP Style is the standard style guide most U.S. newsrooms use for consistency in grammar, punctuation, abbreviations, and numerals.
  • Numbers one through nine are spelled out; 10 and above use numerals
  • Titles are capitalized before a name, lowercase after
  • Attribution typically uses 'said,' not more dramatic synonyms
  • Avoid editorializing adjectives; let facts speak for themselves
  • Consistent style builds credibility and reader trust
Attribution and Sourcing in Copy
Every factual claim in a news story should be attributed to a clear, identifiable source so readers can judge its credibility.
  • Direct quotes must be word-for-word accurate
  • Paraphrased information still needs attribution
  • Attribution should come early in a sentence when the source matters
  • Avoid anonymous sourcing unless justified and approved by editors
  • Never present opinion as unattributed fact in a news story
Burying the lead — putting the most important fact several paragraphs in, not right at the top
Writing a lead that tries to answer all six Ws at once instead of prioritizing the most newsworthy ones
Using dramatic attribution verbs like 'claimed' or 'admitted' instead of the neutral 'said'
Treating the nut graf as optional filler rather than the paragraph that justifies the story's importance
Unit 3: Interviewing Skills5 concepts▾
Preparing for an Interview
Strong interviews start before the recorder turns on: research the subject, define your goal, and draft flexible questions.
  • Research the subject's background, prior statements, and public record
  • Identify the story's central question the interview must answer
  • Draft open-ended questions but stay ready to improvise
  • Decide on setting, timing, and recording method in advance
  • Anticipate sensitive topics and plan how to raise them respectfully
Open vs. Closed Questions
Open-ended questions invite detailed, reflective answers, while closed questions elicit short factual confirmations — skilled interviewers use both strategically.
  • Open questions ('How did that feel?') encourage elaboration and quotes
  • Closed questions ('Did you sign the contract?') confirm specific facts
  • Leading questions that suggest an answer should be avoided
  • Follow-up questions probe deeper into unclear or evasive answers
  • Silence after a question can prompt a source to keep talking
Active Listening and Follow-Up
Effective interviewers listen more than they talk, catching details that spark better follow-up questions than any pre-written list.
  • Maintain eye contact and minimal interruptions to build rapport
  • Note surprising or vague statements for immediate follow-up
  • Avoid rigidly following a script when a better angle emerges
  • Paraphrasing an answer back can confirm understanding and get clarity
  • Take detailed notes even when recording, in case of technical failure
Handling Difficult Sources
Evasive, hostile, or emotionally distressed sources require patience, tact, and persistence without crossing ethical lines.
  • Rephrase a dodged question rather than abandoning it
  • Stay calm and professional even if a source becomes defensive
  • Give grieving or traumatized sources space and let them decline questions
  • Avoid badgering; repeated respectful follow-ups work better than pressure
  • Know when to end an interview that has become unproductive or unsafe
On/Off the Record and Attribution Levels
Sources and reporters must agree clearly on ground rules before information is shared, since these terms govern how it can be used.
  • On the record: everything can be quoted and attributed by name
  • On background: information can be used but not attributed to the named source
  • On deep background: information can be used but with no attribution at all
  • Off the record: information cannot be published at all
  • Ground rules must be agreed upon before the information is shared, not after
Asking leading questions that suggest the desired answer instead of neutral open questions
Assuming 'off the record' means the same as 'not for attribution' — they are different levels
Sticking rigidly to a prepared question list instead of following up on unexpected answers
Agreeing to off-the-record terms after the source has already revealed the information
Unit 4: Fact-Checking & Ethics5 concepts▾
The SPJ Code of Ethics
The Society of Professional Journalists' Code of Ethics organizes journalistic responsibility into four core principles that guide everyday newsroom decisions.
  • Seek truth and report it: verify information and provide context
  • Minimize harm: treat sources, subjects, and the vulnerable with compassion
  • Act independently: avoid conflicts of interest and outside influence
  • Be accountable and transparent: acknowledge errors and explain decisions
  • The code is a set of aspirational guidelines, not enforceable law
Verification and Fact-Checking Process
Before publishing, journalists must independently confirm claims using multiple, credible, ideally primary sources.
  • Cross-check facts with at least two independent sources when possible
  • Prefer primary sources (documents, direct witnesses) over secondary ones
  • Verify names, titles, dates, numbers, and quotes exactly
  • Be skeptical of viral or unverified social media claims
  • Document sourcing so claims can be defended if challenged
Conflicts of Interest
A conflict of interest arises when a journalist's personal relationships, financial ties, or biases could compromise — or appear to compromise — their objectivity.
  • Financial ties to a story's subject must be disclosed or avoided
  • Personal relationships with sources can create real or perceived bias
  • Journalists generally avoid political activism related to their beat
  • Gifts or favors from sources should be declined
  • Even the appearance of a conflict can damage credibility
Plagiarism and Fabrication
Plagiarism (using others' work without credit) and fabrication (inventing facts, quotes, or sources) are among journalism's most serious ethical violations.
  • Plagiarism includes copying text, ideas, or structure without attribution
  • Fabrication includes invented quotes, composite characters, or fake events
  • Both violations can end careers and destroy outlet credibility
  • Proper attribution and original reporting prevent plagiarism
  • Corrections cannot fix fabrication — it requires retraction and accountability
Corrections and Accountability
When errors occur, credible news organizations promptly and transparently correct the record rather than quietly editing or ignoring mistakes.
  • Corrections should be clearly labeled and explain what was wrong
  • Minor typos may be fixed silently; substantive errors require a formal correction
  • Transparency about mistakes builds long-term audience trust
  • Ombudsmen or public editors at some outlets review complaints independently
  • Failing to correct known errors is itself an ethical failure
Confusing plagiarism (uncredited copying) with fabrication (inventing information) — both are serious but distinct violations
Assuming a single source is enough verification for a significant or controversial claim
Believing the SPJ Code of Ethics is legally binding — it is a professional guideline, not law
Quietly fixing a substantive factual error without publishing a formal correction
Unit 5: Feature Writing5 concepts▾
Narrative Structure
Unlike hard news, features are usually built as stories with a beginning, middle, and end rather than an inverted pyramid — information is released for dramatic and emotional effect, not just efficiency.
  • The 'hourglass' and narrative arc structures replace the inverted pyramid for many features
  • A strong lede (anecdotal, scene-setting, or descriptive) draws the reader in before the nut graf explains why the story matters
  • The nut graf (or 'nut paragraph') tells the reader the point of the story after the narrative hook
  • Scenes, chronology, and character development pace the story like fiction, even though the facts must be true
  • The kicker — a strong final quote, image, or detail — closes the story with resonance
  • Foreshadowing and callbacks to the opening scene give features a sense of unity
Human-Interest Angles
Human-interest features find the personal, emotional dimension of a larger issue by focusing on how it affects a specific person or small group.
  • A human-interest angle uses one person's experience as a lens onto a bigger trend or issue
  • Profiles are a common feature form, built around a single subject's life, work, or personality
  • Empathy and specificity — names, details, dialogue — make readers care about the subject
  • Reporters must balance emotional appeal with factual accuracy; a moving story is not an excuse to embellish
  • Human-interest stories often run alongside hard-news coverage of the same event to add context and emotional depth
Descriptive Detail and Scene-Setting
Features rely on sensory, specific detail to put readers in a scene, rather than the general summary language typical of straight news writing.
  • Show, don't tell: concrete sensory details (sight, sound, smell) work better than abstract adjectives
  • Specific details (a person's cracked hands, a particular smell in a room) are more credible and vivid than vague ones
  • Description should serve the story's point, not just decorate it
  • Dialogue and direct quotation, reproduced accurately, bring scenes to life
  • Overwriting — piling on adjectives or purple prose — can undermine credibility and clarity
Types of Feature Stories
Feature writing covers several recognizable subgenres, each with its own conventions.
  • Profile features focus on one person's life, achievements, or personality
  • Trend stories identify and illustrate a pattern happening across many people or places
  • Human-interest features center on emotionally engaging personal stories
  • Explanatory/backgrounder features unpack the context behind a complex ongoing story
  • How-to and service features give readers practical, actionable information
  • Seasonal or 'evergreen' features are not tied to a specific news event and can run anytime
Reporting for Features
Because features rely on scene and character, they typically demand more time-intensive reporting than a daily news story.
  • Feature reporters spend extended time observing subjects to gather authentic scenes and quotes
  • Detailed notes on setting, appearance, and behavior support later descriptive writing
  • Multiple interviews, sometimes over weeks, build a fuller picture of a subject
  • Fact-checking remains as rigorous as hard news — narrative style never excuses inaccuracy
  • Feature writers still need a clear focus or theme (a 'nut graf' idea) before drafting, to avoid a meandering story
A feature can bury the lede with a long anecdote and no nut graf, not skip factual verification — accuracy standards do not relax in feature writing.
Descriptive detail should be concrete and specific ('rust-streaked gutters'), not vague adjectives ('a nice old house').
A kicker is the story's closing line or image, not a quick summary paragraph like a hard-news story's ending.
Human interest means finding the personal angle on real events, not fabricating composite characters or invented scenes for effect.
Unit 6: Opinion vs News5 concepts▾
Fact vs. Opinion
A fact is a statement that can be verified as true or false with evidence; an opinion expresses a belief, judgment, or interpretation that cannot be proven the same way.
  • Facts are verifiable through documents, data, records, or direct observation
  • Opinions rely on values, preferences, or interpretation and can vary between people
  • News reporting should stick to verifiable facts and attributed claims, not the reporter's judgments
  • Opinion writing is explicitly labeled as opinion, editorial, or commentary so readers know the difference
  • A statement can mix fact and opinion, e.g. 'the mayor raised taxes' (fact) 'which was reckless' (opinion)
Editorials and the Editorial Board
An editorial is an unsigned opinion piece that represents the official position of a publication, typically written or approved by an editorial board separate from the newsroom.
  • Editorials are institutional opinions, not the view of any single reporter
  • Most publications keep a wall of separation between the editorial board and the news reporting staff
  • Editorials often argue for a specific action or position, such as endorsing a candidate or policy
  • They typically appear on a dedicated 'opinion' or 'editorial' page, clearly separated from news
  • Editorial boards may include publishers, senior editors, and sometimes community members
Op-Eds and Columns
An op-ed ('opposite the editorial page') is a signed opinion piece written by someone outside the editorial board, while a column is a recurring, signed piece reflecting one writer's consistent viewpoint.
  • Op-eds are typically submitted by outside experts, politicians, or public figures, not staff editors
  • Columns run regularly and are tied to one writer's byline and voice
  • Both op-eds and columns must be clearly labeled as opinion content
  • Op-ed writers are still expected to support their arguments with accurate facts and evidence
  • News organizations often have separate editorial standards and fact-checking processes for opinion content
Objectivity and Balance
Objectivity is the journalistic standard of reporting facts without personal bias, while balance means fairly representing relevant perspectives on a controversy.
  • Objectivity does not mean 'no viewpoint exists' — it means the reporter's own opinion should not shape the reporting
  • Balance requires including significant, relevant viewpoints, not necessarily equal space to every claim
  • False balance occurs when a fringe or unsupported view is given equal weight to a well-evidenced one
  • Objectivity is upheld through sourcing, attribution, and separating fact from interpretation
  • Some newsrooms now favor 'fairness' or 'accuracy' over strict objectivity, while still avoiding advocacy in news stories
Recognizing Bias in Coverage
Bias can enter news coverage through word choice, story selection, source selection, and framing, even without an explicit statement of opinion.
  • Loaded language (e.g., 'regime' vs. 'government') can signal bias without stating an opinion directly
  • Story selection bias occurs when certain topics are consistently covered or ignored
  • Source selection bias happens when only one side's experts or witnesses are quoted
  • Framing bias shapes how an issue is presented, such as emphasizing conflict over cooperation
  • Critical readers check for attribution, multiple sources, and loaded language to spot bias
  • Confirmation bias in audiences can make biased coverage feel 'objective' if it matches existing beliefs
An editorial reflects the publication's institutional stance, not the personal opinion of an individual reporter.
Objectivity means keeping the reporter's own opinion out of the story, not pretending that no legitimate viewpoints exist.
Balance means including significant, relevant perspectives, not giving equal weight to a fringe claim and a well-evidenced one (false balance).
Op-eds must still be factually accurate; being labeled 'opinion' permits argument and interpretation, not fabrication.
Unit 7: Media Law Basics5 concepts▾
Libel and Slander (Defamation)
Defamation is a false statement presented as fact that damages someone's reputation; libel is written/published defamation, while slander is spoken defamation.
  • To prove defamation, a plaintiff generally must show a false statement of fact, publication to a third party, identification, and damage to reputation
  • Truth is an absolute defense against a defamation claim in the U.S.
  • Public officials and public figures must prove 'actual malice' — that the publisher knew the statement was false or acted with reckless disregard for the truth (New York Times v. Sullivan, 1964)
  • Private individuals generally only need to prove negligence, a lower standard than actual malice
  • Opinion, clearly labeled as such, is generally protected and cannot be defamatory if it doesn't imply false facts
  • Retractions and corrections can limit damages but do not automatically erase liability
Shield Laws and Protecting Sources
Shield laws protect journalists from being legally compelled to reveal confidential sources or unpublished material in court.
  • Shield laws vary by state; there is no comprehensive federal shield law in the U.S.
  • They exist to protect the free flow of information by letting sources speak without fear of exposure
  • Protections are not absolute — courts can sometimes compel disclosure in criminal cases or when no other source can provide the information
  • Confidentiality promises to sources are treated as serious ethical commitments even when legal protection is limited
  • Reporters who refuse court orders to reveal sources can face contempt of court charges, including fines or jail time
Prior Restraint
Prior restraint is government action that blocks speech or publication before it happens, rather than punishing it afterward; U.S. courts treat it as the most serious First Amendment violation.
  • The Supreme Court has held prior restraint is presumptively unconstitutional and requires an extremely high burden of justification
  • New York Times Co. v. United States (1971, the 'Pentagon Papers' case) rejected the government's attempt to block publication of classified material
  • Courts generally prefer to let material be published and pursue after-the-fact remedies (like defamation suits) rather than censor in advance
  • National security claims alone are not automatically sufficient to justify prior restraint
  • Prior restraint differs from post-publication liability, which punishes a publisher after the fact for content already published
Freedom of Information Act (FOIA)
FOIA is a federal law giving the public and press the right to request access to records held by federal government agencies, with state-level equivalents for state and local records.
  • FOIA requests can be filed by anyone, not just journalists, to obtain government records
  • Agencies must generally respond within a set statutory timeframe, though delays are common in practice
  • FOIA includes specific exemptions, such as national security, personal privacy, and law enforcement investigations
  • State versions of FOIA (often called 'sunshine laws' or open-records laws) apply to state and local government records
  • FOIA is a key investigative journalism tool for uncovering government documents, data, and communications
Privacy Torts and Newsgathering Limits
Beyond defamation, journalists must navigate privacy law and other legal limits when gathering and publishing information about individuals.
  • Invasion of privacy can include intrusion upon seclusion, public disclosure of private facts, and false light
  • Newsworthiness is a key defense: information of legitimate public concern is generally protected even if private
  • Trespassing or using hidden recording devices to gather news can create separate legal liability, distinct from what is ultimately published
  • Many states have laws governing consent for recording conversations (one-party vs. two-party consent states)
  • Copyright law limits the unauthorized reuse of others' photos, video, or text, separate from defamation or privacy concerns
Libel is written defamation and slander is spoken defamation, not interchangeable terms for the same thing.
Public figures must prove actual malice, not mere negligence — private individuals face the lower negligence standard, not the other way around.
Shield laws vary by state and are not absolute; there is no comprehensive federal shield law protecting all journalists in all situations.
FOIA gives a right to request records, not an unconditional right to receive them — exemptions like national security and privacy can justify withholding.
Unit 8: Digital Storytelling5 concepts▾
Multimedia Storytelling Formats
Digital journalism combines text, photography, video, audio, and interactive graphics to tell stories in the format best suited to the content, rather than relying on text alone.
  • Multimedia packages let editors choose the best medium for each part of a story (e.g., video for action, graphics for data, text for analysis)
  • Interactive graphics let readers explore data or scenarios themselves, such as maps or searchable databases
  • Live blogs provide continuously updated, chronological coverage of breaking events
  • Podcasts and audio journalism allow long-form, voice-driven storytelling distinct from written articles
  • Mobile-first design considerations (short paragraphs, vertical video, fast load times) shape how digital stories are built
SEO Basics for Journalists
Search engine optimization (SEO) is the practice of structuring content so it is more easily found through search engines, helping journalism reach readers who are actively searching for information.
  • Clear, descriptive headlines that include relevant keywords help search engines and readers understand a story's topic
  • Metadata (title tags, meta descriptions) summarizes a page's content for search engine results pages
  • Structuring articles with subheadings and clear paragraphs improves readability and searchability
  • SEO should never override accuracy or ethics — 'clickbait' headlines that mislead readers damage credibility
  • Internal links to related stories keep readers on a site longer and helps search engines understand site structure
  • Search intent (what a searcher is actually trying to find) should guide how a story is framed and titled
Social Media Journalism Ethics
Social media has become both a newsgathering tool and a publishing platform, raising ethical questions about verification, transparency, and a journalist's personal versus professional voice.
  • Content found on social media (photos, videos, claims) must still be verified before republication, just like any other source
  • Reverse image searches and metadata checks help confirm whether social media content is authentic and correctly dated
  • Journalists must consider how personal opinions posted on social media can affect perceptions of their outlet's objectivity
  • Many newsrooms have explicit social media policies governing what staff can post, especially about topics they cover
  • Engaging with audiences on social media (replying, correcting errors publicly) is part of modern accountability journalism
  • Speed pressure on social media increases the risk of spreading unverified information or misinformation
Data Journalism and Interactive Visualization
Data journalism uses data analysis and visualization to uncover and communicate stories, often paired with interactive tools that let readers investigate the data themselves.
  • Data journalists must verify the source, methodology, and limitations of any dataset before publishing conclusions from it
  • Visualizations (charts, maps, timelines) should accurately represent data without misleading scale or cherry-picked ranges
  • Interactive tools can let readers filter data relevant to their own location or interests
  • Open data portals and FOIA requests are common sources for original data journalism projects
  • Clear sourcing and methodology notes let readers evaluate a data story's reliability
Digital Ethics and Verification Tools
Digital storytelling introduces new verification challenges, from manipulated images to AI-generated content, requiring updated tools and standards.
  • Reverse image search tools help detect recycled or out-of-context photos and videos
  • Deepfakes and AI-generated images/video pose a growing verification challenge for newsrooms
  • Digital manipulation of photos (beyond basic cropping/color correction) violates most newsroom photo ethics policies
  • Corrections and updates should be clearly marked and timestamped on digital articles, since online content can be edited invisibly
  • Archiving original versions of digital content (via screenshots or web archiving tools) helps preserve evidence before it can be altered or deleted
SEO means optimizing accurate, clear headlines for discoverability, not writing misleading clickbait that inflates clicks at the expense of accuracy.
Content found on social media still requires verification before being republished — it is not automatically trustworthy just because it is public.
A journalist's personal social media posts can still affect perceptions of their outlet's objectivity, so 'personal opinion' is not fully separate from professional reporting.
Digital corrections must be clearly marked and dated; quietly editing a published article without disclosure undermines transparency, not just style.

Framing Bias
Bias introduced by how a story's context or emphasis shapes reader interpretation, such as stressing conflict over cooperation.
Attribution
Identifying the source of a claim or quote so readers can judge its reliability, a core tool for maintaining objectivity.
Fact
A statement that can be verified as true or false using evidence, records, or direct observation.

Spoken defamation — a false oral statement of fact that damages a person's reputation.

Defamation
A false statement presented as fact that harms someone's reputation, encompassing both libel and slander.

A state-level open-records or open-meetings law giving the public access to state and local government records and proceedings.
False Light
A privacy tort in which a publication portrays someone in a misleading way that would be highly offensive to a reasonable person, even without literal defamation.

Unit 1: What Makes News

News Values
Editors decide what becomes news by weighing a shared set of news values against each other; a story rarely needs all of them, but the strongest stories carry several at once.
Oddity and Novelty
Unusual or unexpected events are newsworthy precisely because they break from routine — 'dog bites man' is not news, but 'man bites dog' is.
The News Cycle
The news cycle is the ongoing process by which stories are reported, updated, and eventually replaced by newer developments.
Hard News vs. Soft News
Hard news covers timely, significant events (crime, politics, disasters); soft news covers lifestyle, entertainment, and human-interest topics with less time pressure.
Gatekeeping and Agenda-Setting
Editors act as gatekeepers, deciding which stories reach the public; by choosing what to cover, the press also shapes what audiences think about (agenda-setting), even if not what to think.
Key fact
The core news values are timeliness, proximity, impact, prominence, conflict, novelty, and human interest.
Key fact
A story's newsworthiness usually comes from combining several values, not just one.
Key fact
Gatekeeping is the editorial process of deciding which stories get published.
Key fact
Agenda-setting theory says media coverage influences what topics the public considers important.

Unit 2: News Writing & Inverted Pyramid

The Inverted Pyramid
The inverted pyramid structures a news story from most to least important information, so editors can cut from the bottom and readers get the key facts immediately.
The Lead (Lede)
The lead is the opening sentence or paragraph that summarizes the story's most essential information and hooks the reader.
The Five Ws and H
Who, What, When, Where, Why, and How are the essential questions every basic news story must answer.
Nut Graf and Body Structure
The nut graf (nutshell paragraph) follows the lead to explain why the story matters, then the body elaborates with supporting facts and quotes in descending importance.
AP Style and News Writing Mechanics
AP Style is the standard style guide most U.S. newsrooms use for consistency in grammar, punctuation, abbreviations, and numerals.
Attribution and Sourcing in Copy
Every factual claim in a news story should be attributed to a clear, identifiable source so readers can judge its credibility.
Key fact
The inverted pyramid places the most newsworthy information first, least important last.
Key fact
A hard-news lead should answer the most important of the Five Ws and H in one concise sentence.
Key fact
The nut graf explains why a story matters, right after the lead.
Key fact
AP Style provides the standardized grammar, punctuation, and formatting rules most U.S. newsrooms follow.

Unit 3: Interviewing Skills

Preparing for an Interview
Strong interviews start before the recorder turns on: research the subject, define your goal, and draft flexible questions.
Open vs. Closed Questions
Open-ended questions invite detailed, reflective answers, while closed questions elicit short factual confirmations — skilled interviewers use both strategically.
Active Listening and Follow-Up
Effective interviewers listen more than they talk, catching details that spark better follow-up questions than any pre-written list.
Handling Difficult Sources
Evasive, hostile, or emotionally distressed sources require patience, tact, and persistence without crossing ethical lines.
On/Off the Record and Attribution Levels
Sources and reporters must agree clearly on ground rules before information is shared, since these terms govern how it can be used.
Key fact
Effective interviews rely on preparation: research the subject and identify the story's key question beforehand.
Key fact
Open-ended questions draw out detail and reflection; closed questions confirm specific facts.
Key fact
The four attribution levels are on the record, on background, on deep background, and off the record.
Key fact
Ground rules for attribution must be negotiated before information is shared, not retroactively.

Unit 4: Fact-Checking & Ethics

The SPJ Code of Ethics
The Society of Professional Journalists' Code of Ethics organizes journalistic responsibility into four core principles that guide everyday newsroom decisions.
Verification and Fact-Checking Process
Before publishing, journalists must independently confirm claims using multiple, credible, ideally primary sources.
Conflicts of Interest
A conflict of interest arises when a journalist's personal relationships, financial ties, or biases could compromise — or appear to compromise — their objectivity.
Plagiarism and Fabrication
Plagiarism (using others' work without credit) and fabrication (inventing facts, quotes, or sources) are among journalism's most serious ethical violations.
Corrections and Accountability
When errors occur, credible news organizations promptly and transparently correct the record rather than quietly editing or ignoring mistakes.
Key fact
The SPJ Code of Ethics rests on four pillars: seek truth and report it, minimize harm, act independently, and be accountable and transparent.
Key fact
Facts should be verified with multiple independent, preferably primary, sources before publication.
Key fact
A conflict of interest can damage credibility even if it's only a perceived, not actual, bias.
Key fact
Fabrication (inventing facts or quotes) is a more severe violation than a simple factual error and typically ends careers.

Unit 5: Feature Writing

Narrative Structure
Unlike hard news, features are usually built as stories with a beginning, middle, and end rather than an inverted pyramid — information is released for dramatic and emotional effect, not just efficiency.
Human-Interest Angles
Human-interest features find the personal, emotional dimension of a larger issue by focusing on how it affects a specific person or small group.
Descriptive Detail and Scene-Setting
Features rely on sensory, specific detail to put readers in a scene, rather than the general summary language typical of straight news writing.
Types of Feature Stories
Feature writing covers several recognizable subgenres, each with its own conventions.
Reporting for Features
Because features rely on scene and character, they typically demand more time-intensive reporting than a daily news story.
Key fact
The inverted pyramid is replaced in features by narrative structures like the hourglass, where an anecdotal lede leads into a nut graf that states the story's significance.
Key fact
A 'nut graf' explains why the story matters and what it is really about, usually placed after the opening scene or anecdote.
Key fact
Human-interest stories use one person's specific experience to illuminate a broader issue or trend.
Key fact
Features demand the same factual accuracy as hard news; vivid, specific detail — not invented detail — creates believability.

Unit 6: Opinion vs News

Fact vs. Opinion
A fact is a statement that can be verified as true or false with evidence; an opinion expresses a belief, judgment, or interpretation that cannot be proven the same way.
Editorials and the Editorial Board
An editorial is an unsigned opinion piece that represents the official position of a publication, typically written or approved by an editorial board separate from the newsroom.
Op-Eds and Columns
An op-ed ('opposite the editorial page') is a signed opinion piece written by someone outside the editorial board, while a column is a recurring, signed piece reflecting one writer's consistent viewpoint.
Objectivity and Balance
Objectivity is the journalistic standard of reporting facts without personal bias, while balance means fairly representing relevant perspectives on a controversy.
Recognizing Bias in Coverage
Bias can enter news coverage through word choice, story selection, source selection, and framing, even without an explicit statement of opinion.
Key fact
News reporting presents verifiable, attributed facts; opinion content (editorials, op-eds, columns) is explicitly labeled and argues a viewpoint.
Key fact
An editorial represents a publication's institutional position, decided by an editorial board, separate from the newsroom that reports the news.
Key fact
An op-ed is a signed opinion piece from someone outside the publication's staff; a column is a recurring signed piece by one writer.
Key fact
Bias can appear in reporting through word choice, story selection, source selection, and framing, even when no explicit opinion is stated.

Unit 7: Media Law Basics

Libel and Slander (Defamation)
Defamation is a false statement presented as fact that damages someone's reputation; libel is written/published defamation, while slander is spoken defamation.
Shield Laws and Protecting Sources
Shield laws protect journalists from being legally compelled to reveal confidential sources or unpublished material in court.
Prior Restraint
Prior restraint is government action that blocks speech or publication before it happens, rather than punishing it afterward; U.S. courts treat it as the most serious First Amendment violation.
Freedom of Information Act (FOIA)
FOIA is a federal law giving the public and press the right to request access to records held by federal government agencies, with state-level equivalents for state and local records.
Privacy Torts and Newsgathering Limits
Beyond defamation, journalists must navigate privacy law and other legal limits when gathering and publishing information about individuals.
Key fact
Libel is written defamation; slander is spoken defamation. Truth is an absolute defense to both.
Key fact
Public officials and public figures must prove 'actual malice' (knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth) to win a defamation case, per New York Times v. Sullivan (1964).
Key fact
Prior restraint — blocking publication before it happens — is presumptively unconstitutional and was rejected in the Pentagon Papers case, New York Times Co. v. United States (1971).
Key fact
FOIA lets the public and press request federal government records, subject to specific exemptions; state open-records laws cover state and local records.

Unit 8: Digital Storytelling

Multimedia Storytelling Formats
Digital journalism combines text, photography, video, audio, and interactive graphics to tell stories in the format best suited to the content, rather than relying on text alone.
SEO Basics for Journalists
Search engine optimization (SEO) is the practice of structuring content so it is more easily found through search engines, helping journalism reach readers who are actively searching for information.
Social Media Journalism Ethics
Social media has become both a newsgathering tool and a publishing platform, raising ethical questions about verification, transparency, and a journalist's personal versus professional voice.
Data Journalism and Interactive Visualization
Data journalism uses data analysis and visualization to uncover and communicate stories, often paired with interactive tools that let readers investigate the data themselves.
Digital Ethics and Verification Tools
Digital storytelling introduces new verification challenges, from manipulated images to AI-generated content, requiring updated tools and standards.
Key fact
Multimedia storytelling matches each part of a story to its best format — video, interactive graphics, audio, or text — rather than defaulting to text alone.
Key fact
SEO means writing clear, keyword-relevant headlines and structured content so search engines and readers can find accurate journalism, without resorting to misleading clickbait.
Key fact
Content sourced from social media must be independently verified (e.g., via reverse image search) before republication, exactly like any other source.
Key fact
Digital corrections should be clearly marked and timestamped, since online articles can otherwise be edited invisibly after publication.
