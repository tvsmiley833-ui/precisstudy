Unit 1: Sociological Perspective3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

The Sociological Imagination

Sociology studies how society shapes individual lives, and how individuals shape society.
  • C. Wright Mills coined 'the sociological imagination': connecting personal troubles to public issues
  • One person unemployed is a personal trouble; millions unemployed is a public issue rooted in the economy
  • Sociologists look for patterns in behavior rather than explaining events only by individual choice
  • Auguste Comte coined the word 'sociology' and argued society could be studied scientifically (positivism)
  • Sociology overlaps with psychology, economics and anthropology but focuses on groups and social structure

Founders

Several 19th- and early 20th-century thinkers set the questions sociology still asks.
  • Émile Durkheim: social facts, social solidarity, and his study showing suicide rates vary with social integration
  • Karl Marx: society is shaped by conflict between classes over the means of production
  • Max Weber: verstehen (understanding meaning), bureaucracy, and the Protestant ethic's link to capitalism
  • Harriet Martineau translated Comte and studied social life and women's roles
  • W. E. B. Du Bois studied race in America and described 'double consciousness'

Theoretical Perspectives

Three major lenses ask different questions about the same social fact.
  • Structural functionalism: society is a system of parts that work together to keep stability (Durkheim, Parsons, Merton)
  • Merton: manifest functions are intended; latent functions are unintended (school also provides childcare)
  • Conflict theory: society is shaped by inequality and competition for resources (Marx)
  • Symbolic interactionism: society is built through everyday interaction and shared meanings (Mead, Blumer)
  • Macro perspectives (functionalism, conflict) study large structures; micro (interactionism) studies face-to-face interaction
Sociology isn't just 'common sense'. Research often contradicts assumptions.
Conflict theory comes from Marx; functionalism from Durkheim, not the other way around.
Symbolic interactionism is a micro-level perspective.

Unit 2: Research Methods3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

The Research Process

Sociologists use systematic methods to test ideas about society.
  • Steps: choose a topic, review the literature, form a hypothesis, choose a method, collect data, analyze, report
  • A hypothesis predicts a relationship between an independent variable (cause) and a dependent variable (effect)
  • Operational definitions state exactly how a concept will be measured
  • Reliability means consistent measurement; validity means measuring what you intend to measure
  • Correlation isn't causation: a spurious correlation may be caused by a third variable

Methods

Each method suits different questions.
  • Surveys (questionnaires, interviews) collect data from many people; random samples allow generalization
  • Experiments test cause and effect by manipulating an independent variable, often in controlled settings
  • Participant observation and ethnography study groups from the inside over time
  • Secondary analysis uses existing data such as census records
  • Quantitative methods use numbers; qualitative methods explore meanings in depth

Ethics and Bias

Research involving people must protect them.
  • Informed consent: participants must know what they're agreeing to
  • Confidentiality and anonymity protect participants' identities
  • Institutional review boards (IRBs) review studies for ethical risks
  • The Hawthorne effect: people change their behavior when they know they're being studied
  • Value neutrality (Weber): researchers should keep personal values from distorting findings
A reliable measure can still be invalid (a scale that's consistently 5 lb off).
Correlation alone never proves causation.
Participant observation results usually can't be generalized to a whole population.

Unit 3: Culture & Socialization3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

Elements of Culture

Culture is the shared beliefs, practices and material objects of a group.
  • Material culture: physical objects (phones, clothing); nonmaterial culture: ideas, values, norms, language
  • Values are broad ideas about what is good; norms are rules for behavior
  • Folkways are casual norms; mores are strongly held moral norms; laws are formal norms enforced by the state
  • Symbols and language let people share meaning; the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis says language shapes thought
  • Sanctions reward or punish behavior to enforce norms

Cultural Variation

Cultures differ widely, and how we judge that difference matters.
  • Ethnocentrism: judging other cultures by the standards of your own
  • Cultural relativism: understanding a culture on its own terms
  • Subcultures share distinctive elements within a larger culture; countercultures reject major norms
  • Culture shock: disorientation when encountering an unfamiliar culture
  • Cultural diffusion spreads ideas and objects between cultures; cultural lag is when nonmaterial culture trails technology (Ogburn)

Socialization

Socialization is the lifelong process of learning the culture and developing a self.
  • Agents of socialization: family, school, peers, mass media, workplace, religion
  • Cooley's looking-glass self: we see ourselves as we think others see us
  • Mead: the self develops through play and games, taking the role of the 'generalized other'
  • Feral and isolated children (e.g. 'Genie') show that social contact is necessary for normal development
  • Resocialization replaces old norms, often in total institutions like prisons or boot camp (Goffman)
Values and norms aren't the same: values are ideals, norms are behavior rules.
Cultural relativism doesn't mean approving of every practice. It's a method of understanding.
Subculture vs. counterculture: only a counterculture opposes mainstream norms.

Unit 4: Social Structure & Groups3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

Status and Roles

Social structure is the pattern of relationships that organizes a society.
  • Status: a position in society; ascribed statuses are assigned (age, race), achieved statuses are earned (lawyer)
  • Master status: the status that dominates others in how people are seen
  • Role: the expected behavior of someone with a status
  • Role conflict: clashes between different statuses (parent vs. employee); role strain: tension within one status
  • Goffman's dramaturgy: people manage impressions like actors on a stage (front stage vs. backstage)

Groups

Groups shape identity and behavior.
  • Primary groups are small and intimate (family, close friends); secondary groups are larger and goal-oriented (coworkers)
  • In-groups command loyalty; out-groups are viewed with competition or hostility
  • Reference groups set standards we use to evaluate ourselves
  • Dyads (2 people) are intimate but fragile; triads (3) are more stable but allow coalitions (Simmel)
  • Groupthink: pressure for agreement leads to poor decisions

Conformity and Organizations

Group pressure and bureaucracy shape how people act.
  • Asch's line experiments: many people conformed to an obviously wrong group answer
  • Milgram's obedience studies: most participants obeyed an authority figure's orders to give apparent shocks
  • Weber's ideal bureaucracy: division of labor, hierarchy, written rules, impersonality, merit hiring
  • The iron law of oligarchy (Michels): organizations tend to be run by a small elite
  • McDonaldization (Ritzer): efficiency, calculability, predictability and control spreading through society
Role conflict involves two statuses; role strain involves one status.
Asch studied conformity to peers; Milgram studied obedience to authority.
A master status isn't always achieved. It can be ascribed, like race or disability.

Unit 5: Deviance & Stratification3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

Explaining Deviance

Deviance is behavior that violates norms. Sociologists ask why it happens and who gets labeled.
  • Deviance is relative: what's deviant depends on time, place and group
  • Durkheim: deviance clarifies norms and can promote social change
  • Merton's strain theory: deviance results when people lack legitimate means to reach cultural goals (innovation, ritualism, retreatism, rebellion)
  • Labeling theory (Becker): deviance is a label applied by others; a label can become a self-fulfilling identity
  • Differential association (Sutherland): deviance is learned from the people we interact with

Crime and Social Control

Societies use informal and formal controls to enforce norms.
  • Informal social control: disapproval, gossip, praise; formal: police, courts, laws
  • White-collar crime is committed by high-status people in the course of their jobs (Sutherland)
  • Conflict theorists note that laws and enforcement often reflect the interests of the powerful
  • Recidivism is the rate at which released offenders reoffend
  • Broken windows theory: visible disorder encourages more serious crime (a contested idea)

Social Stratification

Stratification ranks people into layers with unequal access to resources.
  • Weber's three dimensions: class (wealth), status (prestige) and power
  • Caste systems are closed (status is ascribed); class systems are open (mobility is possible)
  • Social mobility can be upward, downward, intergenerational or intragenerational
  • Davis–Moore thesis (functionalist): inequality motivates people to fill important positions
  • Conflict theorists argue stratification benefits the powerful and reproduces itself across generations
Deviance isn't the same as crime. Many deviant acts are legal.
Labeling theory focuses on reactions to behavior, not the behavior itself.
The Davis–Moore thesis is functionalist; conflict theorists criticize it.

Unit 6: Race, Gender & Inequality3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

Race and Ethnicity

Race and ethnicity are social categories with real consequences.
  • Race is socially constructed: categories based on perceived physical traits that vary across societies and time
  • Ethnicity refers to shared culture, ancestry, language or religion
  • Prejudice is an attitude; discrimination is an action
  • Institutional (systemic) discrimination is built into policies and practices, like historical redlining in housing
  • Patterns of intergroup relations include assimilation, pluralism, segregation and genocide

Sex and Gender

Gender is the set of social expectations attached to being male or female.
  • Sex refers to biological characteristics; gender refers to socially learned roles and identities
  • Gender socialization begins early through toys, clothing, media and expectations
  • The gender pay gap persists partly because of occupational segregation and caregiving expectations
  • The 'glass ceiling' describes invisible barriers to advancement
  • Feminist theory examines how gender inequality is created and maintained

Intersectionality and Minority Groups

Inequalities overlap and combine.
  • Intersectionality (Kimberlé Crenshaw) examines how race, class, gender and other identities combine to shape experience
  • A minority group is defined by less power, not by smaller numbers
  • Stereotypes are oversimplified generalizations about a group
  • Scapegoating blames a less powerful group for problems
  • The Civil Rights Act of 1964 outlawed many forms of discrimination in the U.S.
A minority group isn't necessarily a numerical minority. It's about power.
Discrimination can happen without individual prejudice, through institutions.
Sex (biological) and gender (social) aren't the same concept.

Unit 7: Family & Religion3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

Family Structures

Family forms vary widely across cultures and have changed over time.
  • Nuclear family: parents and children; extended family: several generations or relatives
  • Monogamy is marriage to one person; polygamy to several (polygyny: one husband, several wives)
  • Patrilineal, matrilineal and bilateral descent trace kinship differently
  • Trends in the U.S.: later marriage, more cohabitation, more single-parent and blended families
  • Endogamy: marrying within a group; exogamy: marrying outside it

Perspectives on Family

Each theoretical lens highlights different family functions and problems.
  • Functionalists: families socialize children, regulate sexuality, provide economic and emotional support
  • Conflict theorists: families can reproduce inequality in wealth and gendered power
  • Symbolic interactionists: family members create meaning through daily interaction
  • The 'second shift' (Hochschild): working women often do most housework after paid work
  • Family violence and divorce are studied as social patterns, not just private troubles

Religion

Religion is a system of beliefs and practices about the sacred.
  • Durkheim distinguished the sacred (set apart, awe-inspiring) from the profane (ordinary)
  • Functions of religion: meaning, social cohesion, social control, support in crisis
  • Marx called religion 'the opium of the people,' arguing it distracts from inequality
  • Weber linked the Protestant ethic to the rise of capitalism
  • Secularization is the declining influence of religion in public life; fundamentalism resists it
Polygamy is the general term; polygyny is specifically one husband with several wives.
Secularization doesn't mean religion disappears. It means its public influence declines.
Durkheim's 'profane' means ordinary, not offensive.

Unit 8: Social Change & Movements3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

Sources of Social Change

Societies change through technology, population, environment, ideas and conflict.
  • Technology (the printing press, the internet) reshapes work, communication and relationships
  • Demographic change: population growth, aging, migration and urbanization
  • Cultural diffusion spreads ideas across societies
  • Modernization: the shift from traditional, small-scale societies to industrial, urban ones
  • Tönnies contrasted Gemeinschaft (close community) with Gesellschaft (impersonal society)

Collective Behavior

Not all group behavior is organized or planned.
  • Crowds, mobs, riots, panics, fads and fashions are forms of collective behavior
  • Contagion theory: crowds make people act irrationally (Le Bon)
  • Convergence theory: like-minded people come together, so crowds reflect members' existing views
  • Emergent-norm theory: crowds develop new norms as a situation unfolds
  • Rumors and moral panics spread quickly, especially through media

Social Movements

Social movements are organized efforts to promote or resist change.
  • Types: reform (change some aspects), revolutionary (overturn the system), resistance (block change), alternative (change individuals)
  • Resource mobilization theory: success depends on money, members, leaders and organization
  • Relative deprivation: people mobilize when they feel worse off than others or than expected
  • Life cycle: emergence, coalescence, bureaucratization, decline
  • Examples: the Civil Rights Movement, the women's suffrage movement, environmental movements
Relative deprivation is about perceived gaps, not absolute poverty.
Reform movements seek partial change; revolutionary movements seek total change.
Convergence theory says crowds reflect members' existing views. It isn't about contagion.
