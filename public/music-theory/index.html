Unit 1: Notes, Staff & Clefs5 concepts · ~23 min▾
The Staff and Ledger Lines
Music is written on a staff: five horizontal lines and four spaces, counted from bottom to top.
  • The staff has 5 lines and 4 spaces, each representing a specific pitch once a clef is applied.
  • Ledger lines are short extra lines added above or below the staff to notate pitches outside its range.
  • Middle C sits on the first ledger line below the treble staff and the first ledger line above the bass staff.
  • A grand staff (piano staff) joins a treble staff and a bass staff with a brace and a connecting barline.
  • Notes are placed either on a line or in a space; consecutive note names alternate line-space-line-space.
The Treble Clef (G Clef)
The treble clef curls around the second line from the bottom, fixing that line as G4.
  • Treble clef lines (bottom to top): E-G-B-D-F, remembered as 'Every Good Boy Does Fine'.
  • Treble clef spaces (bottom to top): F-A-C-E, spelling the word FACE.
  • The treble clef is used for higher-pitched instruments and voices: violin, flute, right hand of piano, soprano.
  • It is also called the G clef because its spiral center circles the G line.
  • Notes above the staff use ledger lines going up (e.g., the space just above the top line is G5).
The Bass Clef (F Clef)
The bass clef's two dots surround the fourth line from the bottom, fixing that line as F3.
  • Bass clef lines (bottom to top): G-B-D-F-A, remembered as 'Good Boys Do Fine Always'.
  • Bass clef spaces (bottom to top): A-C-E-G, remembered as 'All Cows Eat Grass'.
  • The bass clef is used for lower-pitched instruments and voices: cello, bassoon, left hand of piano, bass.
  • It is also called the F clef because its two dots straddle the F line.
  • On the grand staff, the bass clef sits below the treble clef and connects at middle C via ledger lines.
Note Names and the Musical Alphabet
Pitches are named using only seven letters, A through G, which then repeat in higher or lower octaves.
  • The musical alphabet is A, B, C, D, E, F, G, then it repeats: ...G, A, B, C...
  • An octave is the distance from one letter name to the next instance of that same letter name.
  • Scientific pitch notation adds an octave number, so middle C is C4, the C above it is C5, and so on.
  • Each octave register sounds like the same note class but at double or half the frequency.
  • Piano keys alternate white keys (natural notes) with black keys (sharps/flats) in a repeating 12-key pattern.
Accidentals and Enharmonic Spelling
Accidentals raise or lower a note by a half step and apply for the rest of that measure unless canceled.
  • A sharp (#) raises a pitch by one half step; a flat (b) lowers a pitch by one half step.
  • A natural sign cancels a previous sharp or flat, returning the note to its unaltered pitch.
  • Double sharps (x) raise by a whole step and double flats (bb) lower by a whole step.
  • Enharmonic equivalents are two different note names for the same sounding pitch, e.g., C# and Db.
  • An accidental placed before a note affects every occurrence of that same pitch in the same measure.
Sharps and flats are half steps, not whole steps — don't confuse a half step with a whole step (two half steps).
A key signature accidental applies to every octave of that letter name, not just the one it's drawn on.
An accidental in a measure lasts only until the next barline, not for the rest of the piece.
C# and Db sound identical on a piano (enharmonic) but are spelled differently depending on musical context.
Unit 2: Rhythm & Meter5 concepts · ~23 min▾
Note and Rest Values
Rhythm is built from a system of proportional note values, each worth half the duration of the one before it.
  • A whole note gets 4 beats in 4/4 time and splits into two half notes (2 beats each).
  • A half note splits into two quarter notes (1 beat each); a quarter note splits into two eighth notes.
  • An eighth note splits into two sixteenth notes; eighth and sixteenth notes are beamed together in groups.
  • Every note value has a matching rest symbol of identical duration but silent: whole, half, quarter, eighth, sixteenth rests.
  • A dot placed after a note or rest adds half of that note's original value (a dotted half note = 3 beats).
Time Signatures: Simple Meter
A time signature's top number tells how many beats per measure; the bottom number tells what note value equals one beat.
  • In simple meter, the beat divides naturally into two equal parts.
  • 2/4 = duple simple meter (2 quarter-note beats per measure); 3/4 = triple simple (3 quarter-note beats); 4/4 = quadruple simple (4 quarter-note beats).
  • 4/4 is also called common time and is sometimes notated with a large C symbol.
  • The bottom number 4 means the quarter note is the beat unit; a bottom number of 2 means the half note is the beat unit.
  • Cut time (2/2, symbol C with a slash) means two half-note beats per measure, felt faster than 4/4.
Time Signatures: Compound Meter
In compound meter the beat is a dotted note that divides naturally into three equal parts instead of two.
  • 6/8, 9/8, and 12/8 are the most common compound meters.
  • 6/8 = duple compound (2 dotted-quarter beats per measure, each beat splits into 3 eighth notes).
  • 9/8 = triple compound (3 dotted-quarter beats per measure); 12/8 = quadruple compound (4 dotted-quarter beats).
  • To find the number of beats in compound meter, divide the top number by 3 (6/8 has 6÷3 = 2 beats).
  • Compound meter has a rolling, triplet-like feel compared to the even division of simple meter.
Tempo and Expression Markings
Tempo markings tell performers how fast or slow to play, either with Italian terms or an exact metronome marking.
  • Common tempo terms from slowest to fastest: Largo, Adagio, Andante, Moderato, Allegro, Presto.
  • A metronome marking such as quarter note = 120 (♩=120) states an exact number of beats per minute (BPM).
  • Ritardando (rit.) means gradually slowing down; accelerando (accel.) means gradually speeding up.
  • A fermata symbol tells the performer to hold a note or rest longer than its written value.
  • Tempo markings are usually placed above the staff at the start of a piece or at a change in speed.
Ties, Syncopation, and the Beat
Beyond basic note values, rhythm gets its energy from ties, syncopation, and how rhythms interact with the underlying pulse.
  • A tie connects two notes of the same pitch across a barline or within a measure, combining their durations into one sustained sound.
  • A slur looks similar to a tie but connects notes of different pitches, indicating smooth, connected articulation (legato).
  • Syncopation occurs when accents fall on normally weak beats or off-beats, working against the expected pulse.
  • The downbeat is the strong first beat of a measure; the upbeat (or pickup/anacrusis) is a note or notes before beat one.
  • A measure (or bar) is the space between two barlines containing the number of beats set by the time signature.
6/8 is NOT the same feel as 3/4, even though both can total the same number of eighth notes — 6/8 groups in 2, 3/4 groups in 3.
A tie combines durations of the SAME pitch; a slur connects DIFFERENT pitches for smooth phrasing — don't mix them up.
In compound meter the beat is a dotted note, not a plain quarter note, so counting divides by 3, not by the bottom number alone.
A fermata affects note length, not tempo of the whole piece — it's a pause, not a permanent tempo change.
Unit 3: Scales & Keys5 concepts · ~23 min▾
Building the Major Scale
A major scale is built from a fixed pattern of whole steps (W) and half steps (H) starting on the tonic.
  • The major scale formula is W-W-H-W-W-W-H (e.g., C major: C-D-E-F-G-A-B-C).
  • Half steps naturally occur between scale degrees 3-4 and 7-8 in a major scale.
  • Every major scale contains 7 different letter names before repeating the tonic an octave higher.
  • The major scale sound is often described as bright, happy, or resolved.
  • Applying the W-W-H-W-W-W-H formula to any starting pitch produces that pitch's major scale.
Building Minor Scales
Minor scales come in three common forms — natural, harmonic, and melodic — each altering the natural minor pattern differently.
  • Natural minor formula: W-H-W-W-H-W-W (e.g., A natural minor: A-B-C-D-E-F-G-A).
  • Harmonic minor raises the 7th scale degree by a half step, creating a strong pull to the tonic (leading tone) and a distinctive augmented 2nd between degrees 6 and 7.
  • Melodic minor raises both the 6th and 7th degrees when ascending, but reverts to natural minor when descending.
  • The minor scale sound is often described as dark, sad, or unresolved compared to major.
  • All three minor forms share the same first five scale degrees; they differ only in degrees 6 and 7.
Key Signatures and the Circle of Fifths
A key signature is a set of sharps or flats placed at the start of every staff line, showing which notes are consistently altered.
  • Order of sharps: F#, C#, G#, D#, A#, E#, B# (mnemonic: Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle).
  • Order of flats: Bb, Eb, Ab, Db, Gb, Cb, Fb (mnemonic: Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles' Father — the reverse of sharps).
  • The circle of fifths arranges all 12 major keys so each step clockwise adds one sharp (or removes one flat), moving up a perfect 5th each time.
  • C major and A minor have no sharps or flats, sitting at the top of the circle of fifths.
  • To find a sharp key's name, take the last sharp in the signature and go up one half step; to find a flat key's name (with 2+ flats), the second-to-last flat is the key name.
Relative and Parallel Keys
Major and minor keys relate to each other in two distinct ways: sharing a key signature, or sharing a tonic.
  • Relative minor starts on the 6th scale degree of its relative major and shares the exact same key signature (C major / A minor).
  • Parallel keys share the same tonic note but have different key signatures (C major / C minor).
  • To find a relative minor from a major key, count down 3 half steps (or up to scale degree 6).
  • Relative keys sound different in mood despite sharing identical notes/accidentals, because the tonal center shifts.
  • Parallel major and minor keys sound related in color because they share a tonic, but differ by 3 accidentals in signature.
Scale Degree Names
Each note of a scale has a functional name describing its role relative to the tonic, used constantly in harmonic analysis.
  • 1 = Tonic (home base), 2 = Supertonic, 3 = Mediant, 4 = Subdominant.
  • 5 = Dominant (strongest pull back to tonic), 6 = Submediant, 7 = Leading Tone (in major/harmonic minor) or Subtonic (in natural minor).
  • The leading tone is a half step below the tonic and strongly wants to resolve upward to it.
  • The subtonic (natural minor's 7th) is a whole step below the tonic and has a weaker pull to resolve.
  • The dominant (5th degree) and tonic (1st degree) relationship is the backbone of most Western harmonic progressions.
Relative minor is NOT the same as parallel minor — relative shares a key signature, parallel shares a tonic note.
The order of flats is the reverse of the order of sharps, not the same order.
Melodic minor changes its own descending form back to natural minor — it is not raised both ways.
The 'last sharp + half step up' trick finds a major key; for flats, use the second-to-last flat instead.
Unit 4: Intervals5 concepts · ~23 min▾
Measuring Intervals: Number and Quality
An interval is the distance between two pitches, described by a number (how many letter names it spans) and a quality (its exact size).
  • The interval number counts letter names inclusively: C to E is a 3rd because it spans C-D-E (3 letters).
  • Interval quality labels include: perfect, major, minor, augmented, and diminished.
  • Unisons, 4ths, 5ths, and octaves can be perfect, augmented, or diminished — but never major or minor.
  • 2nds, 3rds, 6ths, and 7ths can be major, minor, augmented, or diminished — but never perfect.
  • To fully name an interval, state both quality and number together, e.g., 'major 3rd' or 'perfect 5th'.
Perfect Intervals
Perfect intervals (unison, 4th, 5th, octave) are considered the most stable and consonant because of their simple frequency ratios.
  • A perfect unison is the same pitch played or sung twice (0 half steps).
  • A perfect 4th spans 5 half steps (e.g., C to F); a perfect 5th spans 7 half steps (e.g., C to G).
  • A perfect octave spans 12 half steps and returns to the same letter name (e.g., C4 to C5).
  • Raising a perfect interval by a half step makes it augmented; lowering it by a half step makes it diminished.
  • Perfect intervals sound highly stable and are foundational to power chords and open harmony.
Major and Minor Intervals
2nds, 3rds, 6ths, and 7ths come in major or minor quality, differing from each other by exactly one half step.
  • A major 2nd = 2 half steps (whole step); a minor 2nd = 1 half step.
  • A major 3rd = 4 half steps; a minor 3rd = 3 half steps — this single half-step difference is what makes chords sound major or minor.
  • A major 6th = 9 half steps; a minor 6th = 8 half steps.
  • A major 7th = 11 half steps; a minor 7th = 10 half steps.
  • Lowering a major interval by a half step makes it minor; lowering a minor interval by a half step makes it diminished.
Interval Inversion
Inverting an interval means moving the lower note up an octave (or the upper note down an octave) so the two notes swap position.
  • When an interval is inverted, its number and the inverted number always add up to 9 (a 3rd inverts to a 6th, a 5th inverts to a 4th).
  • Perfect intervals invert to perfect intervals (perfect 4th inverts to perfect 5th, and vice versa).
  • Major intervals invert to minor intervals, and minor intervals invert to major intervals.
  • Augmented intervals invert to diminished intervals, and diminished intervals invert to augmented intervals.
  • Inversion is a quick way to identify a large interval by finding its smaller, easier-to-hear complement.
Consonance, Dissonance, and Compound Intervals
Intervals are also classified by how stable or tense they sound, and by whether they exceed a single octave.
  • Consonant intervals (perfect unison/4th/5th/octave, major/minor 3rds and 6ths) sound stable and restful.
  • Dissonant intervals (2nds, 7ths, and any augmented/diminished interval, plus the tritone) sound tense and want to resolve.
  • The tritone (augmented 4th / diminished 5th, 6 half steps) splits the octave exactly in half and is the most unstable interval.
  • A compound interval is larger than an octave, such as a 9th (octave + 2nd) or an 11th (octave + 4th).
  • Compound intervals are named by adding 7 to their simple interval number (a 2nd becomes a 9th, a 3rd becomes a 10th).
You can never call a 4th or 5th 'major' or 'minor' — only perfect, augmented, or diminished.
Counting half steps alone can mislabel an interval; always count letter names first to get the correct number.
Inversion numbers sum to 9, not 8 or 10 — a common arithmetic slip.
A diminished 5th and an augmented 4th sound identical (both the tritone) but are spelled differently depending on context.
Unit 5: Chords & Harmony5 concepts · ~23 min▾
Building Triads
A triad is a three-note chord built by stacking two thirds on top of a root note.
  • Major triad = major 3rd + minor 3rd on top (root-3rd-5th), e.g., C-E-G.
  • Minor triad = minor 3rd + major 3rd on top, e.g., C-Eb-G.
  • Diminished triad = minor 3rd + minor 3rd (both thirds minor), e.g., C-Eb-Gb — sounds tense and unstable.
  • Augmented triad = major 3rd + major 3rd (both thirds major), e.g., C-E-G# — sounds tense and unresolved in a different way.
  • The root, 3rd, and 5th of a triad are named for their interval distance above the root.
Seventh Chords
A seventh chord adds a fourth note, a 7th above the root, on top of a triad for extra color and tension.
  • Major 7th chord = major triad + major 7th above root (e.g., Cmaj7: C-E-G-B) — smooth, lush sound.
  • Dominant 7th chord = major triad + minor 7th above root (e.g., C7: C-E-G-Bb) — tense, wants to resolve, built on scale degree 5.
  • Minor 7th chord = minor triad + minor 7th above root (e.g., Cm7: C-Eb-G-Bb) — mellow, jazzy sound.
  • Half-diminished 7th chord = diminished triad + minor 7th above root (e.g., Cm7b5: C-Eb-Gb-Bb).
  • Fully diminished 7th chord = diminished triad + diminished 7th above root (e.g., Cdim7: C-Eb-Gb-Bbb), dividing the octave into equal minor 3rds.
Chord Inversions
Inversions describe which chord tone is in the lowest (bass) voice, changing the chord's sound without changing its notes.
  • Root position: the root is in the bass (lowest note), e.g., C-E-G.
  • First inversion: the 3rd is in the bass, e.g., E-G-C — labeled with figured bass symbol '6'.
  • Second inversion: the 5th is in the bass, e.g., G-C-E — labeled with figured bass symbol '6/4'.
  • Inversions let a bass line move more smoothly (stepwise) between chords instead of jumping between roots.
  • For seventh chords, third inversion places the 7th in the bass, labeled '4/2' in figured bass.
Roman Numeral Analysis
Roman numerals label chords by their scale degree root and quality within a key, showing harmonic function at a glance.
  • Uppercase Roman numerals (I, IV, V) represent major chords; lowercase (ii, iii, vi) represent minor chords.
  • A small circle (vii°) marks a diminished chord; a plus sign (III+) marks an augmented chord.
  • In a major key, the diatonic triads are: I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, vii°.
  • In natural minor, the diatonic triads are: i, ii°, III, iv, v, VI, VII.
  • Adding a superscript 7 (V7, ii7) indicates a seventh chord built on that scale degree.
Harmonic Function and Cadences
Chords group into three functional families — tonic, subdominant, and dominant — that create tension and release, ending phrases with cadences.
  • Tonic function (I, vi) feels stable and 'home'; dominant function (V, vii°) feels tense and wants to resolve to tonic.
  • Subdominant function (IV, ii) feels like it's moving away from home, often leading toward the dominant.
  • An authentic cadence (V-I or V7-I) is the strongest, most conclusive ending in tonal music.
  • A plagal cadence (IV-I) is often called the 'Amen cadence' from its use at the end of hymns.
  • A half cadence ends on V (sounds unfinished); a deceptive cadence resolves V to vi instead of I (a surprise ending).
A diminished triad stacks two minor 3rds, not a minor 3rd plus a major 3rd — that combination is a minor triad.
Lowercase Roman numerals mean minor chords, not 'lower in pitch' — case indicates quality, not register.
A plagal cadence (IV-I) is weaker/different from an authentic cadence (V-I) — don't treat all two-chord endings as equal.
Inversion changes the bass note only, not the chord's letter names or its Roman numeral label.
Unit 6: Song Form & Structure5 concepts · ~23 min▾
Strophic and Verse-Chorus Form
Many songs are organized around repeating sections, the simplest being strophic form and the most common in popular music being verse-chorus form.
  • Strophic form repeats the same music for every verse with new lyrics each time (common in hymns and folk songs).
  • Verse-chorus form alternates contrasting verse sections (advancing the story, same music/new lyrics) with a chorus (same music AND lyrics each time, carrying the main hook).
  • A pre-chorus is a short transitional section that builds energy from the verse into the chorus.
  • A bridge is a contrasting section, usually appearing once, that provides a break from the verse/chorus pattern before a final chorus.
  • An intro and outro frame the song at the beginning and end and are not usually labeled as verse or chorus.
AABA (32-Bar) Form
AABA form, common in jazz standards and Tin Pan Alley songs, divides a chorus into four 8-bar sections.
  • AABA form totals 32 bars: A (8 bars) - A (8 bars) - B (8 bars) - A (8 bars).
  • The 'A' sections share the same melody and harmonic material, usually with the same or very similar lyrics.
  • The 'B' section, called the bridge or 'the release,' contrasts in melody and often modulates to a related key.
  • The final 'A' typically returns to the original key and material to bring the tune to a close.
  • Many Great American Songbook standards ('Over the Rainbow,' 'I Got Rhythm') use AABA form.
Binary and Ternary Form
Binary and ternary forms describe classical and instrumental structures built from two or three contrasting large sections.
  • Binary form (AB) has two distinct sections, often each repeated, with the second section contrasting or developing the first.
  • Ternary form (ABA) has three sections where the third repeats or closely resembles the first, framing a contrasting middle section.
  • A minuet and trio is a classic ternary form example: minuet (A) - trio (B) - minuet repeated (A).
  • In binary form, the two sections may be labeled 'rounded binary' if the A material briefly returns at the end of the B section.
  • Ternary form differs from AABA because it usually has three broader sections rather than four short 8-bar phrases.
Rondo and Larger Forms
Rondo form and sonata form organize longer instrumental works by alternating or developing thematic material across multiple sections.
  • Rondo form repeats a main theme (A) between contrasting episodes: ABACA or ABACABA.
  • The recurring 'A' section in a rondo acts as a stable anchor that listeners recognize each time it returns.
  • Sonata form has three main parts: exposition (introduces themes, usually in two keys), development (themes are fragmented and explored in different keys), and recapitulation (themes return, now in the home key).
  • Sonata form is common in the first movements of Classical-era symphonies, sonatas, and string quartets.
  • Both rondo and sonata form rely on the tension between a stable 'home' idea and contrasting departures from it.
Formal Terminology and Phrase Structure
Smaller-scale terms describe how phrases combine to build the larger sections found in song and instrumental forms.
  • A phrase is a musical sentence, often 4 or 8 bars long, ending with a cadence.
  • A period is a pair of phrases (antecedent and consequent) where the first ends less conclusively and the second answers it with a stronger cadence.
  • A motif (or motive) is a short, recognizable musical idea (rhythm and/or pitch pattern) that can be repeated or developed throughout a piece.
  • A hook is a short, catchy musical or lyrical phrase designed to be memorable, often found in the chorus.
  • Formal labels (A, B, verse, chorus) describe function and repetition pattern, not any fixed absolute length.
A 'bridge' in a verse-chorus pop song and the 'B' (release) in AABA form are related ideas but not identically defined — context matters.
Strophic form repeats music with NEW lyrics each verse; it is not the same as a chorus, which repeats both music and lyrics.
Ternary form (ABA) is a broad three-part structure, not the same thing as the four 8-bar sections of AABA form.
Sonata form's 'recapitulation' restates themes in the home key — it is not identical to the exposition, which often modulates.
Unit 7: Ear Training Basics5 concepts · ~23 min▾
Solfège and Scale Degree Function
Movable-do solfège assigns a syllable to each scale degree, training the ear to hear pitches by their function rather than by fixed letter names.
  • Movable-do syllables: Do-Re-Mi-Fa-Sol-La-Ti-Do, always mapped to scale degrees 1-2-3-4-5-6-7-1 regardless of key.
  • 'Do' always represents the tonic (scale degree 1), the most stable, 'home' pitch in a key.
  • 'Ti' (scale degree 7, the leading tone) feels unstable and pulls strongly upward to resolve to 'Do'.
  • 'Fa' (scale degree 4) also feels unstable and often resolves downward to 'Mi' (scale degree 3).
  • In minor keys using movable-do, the lowered scale degrees are altered: 'Me' (b3), 'Le' (b6), 'Te' (b7).
Recognizing Intervals by Ear
A common ear-training technique pairs each interval with the opening notes of a familiar song to make its sound memorable.
  • Ascending major 2nd: opening of 'Happy Birthday' or 'Do-Re-Mi' (Do to Re).
  • Ascending perfect 4th: opening of 'Here Comes the Bride' or the 'Bridal Chorus'.
  • Ascending perfect 5th: opening of 'Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star' or the theme from 'Star Wars'.
  • Ascending major 6th: opening of 'My Bonnie Lies Over the Ocean'.
  • Ascending minor 3rd is often associated with the opening of 'Greensleeves' or a stereotypical siren/cuckoo call.
Major vs. Minor Mode by Ear
One of the first aural skills is telling major and minor tonality apart, based mainly on the quality of the 3rd scale degree.
  • Major mode is typically described as bright, happy, or triumphant sounding.
  • Minor mode is typically described as dark, sad, mysterious, or tense sounding.
  • The key aural difference is the 3rd scale degree: a major 3rd above the tonic sounds major; a minor 3rd sounds minor.
  • Listening to the tonic triad (1-3-5) alone is often enough to identify whether a passage is in major or minor.
  • Film and game composers deliberately use major/minor mode shifts to signal emotional changes to the listener.
Rhythmic Dictation and Subdivision
Rhythmic ear training builds the skill of hearing, counting, and notating rhythms accurately by internalizing a steady subdivided pulse.
  • Subdivision means mentally dividing each beat into smaller equal parts (e.g., counting '1-and' for eighth notes) to place rhythms accurately.
  • Counting systems like '1-e-and-a' help performers place sixteenth notes precisely within a beat.
  • Clapping or tapping the beat while listening helps isolate whether a rhythm is on the beat or syncopated (off the beat).
  • Internalizing a steady pulse (audiation) before dictation prevents rhythms from speeding up or slowing down inaccurately.
  • Rhythmic dictation exercises typically build gradually from quarter/half notes to eighth, then sixteenth note patterns.
Call and Response, and Audiation
Ear training also develops the internal skill of 'hearing' music in the mind, and the practice of echoing and improvising musical ideas.
  • Audiation is the ability to hear and understand music internally, even without the sound physically present.
  • Call and response is a musical technique where one phrase (the call) is directly answered by another (the response), used in training and in genres like blues and gospel.
  • Singing back a melody you just heard trains pitch matching and short-term musical memory simultaneously.
  • Sight-singing (reading notation and singing it accurately) is the visual counterpart to dictation (hearing and writing notation).
  • Regular short daily ear-training practice builds recognition skills faster than occasional long sessions.
'Fixed-do' (where Do always means C) and 'movable-do' (where Do always means the tonic) are different systems — most ear training uses movable-do.
A perfect 5th and a perfect 4th are inversions of each other and can sound similarly 'open' — use reference songs to tell them apart.
Minor mode is not simply 'major but sadder-sounding notes at random' — the defining change is specifically the 3rd (and often 6th/7th) scale degree.
Subdivision must stay steady; speeding up during faster note values is a common rhythmic dictation error.
Unit 8: Analysis & Composition Starter5 concepts · ~23 min▾
Nonharmonic Tones
Nonharmonic (nonchord) tones are notes that don't belong to the underlying chord but add melodic interest and motion.
  • A passing tone connects two chord tones a 3rd apart by step, filling in the gap (approached and left by step in the same direction).
  • A neighbor tone steps away from a chord tone and back to the same chord tone (upper or lower neighbor).
  • A suspension holds over a note from the previous chord that becomes dissonant against the new chord, then resolves down by step.
  • An appoggiatura is approached by leap and resolved by step, landing on a strong beat before resolving — creating an accented, expressive dissonance.
  • An escape tone (échappée) is approached by step and left by leap in the opposite direction.
Voice Leading Basics
Voice leading is the practice of moving each individual musical line (voice) smoothly and correctly from chord to chord.
  • Prefer stepwise motion and common tones between chords over large leaps, especially in inner voices.
  • Parallel 5ths and parallel octaves between two voices are traditionally avoided in four-part harmony because they weaken the independence of the voices.
  • Contrary motion (voices moving in opposite directions) and oblique motion (one voice stays, one moves) create the smoothest, most independent-sounding textures.
  • Resolve the leading tone (scale degree 7) up to the tonic, especially in an outer voice, since it has a strong pull.
  • Double the root of a triad in four-part writing when possible, rather than doubling the leading tone.
Musical Texture
Texture describes how many independent melodic lines are present and how they relate to one another.
  • Monophonic texture is a single melodic line with no accompaniment or harmony (e.g., unaccompanied Gregorian chant).
  • Homophonic texture is a melody supported by chordal accompaniment (most pop, rock, and hymn music).
  • Polyphonic (contrapuntal) texture has two or more independent melodic lines happening simultaneously (e.g., a Bach fugue).
  • Heterophonic texture involves multiple performers playing the same basic melody with slight individual variations at once.
  • Composers often shift texture within a piece to create contrast between simpler and denser passages.
Motif, Sequence, and Development
Composers develop musical ideas by repeating and transforming a short motif in various ways throughout a piece.
  • A motif (motive) is a short, distinctive melodic and/or rhythmic idea that recurs and can be developed.
  • A sequence repeats a musical pattern at a new pitch level (higher or lower), preserving the same intervallic shape.
  • Melodic development techniques include inversion (flipping the melodic contour upside down), retrograde (playing it backward), and augmentation/diminution (stretching or shrinking note values).
  • Fragmentation takes a small piece of a motif and repeats or develops just that fragment.
  • Beethoven's Fifth Symphony opening (short-short-short-long) is a famous example of a motif developed extensively throughout a movement.
Starting a Chord Progression
Basic functional progressions give a beginning composer a reliable harmonic skeleton to write a melody over.
  • The I-IV-V-I progression is one of the most common in Western music, moving from tonic, through subdominant, through dominant, and back to tonic.
  • The ii-V-I progression is extremely common in jazz, moving through the subdominant-function ii chord to the dominant V, resolving to I.
  • The vi-IV-I-V ('pop-punk') progression appears across countless popular songs due to its strong, singable harmonic motion.
  • A I-V-vi-IV progression is another widely used pop pattern, notable for its deceptive-feeling move from V to vi.
  • Starting and ending a short composition on the tonic chord (I or i) reinforces a clear sense of key and closure.
A suspension resolves DOWN by step, not up — don't confuse its resolution direction with the leading tone's upward pull.
Homophonic texture (melody + chords) is not the same as monophonic (single unaccompanied line) — one has harmony, one doesn't.
A sequence changes pitch level but keeps the same shape/pattern; simply repeating the exact same pitches is not a sequence.
ii-V-I is a jazz-associated progression built on scale-degree function, not a fixed set of specific chord letter names across all keys.

Unit 1: Notes, Staff & Clefs

Staff
Five horizontal lines and four spaces on which music notation is written; pitch is determined by a note's position combined with the clef.
Treble Clef (G Clef)
A clef that circles the second line from the bottom, fixing it as G4; used for higher-pitched instruments and voices.
Bass Clef (F Clef)
A clef whose two dots surround the fourth line from the bottom, fixing it as F3; used for lower-pitched instruments and voices.
Ledger Lines
Short extra lines added above or below the staff to notate pitches that fall outside its five-line range.
Middle C
The C nearest the middle of the piano keyboard (C4); sits on the first ledger line below the treble staff and above the bass staff.
Grand Staff
A treble staff and a bass staff joined by a brace, used for piano and other wide-range instruments.
Sharp (#)
An accidental that raises a pitch by one half step.
Flat (b)
An accidental that lowers a pitch by one half step.
Natural Sign
An accidental that cancels a previous sharp or flat, returning a note to its unaltered pitch.
Enharmonic Equivalent
Two different note names that represent the same sounding pitch, such as C# and Db.

Unit 2: Rhythm & Meter

Whole Note
A note lasting 4 beats in 4/4 time; the longest common note value, shown as a hollow oval with no stem.
Time Signature
Two numbers at the start of a piece; the top shows beats per measure, the bottom shows which note value equals one beat.
Simple Meter
A meter in which each beat divides naturally into two equal parts, e.g., 2/4, 3/4, 4/4.
Compound Meter
A meter in which each beat is a dotted note that divides naturally into three equal parts, e.g., 6/8, 9/8, 12/8.
Measure (Bar)
The space between two barlines containing the number of beats specified by the time signature.
Tie
A curved line connecting two notes of the SAME pitch, combining their durations into one continuous sound.
Dotted Note
A note followed by a dot that adds half of the note's original value to its duration.
Tempo
The speed of the beat, indicated by Italian terms (e.g., Allegro) or an exact metronome marking (BPM).
Syncopation
Rhythmic emphasis placed on normally weak beats or off-beats, working against the expected pulse.
Fermata
A symbol instructing the performer to hold a note or rest longer than its written value.

Unit 3: Scales & Keys

Major Scale
A seven-note scale built with the pattern whole-whole-half-whole-whole-whole-half (W-W-H-W-W-W-H).
Natural Minor Scale
A seven-note scale built with the pattern whole-half-whole-whole-half-whole-whole (W-H-W-W-H-W-W).
Harmonic Minor Scale
Natural minor with the 7th scale degree raised a half step, creating a strong leading tone.
Melodic Minor Scale
Natural minor with the 6th and 7th degrees raised a half step ascending, but returning to natural minor descending.
Key Signature
A set of sharps or flats placed at the start of every staff line, showing which notes are consistently altered throughout a piece.
Circle of Fifths
A diagram arranging the 12 major keys so each clockwise step adds one sharp (or removes one flat), moving up a perfect 5th.
Relative Minor
The minor key that shares the exact same key signature as a given major key, starting on scale degree 6.
Parallel Key
A key that shares the same tonic note as another key but has a different key signature (e.g., C major and C minor).
Tonic
Scale degree 1; the 'home' pitch and most stable note of a key.
Leading Tone
Scale degree 7 in major (and harmonic minor); sits a half step below the tonic and pulls strongly to resolve upward to it.

Unit 4: Intervals

Interval
The distance between two pitches, described by a number (letter names spanned) and a quality (exact size).
Perfect Interval
An interval quality (unison, 4th, 5th, octave) considered highly stable and consonant; cannot be called major or minor.
Major Interval
A 2nd, 3rd, 6th, or 7th of the larger of its two possible sizes (one half step larger than the minor version).
Minor Interval
A 2nd, 3rd, 6th, or 7th one half step smaller than its major counterpart.
Augmented Interval
A perfect or major interval raised by one additional half step.
Diminished Interval
A perfect or minor interval lowered by one additional half step.
Interval Inversion
Moving the lower note of an interval up an octave (or vice versa); inverted numbers always sum to 9.
Tritone
The interval spanning 6 half steps (augmented 4th / diminished 5th); the most dissonant common interval, splitting the octave evenly.
Consonance
An interval quality that sounds stable and restful, such as perfect intervals and major/minor 3rds and 6ths.
Compound Interval
An interval larger than one octave, such as a 9th (octave + 2nd) or an 11th (octave + 4th).

Unit 5: Chords & Harmony

Triad
A three-note chord built by stacking two thirds on top of a root note.
Major Triad
A triad built from a major 3rd with a minor 3rd stacked on top (root-3rd-5th).
Minor Triad
A triad built from a minor 3rd with a major 3rd stacked on top.
Diminished Triad
A triad built from two stacked minor 3rds; sounds tense and unstable.
Augmented Triad
A triad built from two stacked major 3rds; sounds tense and unresolved.
Seventh Chord
A four-note chord formed by adding a 7th above the root of a triad, such as a dominant 7th or major 7th chord.
Chord Inversion
A rearrangement of a chord's notes so a tone other than the root is in the bass (lowest voice).
Roman Numeral Analysis
A system labeling chords by scale-degree root and quality within a key (uppercase = major, lowercase = minor, ° = diminished).
Authentic Cadence
A V-I (or V7-I) chord progression that provides the strongest, most conclusive resolution in tonal music.
Half Cadence
A phrase ending on the V chord, sounding unfinished and needing further resolution.

Unit 6: Song Form & Structure

Strophic Form
A song structure that repeats the same music for every verse, with new lyrics each time.
Verse-Chorus Form
A song structure alternating verses (new lyrics, same music) with a chorus (same music and lyrics each time).
AABA Form
A 32-bar form of four 8-bar sections: two matching A sections, a contrasting B (bridge), then a final A.
Binary Form
A two-part musical structure (AB) where the second section contrasts with or develops the first.
Ternary Form
A three-part musical structure (ABA) where the third section repeats or closely resembles the first.
Rondo Form
A form that alternates a recurring main theme (A) with contrasting episodes, e.g., ABACA.
Sonata Form
A large instrumental form with three main parts: exposition, development, and recapitulation.
Bridge
A contrasting section, usually appearing once, that provides a break before returning to familiar material.
Phrase
A musical sentence, often 4 or 8 bars long, that ends with a cadence.
Motif (Motive)
A short, recognizable musical idea that can be repeated or developed throughout a piece.

Unit 7: Ear Training Basics

Solfège
A system assigning syllables (Do, Re, Mi, Fa, Sol, La, Ti) to scale degrees to train pitch recognition by function.
Movable Do
A solfège system in which 'Do' always represents the tonic of the current key, regardless of its letter name.
Audiation
The ability to hear and understand music internally in the mind, even without the sound physically present.
Call and Response
A musical technique where one phrase (the call) is directly answered by another (the response).
Perfect 5th (reference)
A commonly memorized interval sound, recognized by the opening two notes of 'Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star.'
Major 2nd (reference)
A commonly memorized interval sound, recognized by the opening two notes of 'Happy Birthday.'
Aural Skills
The practical listening abilities used to identify pitches, intervals, chords, and rhythms by ear.
Sight-Singing
The skill of reading music notation and singing it accurately without first hearing it played.
Rhythmic Dictation
The exercise of listening to a rhythm and writing down its correct notation.
Subdivision
Mentally dividing each beat into smaller equal parts to place rhythms accurately in time.

Unit 8: Analysis & Composition Starter

Passing Tone
A nonharmonic tone that connects two chord tones a third apart by step, approached and left in the same direction.
Neighbor Tone
A nonharmonic tone that steps away from a chord tone and back to the same chord tone.
Suspension
A nonharmonic tone held over from the previous chord that becomes dissonant, then resolves down by step.
Appoggiatura
A nonharmonic tone approached by leap and resolved by step, often landing accented on a strong beat.
Parallel Fifths/Octaves
Two voices moving in the same direction by the same perfect interval; traditionally avoided in four-part harmony.
Contrary Motion
Two musical voices moving in opposite directions, producing smooth and independent-sounding voice leading.
Monophonic Texture
A musical texture consisting of a single melodic line with no accompaniment or harmony.
Homophonic Texture
A musical texture with one melody supported by chordal accompaniment.
Polyphonic Texture
A musical texture with two or more independent melodic lines happening simultaneously.
Sequence
The repetition of a musical pattern at a new, higher or lower pitch level, preserving the same intervallic shape.

Unit 1: Notes, Staff & Clefs

The Staff and Ledger Lines
Music is written on a staff: five horizontal lines and four spaces, counted from bottom to top.
The Treble Clef (G Clef)
The treble clef curls around the second line from the bottom, fixing that line as G4.
The Bass Clef (F Clef)
The bass clef's two dots surround the fourth line from the bottom, fixing that line as F3.
Note Names and the Musical Alphabet
Pitches are named using only seven letters, A through G, which then repeat in higher or lower octaves.
Accidentals and Enharmonic Spelling
Accidentals raise or lower a note by a half step and apply for the rest of that measure unless canceled.
Key fact
Treble clef lines spell EGBDF (Every Good Boy Does Fine); spaces spell FACE.
Key fact
Bass clef lines spell GBDFA (Good Boys Do Fine Always); spaces spell ACEG (All Cows Eat Grass).
Key fact
Middle C (C4) sits on the ledger line directly between the treble and bass staves on a grand staff.
Key fact
The musical alphabet uses only seven letters (A-G) that repeat across octaves.

Unit 2: Rhythm & Meter

Note and Rest Values
Rhythm is built from a system of proportional note values, each worth half the duration of the one before it.
Time Signatures: Simple Meter
A time signature's top number tells how many beats per measure; the bottom number tells what note value equals one beat.
Time Signatures: Compound Meter
In compound meter the beat is a dotted note that divides naturally into three equal parts instead of two.
Tempo and Expression Markings
Tempo markings tell performers how fast or slow to play, either with Italian terms or an exact metronome marking.
Ties, Syncopation, and the Beat
Beyond basic note values, rhythm gets its energy from ties, syncopation, and how rhythms interact with the underlying pulse.
Key fact
Each note value is worth exactly half of the next-larger value: whole > half > quarter > eighth > sixteenth.
Key fact
Simple meter beats divide into 2; compound meter beats divide into 3.
Key fact
A dot adds half of the original note's value to itself.
Key fact
The time signature's bottom number names the note value that equals one beat (in simple meter).

Unit 3: Scales & Keys

Building the Major Scale
A major scale is built from a fixed pattern of whole steps (W) and half steps (H) starting on the tonic.
Building Minor Scales
Minor scales come in three common forms — natural, harmonic, and melodic — each altering the natural minor pattern differently.
Key Signatures and the Circle of Fifths
A key signature is a set of sharps or flats placed at the start of every staff line, showing which notes are consistently altered.
Relative and Parallel Keys
Major and minor keys relate to each other in two distinct ways: sharing a key signature, or sharing a tonic.
Scale Degree Names
Each note of a scale has a functional name describing its role relative to the tonic, used constantly in harmonic analysis.
Key fact
Major scale pattern: W-W-H-W-W-W-H. Natural minor pattern: W-H-W-W-H-W-W.
Key fact
Relative keys share a key signature; parallel keys share a tonic.
Key fact
Order of sharps: F-C-G-D-A-E-B. Order of flats is the exact reverse: B-E-A-D-G-C-F.
Key fact
Harmonic minor raises scale degree 7; melodic minor raises degrees 6 and 7 ascending only.

Unit 4: Intervals

Measuring Intervals: Number and Quality
An interval is the distance between two pitches, described by a number (how many letter names it spans) and a quality (its exact size).
Perfect Intervals
Perfect intervals (unison, 4th, 5th, octave) are considered the most stable and consonant because of their simple frequency ratios.
Major and Minor Intervals
2nds, 3rds, 6ths, and 7ths come in major or minor quality, differing from each other by exactly one half step.
Interval Inversion
Inverting an interval means moving the lower note up an octave (or the upper note down an octave) so the two notes swap position.
Consonance, Dissonance, and Compound Intervals
Intervals are also classified by how stable or tense they sound, and by whether they exceed a single octave.
Key fact
Perfect intervals: unison, 4th, 5th, octave. All others (2nd, 3rd, 6th, 7th) are major/minor.
Key fact
A major 3rd = 4 half steps; a minor 3rd = 3 half steps — the building block of major/minor chords.
Key fact
Inverted interval numbers always sum to 9; perfect stays perfect, major/minor swap, augmented/diminished swap.
Key fact
The tritone (6 half steps) is the most dissonant common interval and splits the octave evenly.

Unit 5: Chords & Harmony

Building Triads
A triad is a three-note chord built by stacking two thirds on top of a root note.
Seventh Chords
A seventh chord adds a fourth note, a 7th above the root, on top of a triad for extra color and tension.
Chord Inversions
Inversions describe which chord tone is in the lowest (bass) voice, changing the chord's sound without changing its notes.
Roman Numeral Analysis
Roman numerals label chords by their scale degree root and quality within a key, showing harmonic function at a glance.
Harmonic Function and Cadences
Chords group into three functional families — tonic, subdominant, and dominant — that create tension and release, ending phrases with cadences.
Key fact
Major triad = major 3rd + minor 3rd; minor triad = minor 3rd + major 3rd (stacked from the root).
Key fact
Dominant 7th chords are built on scale degree 5 and strongly pull to the tonic.
Key fact
Root position has the root in the bass; 1st inversion has the 3rd in the bass; 2nd inversion has the 5th in the bass.
Key fact
Authentic cadence (V-I) is the strongest resolution; half cadence ends on V; deceptive cadence goes V-vi.

Unit 6: Song Form & Structure

Strophic and Verse-Chorus Form
Many songs are organized around repeating sections, the simplest being strophic form and the most common in popular music being verse-chorus form.
AABA (32-Bar) Form
AABA form, common in jazz standards and Tin Pan Alley songs, divides a chorus into four 8-bar sections.
Binary and Ternary Form
Binary and ternary forms describe classical and instrumental structures built from two or three contrasting large sections.
Rondo and Larger Forms
Rondo form and sonata form organize longer instrumental works by alternating or developing thematic material across multiple sections.
Formal Terminology and Phrase Structure
Smaller-scale terms describe how phrases combine to build the larger sections found in song and instrumental forms.
Key fact
Verse-chorus form: verses change lyrics over the same music; the chorus repeats both music and lyrics.
Key fact
AABA form is 32 bars total: two A sections, a contrasting B (bridge/release), then a final A.
Key fact
Sonata form = exposition, development, recapitulation.
Key fact
A rondo alternates a recurring theme (A) with contrasting episodes: ABACA.

Unit 7: Ear Training Basics

Solfège and Scale Degree Function
Movable-do solfège assigns a syllable to each scale degree, training the ear to hear pitches by their function rather than by fixed letter names.
Recognizing Intervals by Ear
A common ear-training technique pairs each interval with the opening notes of a familiar song to make its sound memorable.
Major vs. Minor Mode by Ear
One of the first aural skills is telling major and minor tonality apart, based mainly on the quality of the 3rd scale degree.
Rhythmic Dictation and Subdivision
Rhythmic ear training builds the skill of hearing, counting, and notating rhythms accurately by internalizing a steady subdivided pulse.
Call and Response, and Audiation
Ear training also develops the internal skill of 'hearing' music in the mind, and the practice of echoing and improvising musical ideas.
Key fact
Movable-do solfège: Do-Re-Mi-Fa-Sol-La-Ti-Do always maps to scale degrees 1-7-1 in any key.
Key fact
The leading tone ('Ti') pulls up to the tonic ('Do'); it is a half step below Do.
Key fact
Major vs. minor mode is distinguished mainly by the quality of the 3rd scale degree above the tonic.
Key fact
Audiation is hearing music internally in the mind, without it sounding out loud.

Unit 8: Analysis & Composition Starter

Nonharmonic Tones
Nonharmonic (nonchord) tones are notes that don't belong to the underlying chord but add melodic interest and motion.
Voice Leading Basics
Voice leading is the practice of moving each individual musical line (voice) smoothly and correctly from chord to chord.
Musical Texture
Texture describes how many independent melodic lines are present and how they relate to one another.
Motif, Sequence, and Development
Composers develop musical ideas by repeating and transforming a short motif in various ways throughout a piece.
Starting a Chord Progression
Basic functional progressions give a beginning composer a reliable harmonic skeleton to write a melody over.
Key fact
Passing tones and neighbor tones are approached and left by step; appoggiaturas are approached by leap, left by step.
Key fact
Avoid parallel 5ths and parallel octaves between voices in traditional four-part harmony.
Key fact
Texture types: monophonic (one line), homophonic (melody + accompaniment), polyphonic (independent simultaneous lines).
Key fact

Unit 1: Notes, Staff & Clefs

Watch out
Sharps and flats are half steps, not whole steps — don't confuse a half step with a whole step (two half steps).
Watch out
A key signature accidental applies to every octave of that letter name, not just the one it's drawn on.
Watch out
An accidental in a measure lasts only until the next barline, not for the rest of the piece.
Watch out
C# and Db sound identical on a piano (enharmonic) but are spelled differently depending on musical context.

Unit 2: Rhythm & Meter

Watch out
6/8 is NOT the same feel as 3/4, even though both can total the same number of eighth notes — 6/8 groups in 2, 3/4 groups in 3.
Watch out
A tie combines durations of the SAME pitch; a slur connects DIFFERENT pitches for smooth phrasing — don't mix them up.
Watch out
In compound meter the beat is a dotted note, not a plain quarter note, so counting divides by 3, not by the bottom number alone.
Watch out
A fermata affects note length, not tempo of the whole piece — it's a pause, not a permanent tempo change.

Unit 3: Scales & Keys

Watch out
Relative minor is NOT the same as parallel minor — relative shares a key signature, parallel shares a tonic note.
Watch out
The order of flats is the reverse of the order of sharps, not the same order.
Watch out
Melodic minor changes its own descending form back to natural minor — it is not raised both ways.
Watch out
The 'last sharp + half step up' trick finds a major key; for flats, use the second-to-last flat instead.

Unit 4: Intervals

Watch out
You can never call a 4th or 5th 'major' or 'minor' — only perfect, augmented, or diminished.
Watch out
Counting half steps alone can mislabel an interval; always count letter names first to get the correct number.
Watch out
Inversion numbers sum to 9, not 8 or 10 — a common arithmetic slip.
Watch out
A diminished 5th and an augmented 4th sound identical (both the tritone) but are spelled differently depending on context.

Unit 5: Chords & Harmony

Watch out
A diminished triad stacks two minor 3rds, not a minor 3rd plus a major 3rd — that combination is a minor triad.
Watch out
Lowercase Roman numerals mean minor chords, not 'lower in pitch' — case indicates quality, not register.
Watch out
A plagal cadence (IV-I) is weaker/different from an authentic cadence (V-I) — don't treat all two-chord endings as equal.
Watch out
Inversion changes the bass note only, not the chord's letter names or its Roman numeral label.

Unit 6: Song Form & Structure

Watch out
A 'bridge' in a verse-chorus pop song and the 'B' (release) in AABA form are related ideas but not identically defined — context matters.
Watch out
Strophic form repeats music with NEW lyrics each verse; it is not the same as a chorus, which repeats both music and lyrics.
Watch out
Ternary form (ABA) is a broad three-part structure, not the same thing as the four 8-bar sections of AABA form.
Watch out
Sonata form's 'recapitulation' restates themes in the home key — it is not identical to the exposition, which often modulates.
