Unit 1: Advanced Preterite vs Imperfect3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

When to Use the Preterite

The preterite presents past actions as completed, with a clear beginning or end.
  • Single completed actions: Ayer compré un libro (Yesterday I bought a book)
  • Actions with a specific time frame or number of times: Viví en México dos años
  • A sequence of events: Me levanté, me duché y salí
  • An action that interrupts another: Estaba leyendo cuando sonó el teléfono
  • Trigger words: ayer, anoche, el año pasado, de repente, una vez

When to Use the Imperfect

The imperfect describes ongoing, habitual or background situations in the past.
  • Habitual actions: De niño, jugaba al fútbol todos los días (I used to play)
  • Background description: Hacía sol y los pájaros cantaban
  • Time, age and ongoing states: Eran las tres. Tenía diez años
  • Actions in progress: Mientras yo cocinaba, él limpiaba
  • Trigger words: siempre, a menudo, todos los días, mientras, de niño

Verbs That Change Meaning

Some verbs mean something different in the preterite.
  • Conocer: conocí = I met (for the first time); conocía = I knew
  • Saber: supe = I found out; sabía = I knew
  • Querer: quise = I tried; no quise = I refused; quería = I wanted
  • Poder: pude = I managed to; no pude = I failed to; podía = I was able to
  • Tener: tuve = I got/received; tenía = I had
'Used to' is always imperfect, never preterite.
Duration alone doesn't force the imperfect: 'Viví allí cinco años' (a closed period) is preterite.
Don't translate 'was/were' automatically as imperfect of estar; ask whether the action was completed.

Unit 2: Future & Conditional3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

The Simple Future

The future tense adds endings to the whole infinitive.
  • Endings for all verbs: -é, -ás, -á, -emos, -éis, -án (hablaré, comerás, vivirá)
  • Irregular stems: tener → tendr-, salir → saldr-, venir → vendr-, poner → pondr-, poder → podr-, saber → sabr-, hacer → har-, decir → dir-, querer → querr-, haber → habr-
  • Ir a + infinitive is also common for near future: Voy a estudiar
  • Future of probability: ¿Dónde estará Ana? (I wonder where Ana is)
  • Hay → habrá (there will be)

The Conditional

The conditional expresses what would happen.
  • Endings on the infinitive: -ía, -ías, -ía, -íamos, -íais, -ían (hablaría, comerías)
  • It uses the same irregular stems as the future: tendría, haría, diría, podría
  • Polite requests: ¿Podría ayudarme? (Could you help me?)
  • Future in the past: Dijo que vendría (He said he would come)
  • Probability in the past: Serían las diez (It was probably ten)

Si Clauses

Conditional sentences pair specific tenses.
  • Real/likely: Si + present, future: Si estudio, aprobaré
  • Hypothetical: Si + imperfect subjunctive, conditional: Si tuviera dinero, viajaría
  • The si clause can come first or second: Viajaría si tuviera dinero
  • Never use the future or conditional directly after si meaning 'if'
  • Deber in the conditional gives advice: Deberías dormir más
Don't say 'Si tendría dinero'. It should be 'Si tuviera dinero'.
Hacer → haré (not haceré); decir → diré (not deciré).
The conditional endings look like -er/-ir imperfect endings but attach to the infinitive.

Unit 3: Present Perfect3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

Forming the Present Perfect

The present perfect uses haber plus a past participle.
  • Haber: he, has, ha, hemos, habéis, han
  • Regular participles: -ar → -ado (hablado); -er/-ir → -ido (comido, vivido)
  • Irregular participles: abrir → abierto, decir → dicho, escribir → escrito, hacer → hecho, morir → muerto, poner → puesto, romper → roto, ver → visto, volver → vuelto, cubrir → cubierto
  • Nothing goes between haber and the participle; pronouns go before haber: Lo he visto
  • Participles with haber never change for gender or number: Ellas han llegado

When to Use It

The present perfect links past actions to the present.
  • Actions in a time period that isn't finished: Este año he leído cinco libros
  • Life experiences: ¿Has viajado a Perú?
  • Recent actions with present relevance: Ya he terminado la tarea
  • Common words: ya (already), todavía no / aún no (not yet), alguna vez (ever), nunca
  • Usage varies by region: Spain uses it more for today's events; much of Latin America prefers the preterite

Past Participles as Adjectives

Participles can also describe nouns.
  • With estar, the participle describes a resulting state: La puerta está abierta
  • As adjectives, participles agree in gender and number: las ventanas cerradas
  • Compare: He cerrado las ventanas (action) vs. Las ventanas están cerradas (state)
  • The pluperfect uses the imperfect of haber: había comido (I had eaten)
  • Acabar de + infinitive means 'to have just': Acabo de comer
Don't put pronouns between haber and the participle: 'Lo he hecho', not 'He lo hecho'.
Hecho, not 'hacido'; visto, not 'veído'.
'Han llegadas' is wrong; after haber the participle is invariable.

Unit 4: Commands Review & Advanced3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

Tú Commands

Informal commands have different affirmative and negative forms.
  • Affirmative tú = él/ella present form: Habla, Come, Escribe
  • Irregular affirmative tú: di, haz, ve, pon, sal, sé, ten, ven
  • Negative tú = present subjunctive tú form: No hables, No comas, No salgas
  • Pronouns attach to affirmative commands (Dímelo) and go before negative ones (No me lo digas)
  • Add an accent when attaching pronouns to keep the stress: Cómpralo

Formal and Nosotros Commands

Usted, ustedes and nosotros commands come from the subjunctive.
  • Usted/ustedes: Hable(n), Coma(n), Escriba(n); negative just adds no
  • Irregular: vaya(n), sea(n), esté(n), dé/den, sepa(n)
  • Nosotros (let's): Hablemos, Comamos; ir → Vamos (affirmative), No vayamos (negative)
  • Reflexive nosotros drops the -s before nos: Sentémonos, Levantémonos
  • Spelling changes: -car → -que (busque), -gar → -gue (llegue), -zar → -ce (empiece)

Using Commands Politely

Commands can sound abrupt; Spanish has softer options.
  • Use usted with strangers, elders and in formal settings
  • Add por favor or use the conditional: ¿Podría pasarme la sal?
  • Vosotros commands (used in Spain): hablad, comed; the negative uses the subjunctive: no habléis
  • Infinitives are used for written instructions: No fumar
  • Double object pronouns: le/les become se before lo/la: Dáselo
Negative tú isn't just 'no' + affirmative: 'No habla' is wrong; it's 'No hables'.
Ve (go) and ve (see) look the same; context decides.
Le/les + lo/la becomes se lo/se la, never 'le lo'.

Unit 5: Subjunctive Deep Dive3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

Forming the Present Subjunctive

Start from the yo form of the present, drop -o, and add 'opposite' endings.
  • -ar verbs take -e endings: hable, hables, hable, hablemos, habléis, hablen
  • -er/-ir verbs take -a endings: coma, comas…; viva, vivas…
  • Yo-form irregulars carry over: tener → tenga, hacer → haga, conocer → conozca
  • Irregulars (DISHES): dar (dé), ir (vaya), ser (sea), haber (haya), estar (esté), saber (sepa)
  • Stem changes follow the present pattern; -ir stem-changers also change in nosotros/vosotros: durmamos, pidamos

WEIRDO Triggers

The subjunctive appears in a dependent clause after certain triggers, usually with a change of subject.
  • Wishes: Quiero que vengas
  • Emotions: Me alegra que estés aquí
  • Impersonal expressions: Es importante que estudies
  • Recommendations: Te recomiendo que duermas más
  • Doubt/denial and Ojalá: Dudo que llueva; No creo que sea verdad; Ojalá que ganemos

Other Uses and the Imperfect Subjunctive

The subjunctive also follows certain conjunctions and unknown antecedents.
  • Conjunctions that always take it: para que, antes de que, sin que, a menos que, con tal de que
  • Cuando, hasta que, en cuanto take the subjunctive for future actions: Cuando llegues, llámame
  • Unknown or nonexistent antecedents: Busco un libro que explique esto
  • Imperfect subjunctive: ellos preterite, drop -ron, add -ra endings: hablaran → hablara, tuviera, fuera
  • Sequence of tenses: a past trigger takes the imperfect subjunctive: Quería que vinieras
Same subject, no que: 'Quiero ir' (not 'Quiero que yo vaya').
'Creo que es verdad' (indicative) vs. 'No creo que sea verdad' (subjunctive).
Cuando with a past or habitual action takes the indicative: 'Cuando llegué, comí'.

Unit 6: Por vs Para & Prepositions3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

Uses of Para

Para points toward a goal, destination or deadline.
  • Purpose (in order to): Estudio para aprender
  • Destination: Salimos para Madrid
  • Recipient: Este regalo es para ti
  • Deadline: La tarea es para el lunes
  • Opinion and comparison: Para mí, es fácil; Para un niño, lee muy bien

Uses of Por

Por looks at cause, means, exchange or movement through.
  • Cause or reason (because of): Llegué tarde por el tráfico
  • Exchange or price: Pagué diez dólares por el libro; Gracias por todo
  • Duration: Estudié por dos horas
  • Means of communication or transportation: por teléfono, por avión
  • Movement through or along: Caminamos por el parque; also 'per': 60 millas por hora

Other Prepositions and Expressions

Some prepositions don't translate one-to-one.
  • Fixed expressions with por: por fin, por ejemplo, por eso, por favor, por supuesto
  • Verbs with a preposition: soñar con, pensar en, depender de, enamorarse de, casarse con
  • Prepositional pronouns: para mí, para ti, con él; con + mí/ti → conmigo, contigo
  • A personal: use 'a' before a specific person as a direct object: Veo a mi abuela
  • De + el → del; a + el → al
Gracias por, not 'gracias para'.
'Para' + infinitive = in order to; don't use 'por' for purpose.
Pensar en = to think about; pensar de = opinion of.

Unit 7: Culture: Spanish-Speaking World3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

Geography and Diversity

Spanish is an official language in 20 countries plus Puerto Rico, and is widely spoken beyond them.
  • Spanish is an official language of 20 countries plus Puerto Rico (a U.S. territory), and has about 500 million native speakers
  • Regions: Spain, Mexico, Central America, the Caribbean, South America and Equatorial Guinea in Africa
  • Vocabulary varies by region: 'bus' can be autobús, camión (Mexico), guagua (Caribbean, Canary Islands) or colectivo (Argentina)
  • Voseo: in Argentina, Uruguay and parts of Central America, vos replaces tú (vos tenés)
  • The U.S. has one of the largest Spanish-speaking populations in the world

History and Heritage

The Spanish-speaking world blends Indigenous, European and African heritage.
  • Great Indigenous civilizations included the Maya, Aztec (Mexica) and Inca
  • Spanish colonization began after 1492; most Latin American countries won independence in the early 1800s
  • Many Indigenous languages remain widely spoken, such as Quechua, Guaraní (co-official in Paraguay) and Nahuatl
  • Spain has co-official regional languages: Catalan, Galician and Basque
  • African heritage shapes Caribbean and coastal music, food and culture

Arts and Traditions

Traditions and artists from the Spanish-speaking world are known worldwide.
  • Día de los Muertos (Mexico, Nov. 1–2) honors deceased loved ones with ofrendas
  • Writers: Miguel de Cervantes (Don Quijote), Gabriel García Márquez and Pablo Neruda (Nobel Prizes), Gabriela Mistral (first Latin American Nobel in Literature, 1945)
  • Artists: Frida Kahlo, Diego Rivera, Pablo Picasso, Salvador Dalí
  • Music and dance: flamenco (Spain), tango (Argentina), salsa, cumbia, reggaetón
  • La quinceañera celebrates a girl's 15th birthday in many Latin American communities
Día de los Muertos isn't 'Mexican Halloween'. It honors the dead with family remembrance.
Brazil speaks Portuguese, not Spanish.
Vocabulary differences are normal, not mistakes: 'coche' vs. 'carro' vs. 'auto'.

Unit 8: Reading & Listening Strategies3 concepts · ~15 min

▾

Before Reading or Listening

Preparing your brain for a text makes comprehension much easier.
  • Predict content from titles, images and headings
  • Activate vocabulary you already know about the topic
  • Identify the text type (news article, ad, interview, story) and its likely purpose
  • Read the questions first on a test so you know what to listen or look for
  • Set a purpose: gist (main idea) or specific details

Decoding Unknown Words

You don't need to know every word to understand a text.
  • Cognates look similar in English and Spanish: información, importante, universidad
  • False cognates (falsos amigos): embarazada = pregnant, librería = bookstore, éxito = success, carpeta = folder
  • Use context clues from surrounding sentences
  • Recognize word families and parts: -mente = -ly (rápidamente); -dad = -ty (ciudad)
  • Skip words that aren't essential to the main idea

Listening Skills

Listening in real time requires different strategies than reading.
  • Listen for key words, numbers, names and time expressions
  • Pay attention to tone, emphasis and connectors (pero, sin embargo, por eso, además)
  • On a first listen get the gist; on a second, catch details
  • Native speakers link words together; practice with authentic audio (podcasts, news, songs)
  • Take quick notes with abbreviations rather than full sentences
'Estoy embarazada' means 'I'm pregnant', not 'embarrassed' (avergonzado/a).
Trying to translate every word slows comprehension.
Connectors like 'sin embargo' signal contrast. Don't skip them.
