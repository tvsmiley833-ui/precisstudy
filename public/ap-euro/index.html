Unit 1: Renaissance & Exploration

Humanism
An intellectual movement emphasizing study of classical Greek/Roman texts, secular subjects, and human potential, originating in 14th-century Italy.
Niccolo Machiavelli
Florentine political theorist whose work The Prince (1513) described pragmatic, sometimes ruthless methods for rulers to gain and keep power.
Desiderius Erasmus
Leading Christian humanist who satirized Church corruption in The Praise of Folly and produced a critical Greek New Testament.
Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)
Papal-brokered agreement dividing New World colonial claims between Spain and Portugal along a meridian line.
Columbian Exchange
The transatlantic transfer of crops, animals, people, and diseases between the Old World and the Americas following 1492.
Encomienda System
Spanish colonial labor system granting colonists the right to forced indigenous labor and tribute in exchange for supposed protection and conversion.
Johannes Gutenberg
German inventor whose movable-type printing press (c. 1450) revolutionized the speed and cost of spreading texts and ideas.
Leonardo da Vinci
Italian Renaissance artist and polymath who painted the Mona Lisa and The Last Supper, epitomizing the 'Renaissance man' ideal.
Price Revolution
Sustained 16th-century European inflation caused largely by massive silver imports from Spanish American mines like Potosi.
Christopher Columbus
Genoese navigator sailing for Spain who reached the Caribbean in 1492 seeking a westward route to Asia, initiating sustained European-American contact.

Unit 2: Reformation & Wars of Religion

Martin Luther
German monk whose 1517 Ninety-Five Theses, attacking indulgences, launched the Protestant Reformation and doctrine of justification by faith alone.
Sola Fide / Sola Scriptura
Luther's core doctrines: salvation by faith alone, and scripture alone (not Church tradition) as religious authority.
Peace of Augsburg (1555)
Agreement letting German princes choose Lutheranism or Catholicism for their territory (cuius regio, eius religio); excluded Calvinism.
John Calvin
French reformer who systematized Protestant theology, emphasizing predestination, and established a theocratic model government in Geneva.
Council of Trent (1545-1563)
Catholic Counter-Reformation council that reaffirmed traditional doctrine and reformed clerical abuses and education.
Jesuits (Society of Jesus)
Catholic religious order founded by Ignatius of Loyola in 1540, central to Counter-Reformation education and missionary work.
Edict of Nantes (1598)
Decree by Henry IV granting French Huguenots substantial religious toleration and civil rights, later revoked by Louis XIV in 1685.
Thirty Years' War (1618-1648)
Devastating conflict beginning as a religious war in the Holy Roman Empire that grew into a broader political struggle, ended by the Peace of Westphalia.
Peace of Westphalia (1648)
Treaty ending the Thirty Years' War, recognizing Calvinism and establishing state sovereignty as the basis of the European political order.
Anabaptists
Radical Reformation group rejecting infant baptism in favor of adult baptism, persecuted by both Catholics and mainstream Protestants.

Unit 3: Absolutism & Constitutionalism

Louis XIV
French 'Sun King' whose reign (1643-1715) epitomized absolutist divine right monarchy centered at the Palace of Versailles.
Glorious Revolution (1688)
Bloodless overthrow of James II in England, replaced by William and Mary under conditions codified in the English Bill of Rights.
John Locke
English philosopher whose Two Treatises of Government argued legitimate government rests on consent and protection of natural rights.
Thomas Hobbes
English philosopher whose Leviathan (1651) argued absolute sovereign power was necessary to prevent the chaotic 'state of nature.'
Peter the Great
Russian tsar (r. 1682-1725) who forcibly westernized Russia's military, bureaucracy, and culture, founding St. Petersburg as a 'window to the West.'
Junkers
Prussian landed nobility who exchanged military/bureaucratic service to the state for control over serfs, key to Prussian absolutism.
Frederick the Great
Prussian king who practiced 'enlightened absolutism,' seizing Silesia from Austria while promoting legal reform and religious toleration.
Dutch East India Company (VOC)
Chartered in 1602, this joint-stock trading company pioneered modern corporate structure and dominated Dutch overseas commerce.
Divine Right Theory
Political doctrine, championed by Bishop Bossuet, holding that monarchs derive authority directly from God and answer only to Him.
English Bill of Rights (1689)
Document affirming parliamentary supremacy, requiring consent for taxation, and limiting royal power after the Glorious Revolution.

Unit 4: Scientific Revolution & Enlightenment

Nicolaus Copernicus
Polish astronomer whose heliocentric model (1543) proposed the sun, not Earth, as the center of the universe, launching the Scientific Revolution.
Isaac Newton
English scientist whose Principia Mathematica (1687) unified earlier discoveries into laws of universal gravitation and motion.
Galileo Galilei
Italian astronomer whose telescopic observations supported heliocentrism; tried by the Roman Inquisition in 1633 for teaching it.
Empiricism vs. Rationalism
Bacon's empiricism stressed observation/experiment (induction); Descartes's rationalism stressed logical deduction from first principles.
Baron de Montesquieu
French Enlightenment philosophe whose The Spirit of the Laws (1748) advocated separating government into executive, legislative, and judicial branches.
Jean-Jacques Rousseau
Enlightenment philosopher whose The Social Contract (1762) argued sovereignty rests in the collective 'general will' of the people.
Encyclopedie
Massive compendium of Enlightenment knowledge edited by Diderot and d'Alembert (1751-1772), aiming to rationalize and spread human understanding.
Deism
Enlightenment-era belief in a rational creator God who does not intervene in the universe's daily workings, often likened to a clockmaker.
Enlightened Absolutism
Governing style where monarchs like Catherine the Great, Joseph II, and Frederick the Great adopted selective Enlightenment reforms while preserving autocratic power.
Adam Smith
Scottish economist whose The Wealth of Nations (1776) argued for free markets and against mercantilist government trade control.

Unit 5: French Revolution & Napoleon

Estates-General
France's traditional assembly of three orders (clergy, nobility, commoners); convened in 1789 for the first time since 1614, triggering the revolution.
Tennis Court Oath
June 1789 pledge by the Third Estate (as the National Assembly) not to disband until France had a written constitution.
Storming of the Bastille
July 14, 1789 attack on a Paris prison-fortress that became the symbolic start of the French Revolution.
Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen
August 1789 document proclaiming liberty, equality, property, and popular sovereignty as natural rights.
Maximilien Robespierre
Radical Jacobin leader who dominated the Committee of Public Safety and directed the Reign of Terror (1793-1794).
Reign of Terror
1793-1794 period of mass executions of perceived enemies of the revolution, ended when Robespierre himself was executed.
Napoleonic Code
1804 legal code that standardized French law and guaranteed legal equality for men, while restricting women's rights.
Continental System
Napoleon's 1806 economic blockade attempting to cut Britain off from European trade.
Invasion of Russia (1812)
Napoleon's disastrous campaign that destroyed most of his Grande Armée and marked the turning point of his downfall.
Congress of Vienna
1814-1815 meeting of European powers that redrew the map of Europe and restored conservative monarchies after Napoleon's defeat.

Unit 6: Industrialization & Isms

Industrial Revolution
The transformation, beginning in Britain around 1750-1850, from hand production to machine-based manufacturing and factories.
James Watt
Inventor whose improved steam engine (1769) powered factories, mines, and eventually railways during industrialization.
Classical Liberalism
19th-century ideology favoring individual rights, constitutional government, and laissez-faire economics, associated with thinkers like Adam Smith and J.S. Mill.
Klemens von Metternich
Austrian statesman who championed conservatism and the balance of power at the Congress of Vienna.
Karl Marx
German philosopher who, with Friedrich Engels, wrote The Communist Manifesto (1848), arguing history is driven by class struggle.
Utopian Socialism
Early 19th-century socialist thought (Robert Owen, Charles Fourier) proposing cooperative communities to fix industrial capitalism's problems.
Camillo di Cavour
Prime minister of Piedmont-Sardinia who used diplomacy and war to lead the political unification of Italy.
Giuseppe Garibaldi
Nationalist leader whose volunteer 'Red Shirts' conquered Sicily and Naples in 1860, aiding Italian unification.
Otto von Bismarck
Prussian chancellor who unified Germany through 'blood and iron' via three wars between 1864 and 1871.
Franco-Prussian War
1870-1871 conflict that Prussia won decisively, leading to the proclamation of the German Empire at Versailles.

Unit 7: WWI & Interwar Period

Triple Alliance / Triple Entente
The two rival pre-WWI alliance systems: Germany-Austria-Hungary-Italy versus France-Russia-Britain.
Assassination of Franz Ferdinand
June 28, 1914 killing of the Austro-Hungarian archduke by Gavrilo Princip, the spark that triggered WWI.
Schlieffen Plan
German strategy for a rapid defeat of France through Belgium before turning to fight Russia; it failed to achieve quick victory.
Trench Warfare
Defensive stalemate warfare on the Western Front, exemplified by the Battles of Verdun and the Somme (1916).
Treaty of Versailles
1919 peace treaty that blamed Germany for WWI (Article 231), imposed reparations, and stripped German territory and colonies.
League of Nations
International organization created after WWI to preserve peace, weakened by lacking a military and by U.S. non-membership.
Benito Mussolini
Founder of Italian Fascism who seized power after the 1922 March on Rome.
Weimar Republic
Germany's fragile democratic government (1919-1933) that collapsed under economic crisis and extremist pressure, paving the way for Hitler.
Enabling Act (1933)
Law granting Hitler dictatorial power to enact legislation without the Reichstag, ending German democracy.
Great Depression
Global economic collapse beginning in 1929 that devastated European economies and fueled the rise of fascism and Nazism.

Unit 8: WWII & Modern Europe

Appeasement
British and French policy of conceding to Hitler's demands (e.g., Munich Conference, 1938) to avoid war; it failed to stop German aggression.
Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact
1939 non-aggression agreement between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union that secretly divided Eastern Europe.
Blitzkrieg
'Lightning war' tactic combining fast tanks, infantry, and air power to rapidly overwhelm opponents like Poland and France.
Operation Barbarossa
Germany's 1941 invasion of the Soviet Union, opening the massive and ultimately decisive Eastern Front.
Battle of Stalingrad
1942-1943 Soviet victory that destroyed the German Sixth Army and marked a major turning point of WWII.
D-Day
June 6, 1944 Allied invasion of Normandy that opened a Western Front against Nazi Germany.
The Holocaust
Nazi Germany's systematic genocide of six million Jews and millions of other victims during WWII.
Wannsee Conference
January 1942 meeting where Nazi officials formalized the 'Final Solution,' the plan to exterminate European Jews.
Iron Curtain
Term popularized by Winston Churchill (1946) describing the ideological divide between Soviet-controlled Eastern Europe and the democratic West.
European Coal and Steel Community
1951 organization (precursor to the EU) linking France, West Germany, Italy, and the Benelux countries to make future war between them 'materially impossible.'

Unit 1: Renaissance & Exploration

Italian Renaissance Humanism
Humanism was a scholarly movement emphasizing classical Greek and Roman texts, secular subjects, and individual human potential, arising first in the wealthy city-states of Italy.
Renaissance Art and Patronage
Renaissance artists broke from medieval flat, religious styles to use linear perspective, anatomical realism, and classical themes, funded largely by wealthy patrons and the Church.
Political Thought: Machiavelli and the Italian City-States
The competitive, war-torn environment of Italian city-states produced new, pragmatic political theory separating politics from traditional morality.
Christian (Northern) Humanism
Northern humanists combined classical learning with a desire to reform the Church and society through education and biblical scholarship, laying groundwork for the Reformation.
Age of Exploration: Motives and Technology
European overseas exploration in the 15th-16th centuries was driven by economic ambition, religious zeal, and national rivalry, made possible by new maritime technology.
Consequences of Exploration: The Columbian Exchange
European colonization of the Americas produced a massive transfer of goods, people, diseases, and ideas between the Old and New Worlds, transforming global economies.
Key fact
Renaissance humanism began in Italian city-states in the 14th century and emphasized classical texts and individual achievement.
Key fact
Gutenberg's printing press (c. 1450) revolutionized the spread of ideas across Europe.
Key fact
The 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas divided New World claims between Spain and Portugal along a papal line.
Key fact
The Columbian Exchange transformed diets, economies, and demographics on both sides of the Atlantic.

Unit 2: Reformation & Wars of Religion

Luther and the Break with Rome
Martin Luther's theological challenge to Catholic practices, especially indulgences, sparked a religious revolution that fractured Western Christianity.
Calvinism and the Radical Reformation
Beyond Luther, other reformers pushed Protestant theology further, producing new denominations with distinct doctrines on salvation, church governance, and society.
The English Reformation
England's break from Rome originated in political and dynastic concerns rather than pure theology, producing a distinctive middle path between Catholicism and Protestantism.
Catholic (Counter) Reformation
The Catholic Church responded to Protestantism with internal reform, renewed doctrine, and active efforts to reclaim lost followers.
The French Wars of Religion
France was torn apart by decades of conflict between Catholics and Protestant Huguenots, culminating in a political compromise.
The Thirty Years' War (1618-1648)
What began as a conflict between Catholics and Protestants within the Holy Roman Empire grew into a broader European power struggle, devastating Central Europe.
Key fact
Luther's 1517 Ninety-Five Theses and doctrine of sola fide launched the Protestant Reformation.
Key fact
The Peace of Augsburg (1555) and later the Peace of Westphalia (1648) established principles of territorial religious settlement.
Key fact
The Council of Trent (1545-1563) defined the Catholic Counter-Reformation response to Protestant theology.
Key fact
The Thirty Years' War (1618-1648) was Europe's most destructive religious-political conflict before the 20th century.

Unit 3: Absolutism & Constitutionalism

French Absolutism under Louis XIV
Louis XIV epitomized absolute monarchy, centralizing power, controlling the nobility, and asserting divine right rule from his palace at Versailles.
English Constitutionalism and the Civil War
England diverged from the continental absolutist model through conflicts between crown and Parliament that established constitutional limits on royal power.
Russian Absolutism: Peter the Great
Peter the Great forcibly modernized and westernized Russia, expanding autocratic state power to compete with Western European states.
Prussian Absolutism and the Hohenzollerns
Brandenburg-Prussia built a powerful, militarized absolutist state through alliance between the monarchy and the landed nobility (Junkers).
The Dutch Republic: A Constitutional Alternative
The Dutch Republic developed a decentralized, commercially driven state without a traditional absolute monarch, becoming a 17th-century economic and cultural powerhouse.
Constitutionalism vs. Absolutism: Comparative Framework
Historians contrast absolutist states, where monarchs claimed unchecked sovereign power, with constitutional states, where power was shared with representative institutions.
Key fact
Louis XIV's Versailles-centered absolutism became the model 'Sun King' style of divine right monarchy.
Key fact
The English Glorious Revolution (1688) and Bill of Rights (1689) established constitutional monarchy limited by Parliament.
Key fact
Peter the Great westernized Russia's military, bureaucracy, and culture while preserving autocratic power.
Key fact
Hobbes's Leviathan and Locke's Two Treatises represent opposing philosophical justifications for absolutism and constitutionalism, respectively.

Unit 4: Scientific Revolution & Enlightenment

The Copernican Revolution
Nicolaus Copernicus's heliocentric model challenged the centuries-old Ptolemaic geocentric universe, launching the Scientific Revolution.
Newton and the New Scientific Method
Isaac Newton synthesized earlier discoveries into a unified system of physical laws, while new methodologies (empiricism and rationalism) reshaped how knowledge was pursued.
Enlightenment Political Philosophy
Enlightenment thinkers applied reason to politics and society, producing influential theories about natural rights, government legitimacy, and the separation of powers.
The Philosophes and the Republic of Letters
A network of writers, salons, and publications spread Enlightenment ideas across Europe, often challenging traditional authority and superstition.
Enlightened Absolutism
Some European monarchs adopted Enlightenment ideas selectively to modernize and strengthen their states while retaining autocratic authority.
Skepticism, Religion, and Society
The Enlightenment also produced new critiques of organized religion and traditional social hierarchy, ranging from deism to outright atheism.
Key fact
The Scientific Revolution shifted authority from ancient/Church texts to observation, experiment, and mathematics (Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo, Newton).
Key fact
Locke, Montesquieu, and Rousseau developed foundational Enlightenment political theories on natural rights, separation of powers, and popular sovereignty.
Key fact
The Encyclopedie and salon culture spread Enlightenment ideas among the educated public across Europe.
Key fact
Enlightened absolutists like Catherine the Great, Joseph II, and Frederick the Great applied reform selectively while preserving autocracy.

Unit 5: French Revolution & Napoleon

Causes of the French Revolution
By 1789 France faced a fiscal crisis layered on top of social inequality and new political ideas, producing a revolutionary situation the monarchy could not contain.
1789: Revolution Begins
The summer of 1789 saw the collapse of royal authority and the first major legislative acts dismantling the Old Regime.
Radicalization and the Reign of Terror
War with European monarchies and internal factional struggles pushed the revolution toward radical, violent measures between 1792 and 1794.
Napoleon's Rise to Power
Napoleon Bonaparte exploited the instability of the Directory to seize power, eventually transforming France into an empire under his personal rule.
Napoleonic Wars and Fall
Napoleon's empire reached its height around 1810 but collapsed after disastrous overreach, ending in his final defeat at Waterloo.
Key fact
The National Assembly's Tennis Court Oath (June 1789) and the storming of the Bastille (July 14, 1789) mark the revolution's opening acts.
Key fact
The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789) enshrined Enlightenment principles of liberty and equality into law.
Key fact
The Reign of Terror (1793-1794), directed by Robespierre's Committee of Public Safety, killed tens of thousands in the name of defending the revolution.
Key fact
Napoleon's Civil Code and administrative reforms spread revolutionary legal principles across Europe even as his empire enforced French domination.

Unit 6: Industrialization & Isms

The Industrial Revolution
Beginning in Britain in the mid-1700s, the Industrial Revolution transformed production from hand labor to machine-based manufacturing, reshaping economies and societies across Europe.
Liberalism and Conservatism
The 19th century's political spectrum was defined by classical liberalism's push for reform and conservatism's defense of tradition after the upheavals of the French Revolution.
Nationalism and Socialism
Nationalism and socialism emerged as powerful ideologies responding to industrial change, offering competing visions of unity and social justice.
Unification of Italy
Italy achieved political unification between 1859 and 1871 through a combination of nationalist idealism, diplomacy, and military conflict, led primarily by the Kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia.
Unification of Germany
German unification was achieved under Prussian leadership through Otto von Bismarck's calculated use of 'blood and iron,' culminating in the proclamation of the German Empire in 1871.
Key fact
The Industrial Revolution began in Britain due to coal, capital, colonial markets, and political stability, then spread to continental Europe after 1830.
Key fact
Liberalism favored individual rights and constitutional limits on power, while conservatism (Metternich, Burke) favored tradition and monarchical order after 1815.
Key fact
Marx and Engels's Communist Manifesto (1848) provided socialism's foundational theory of class struggle and inevitable proletarian revolution.
Key fact
Italy (1861-1870, led by Cavour and Garibaldi) and Germany (1871, led by Bismarck) both unified through combinations of diplomacy, nationalism, and war.

Unit 7: WWI & Interwar Period

Causes and Alliances of World War I
A web of alliances, militarism, imperial rivalry, and nationalism turned a regional Balkan crisis into a continent-wide war in 1914.
The Course of the War
World War I became a grueling war of attrition marked by trench warfare on the Western Front and new, devastating military technology.
The Treaty of Versailles
The 1919 peace settlement imposed harsh terms on Germany and redrew Europe's map, planting seeds of resentment that contributed to future conflict.
Rise of Fascism and Nazism
Economic hardship, political instability, and resentment over WWI's outcomes enabled fascist and Nazi movements to seize power in Italy and Germany.
The Great Depression in Europe
The global economic collapse that began with the 1929 U.S. stock market crash devastated European economies and destabilized fragile democracies.
Key fact
World War I (1914-1918) was caused by militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism, and was ignited by the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand.
Key fact
The Treaty of Versailles (1919) blamed Germany for the war (Article 231), imposed reparations, and created a League of Nations without U.S. membership.
Key fact
The Great Depression (from 1929) devastated Weimar Germany's economy, directly enabling Hitler's Nazi Party to rise to power by 1933.
Key fact
Mussolini's Fascist Italy (from 1922) and Hitler's Nazi Germany (from 1933) both used extreme nationalism and dictatorship to replace fragile democratic governments.

Unit 8: WWII & Modern Europe

Causes and Early Events of World War II
Aggressive fascist expansion, appeasement by Western democracies, and the failure of collective security led to the outbreak of World War II in 1939.
The Course of World War II
The war expanded globally after 1941, with the Eastern Front and the entry of the United States proving decisive to the Allied victory.
The Holocaust
Nazi Germany's systematic, state-organized genocide murdered six million Jews and millions of other victims in the deadliest atrocity of the 20th century.
The Cold War Division of Europe
After 1945, ideological and geopolitical rivalry between the United States and Soviet Union split Europe into competing capitalist and communist blocs for decades.
European Integration and the End of the Cold War
In the postwar decades, Western Europe pursued economic and political integration while the Soviet bloc eventually collapsed, reshaping the continent by the early 1990s.
Key fact
World War II began with Germany's invasion of Poland (September 1939) after the failure of appeasement and the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact.
Key fact
Turning points including Stalingrad (1942-1943), D-Day (1944), and the atomic bombings of Japan (1945) led to Allied victory in Europe (May 1945) and the Pacific (August 1945).
Key fact
The Holocaust was the Nazi regime's systematic genocide of six million Jews and millions of other victims, formalized at the 1942 Wannsee Conference.
Key fact
The Cold War (1945-1991) divided Europe into a NATO-aligned West and Soviet-controlled East until the fall of the Berlin Wall (1989) and Soviet collapse (1991), while Western Europe simultaneously built the institutions that became the European Union.

Unit 1: Renaissance & Exploration

Watch out
The Renaissance was a revival and adaptation of classical ideas, not a total rejection of medieval Christianity.
Watch out
Machiavelli's The Prince describes how power is actually gained and kept; it is not a moral endorsement of cruelty for its own sake.
Watch out
Christian humanists like Erasmus sought Church reform from within, not a break from Catholicism (unlike later Protestant reformers).
Watch out
Exploration was driven by economic and religious motives together, not by scientific curiosity alone.

Unit 2: Reformation & Wars of Religion

Watch out
Henry VIII's break from Rome was driven primarily by the succession/annulment issue, not by embracing Protestant theology.
Watch out
The Peace of Westphalia recognized state sovereignty and added Calvinism to the accepted faiths; it did not end all religious conflict in Europe.
Watch out
The Thirty Years' War became a political struggle (France vs. Habsburgs) as much as a religious one, not purely Catholic vs. Protestant.
Watch out
The Edict of Nantes granted toleration to Huguenots; it did not make Protestantism the state religion of France.

Unit 3: Absolutism & Constitutionalism

Watch out
Absolutist monarchs like Louis XIV still had to negotiate with nobles and could not tax at will without practical limits, not literally unlimited power.
Watch out
The Glorious Revolution was relatively bloodless in England itself; 'glorious' refers to its peaceful transfer of power, not to a lack of any conflict elsewhere.
Watch out
Enlightened absolutists like Frederick the Great and Catherine the Great pursued reforms to strengthen the state, not because they rejected autocracy.
Watch out
The Dutch Republic was a decentralized confederation, not a unified centralized nation-state like France.

Unit 4: Scientific Revolution & Enlightenment

Watch out
Galileo's conflict with the Church was primarily about specific claims of proven fact versus hypothesis, not a simple science-vs-religion war with no nuance.
Watch out
Deism accepted a rational creator God; it did not equal atheism, which only a minority of radical Enlightenment thinkers embraced.
Watch out
Enlightened absolutists reformed to strengthen state power and efficiency, not to relinquish or share their own authority.
Watch out
Rousseau's 'general will' emphasized collective popular sovereignty, distinct from Locke's individual natural rights framework.

Unit 5: French Revolution & Napoleon

Watch out
The storming of the Bastille freed only seven prisoners; its importance is symbolic, not practical.
Watch out
The Reign of Terror was carried out by the radical Jacobins under Robespierre, not by the moderate Girondins, who were among its victims.
Watch out
Napoleon preserved many revolutionary gains (legal equality, abolition of feudalism) even while restoring authoritarian, monarchical-style rule — the revolution was consolidated, not fully reversed.
Watch out
The Congress of Vienna aimed to restore the balance of power and legitimate monarchies, not to punish France into economic ruin (unlike the later Treaty of Versailles).

Unit 6: Industrialization & Isms

Watch out
Bismarck was a conservative monarchist pursuing Realpolitik, not a liberal nationalist — he used nationalism as a tool to strengthen Prussian/monarchical power, not to create a democracy.
Watch out
The Congress of Vienna (1815) aimed to restore balance of power and legitimacy, not to punish France harshly (contrast with the Treaty of Versailles in 1919).
Watch out
Utopian socialism (Owen, Fourier) predates and differs from Marx's 'scientific socialism,' which claimed to be based on historical/economic analysis rather than idealistic community-building.
Watch out
The revolutions of 1848 largely failed in the short term (old regimes were restored), even though they spread liberal and nationalist ideas that shaped later unification movements.

Unit 7: WWI & Interwar Period

Watch out
The assassination of Franz Ferdinand was the spark, not the sole cause, of WWI — deep structural tensions (alliances, militarism, imperialism, nationalism) made a general war likely regardless.
Watch out
The League of Nations failed largely because it lacked an army and the U.S. never joined, not because the concept of collective security itself was tried and rejected worldwide.
Watch out
Hitler was legally appointed chancellor in 1933 through political maneuvering, not a violent coup — the Beer Hall Putsch (1923), his actual coup attempt, had failed.
Watch out
The Great Depression was a global economic crisis triggered largely by the 1929 US stock market crash and international debt structures, not caused primarily by the Treaty of Versailles, though war reparations worsened Germany's vulnerability.

Unit 8: WWII & Modern Europe

Watch out
Appeasement at Munich (1938) was intended to avoid war by conceding the Sudetenland, but it emboldened Hitler rather than satisfying his ambitions — it did not stop further aggression.
Watch out
The Soviet Union was initially allied with Nazi Germany via the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact (1939) before being invaded by Germany in 1941; it was not part of the Allies from the war's start.
Watch out
The Holocaust refers specifically to the genocide of six million Jews, though Nazi persecution and mass murder also targeted Roma, disabled people, and other groups — these are related but distinct from the Holocaust's specific Jewish genocide focus in strict definition.
Watch out
European integration (Coal and Steel Community, EEC, EU) began as an economic and peace project among Western European states in the 1950s, well before the Cold War ended — it was not a response to the Soviet collapse in 1991.
