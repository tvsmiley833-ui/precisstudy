Unit 1: World Literature & Cultural Context4 concepts · ~18 min

▾

Reading across cultures

World lit asks you to bracket assumptions and read values from the inside.
  • Cultural context = beliefs, history, and social structures shaping a text's meaning
  • Avoid ethnocentrism: judge actions by the text's value system first, then compare
  • Translation layers matter: wordplay/idioms may not survive language transfer
  • Universals vs particulars: shared human themes wear culture-specific clothing

Oral traditions & archetypes

Many world texts began spoken — structure carries their fingerprints.
  • Repetition/formula phrases aided memorization (epithets, refrains)
  • Trickster figures (Anansi, Coyote) enforce norms by breaking them
  • Creation myths encode a culture's cosmology and hierarchy
  • Archetypes recur cross-culturally: hero's journey, flood narrative, descent to underworld

Colonial & postcolonial lenses

Power shapes whose stories get told and how.
  • Colonial literature often frames colonized people through the colonizer's gaze
  • Postcolonial writing reclaims voice: hybridity, resisting narratives, writing back
  • Terms: othering, assimilation, diaspora, subaltern
  • Read for whose perspective centers the story and whose is erased

Close reading with context

Context informs but evidence proves — balance both.
  • Research customs only after first-pass reading; don't outsource interpretation
  • Anchor claims in textual detail + cultural knowledge together
  • Note your own lens: what surprises you reveals YOUR assumptions
  • Compare across texts: how do two cultures treat hospitality, honor, fate?
Don't grade historical characters by modern standards without noting the anachronism
'Universal theme' claims still need textual proof from THAT text
Translated names/terms carry weight — check transliteration notes

Unit 2: Rhetorical Analysis4 concepts · ~18 min

▾

The rhetorical situation

Every text answers to speaker, purpose, audience, context, exigence.
  • Speaker: constructed voice with stakes and credibility moves
  • Purpose: what the text tries to DO (persuade, move, console, provoke)
  • Audience: intended readers shape vocabulary, allusions, tone choices
  • Exigence: the occasion/urgency that called the text into being

Tone & diction analysis

Word choice is the fingerprint of attitude.
  • Name tone precisely: wry, elegiac, indignant, clinical — never just 'negative'
  • Diction levels: formal/casual/colloquial/archaic — shifts signal pivots
  • Connotation vs denotation: 'thrifty' vs 'stingy' same denotation, opposite spin
  • Imagery patterns cluster around motifs supporting the purpose

Structure & syntax moves

How sentences build IS argumentation.
  • Short sentence after long ones lands like a hammer — deliberate emphasis
  • Parallelism binds equal ideas; antithesis sets opposites colliding
  • Repetition + anaphora build momentum toward a climax
  • Opening/closing positions carry primacy/recency weight — authors load them

Writing the analysis essay

Analysis explains HOW choices work on audiences — not whether you agree.
  • Thesis names strategy + purpose: 'Through X, author Y accomplishes Z for audience W'
  • Body paragraphs organize by MOVES, not by chronology of the passage
  • Quote → name device → explain audience effect → tie to purpose
  • Never editorialize about the topic itself — analyze the persuasion craft
Summarizing content ≠ analyzing rhetoric — always answer HOW the writing works
Device-spotting without effect ('this has anaphora') earns nothing
Your opinion of the ISSUE is irrelevant in rhetorical analysis

Unit 3: Shakespearean Drama & Tragedy4 concepts · ~18 min

▾

Tragic hero architecture

The noble protagonist falls through hamartia — fatal flaw meeting circumstance.
  • Hamartia: tragic error/flaw (ambition, jealousy, indecision)
  • Peripeteia: reversal of fortune at the midpoint turn
  • Anagnorisis: recognition — hero finally sees truth, too late
  • Catharsis: audience purges pity+fear through witnessing the fall

Soliloquy, aside, monologue

Shakespeare's interiority tools differ precisely.
  • Soliloquy: ALONE on stage, speaking thoughts to audience — direct mind-access
  • Aside: brief remark to audience hidden from other characters
  • Monologue: long speech TO other characters (they hear it)
  • These expose motive gaps between appearance and reality — dramatic irony fuel

Dramatic irony & foreshadowing

Audience foreknowledge converts scenes into suspense machines.
  • Witches' prophecies make every choice read as fate-vs-free-will evidence
  • Dramatic irony: we know Duncan's trust is doomed; he doesn't
  • Foreshadowing devices: omens, storms, blood imagery accumulating
  • Pathetic fallacy: nature mirrors moral disorder (thunder during regicide)

Language: verse, prose & imagery

Form marks class and sanity in Shakespeare.
  • Nobles speak blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter); commoners prose
  • Falling INTO prose can signal madness/loss of control; rising to verse = restored order
  • Motif webs: blood, sleep, hands, darkness track guilt in Macbeth
  • Apostrophe: addressing absent things ('Sleep, O gentle Sleep…')
Monologue ≠ soliloquy — presence of listeners is the test
Prophecies don't excuse characters: they act on interpretations (free will debate)
Comic relief scenes often deepen theme, not distract from it

Unit 4: Dystopian Literature & Allegory4 concepts · ~18 min

▾

Dystopia as warning

Dystopias exaggerate present trends into cautionary futures.
  • Utopia = perfect society (ironically 'no-place'); dystopia = its nightmare inversion
  • Control levers: surveillance, language restriction, history rewriting, engineered pleasure
  • Protagonist awakening arc: compliance → doubt → rebellion → ambiguous cost
  • Warnings target the author's PRESENT — read historically to decode targets

Totalitarian toolkits in fiction

Classic dystopias map how freedom erodes step by step.
  • Newspeak-style language control limits thinkable thoughts (linguistic determinism)
  • Surveillance state: panopticon effect — victims internalize the watching gaze
  • Slogans replace reasoning; contradictions embraced as loyalty tests (doublethink)
  • Bread & circuses: engineered distraction pacifies more efficiently than fear

Allegory mechanics

Allegories run double stories: surface plot plus systematic second meaning.
  • Every element maps consistently: characters/settings/events = abstract ideas
  • Fable = brief allegory with stated moral; parable = spiritual teaching frame
  • Track correspondences on a character-concept chart for sustained allegories
  • Degree matters: pure one-to-one allegory vs symbolic realism differ in rigidity

Satire inside allegory

Many allegories satirize specific regimes or human failings.
  • Satire critiques through humor/irony — Horatian gentle vs Juvenalian biting
  • Exaggeration + inversion expose real absurdities by magnifying them
  • Targets may be systems (totalitarianism) AND betrayals within resistance movements
  • Ask what behavior gets punished or mocked — that reveals the defended value
Don't flatten allegory into decoder-ring exercise — literary texture still carries meaning
'Orwellian' means language/truth manipulation, not just any government disliked
One group's utopia can be another's dystopia — perspective determines label

Unit 5: Argumentation & Logical Fallacies4 concepts · ~18 min

▾

Formal logic basics

Arguments chain premises into conclusions; validity and truth are separate checks.
  • Deductive reasoning: true premises + valid structure = necessarily true conclusion
  • Inductive reasoning: observed patterns support probable (not guaranteed) conclusions
  • Syllogism: major premise + minor premise → conclusion
  • Soundness requires BOTH valid structure AND actually-true premises

Relevance fallacies

These fallacies distract from the actual question at issue.
  • Ad hominem attacks the arguer instead of the argument's merits
  • Straw man refutes a distorted, weakened version of the opponent's actual claim
  • Red herring drags in irrelevant material to divert attention
  • Bandwagon appeal treats popularity as evidence — it is not

Causation & emotional fallacies

Cause-effect shortcuts and feeling substitutions replace proof.
  • Post hoc ergo propter hoc: sequence mistaken for causation
  • Hasty generalization projects sweeping claims from tiny samples
  • Slippery slope asserts an unproven chain of escalating consequences
  • Appeal to fear/pity substitutes emotional pressure for evidence

Resisting manipulation

Media literacy is fallacy detection at reading speed.
  • Loaded language smuggles conclusions into supposedly neutral descriptions
  • Check source incentives: who profits if this claim spreads?
  • Steelman opposing views before concluding — test against their best case
  • Statistics without denominators mislead: 'doubled' from 1 to 2 cases
Ad hominem ≠ insulting someone generally — it's dismissing their ARGUMENT via the person
Each slippery-slope link needs independent justification; chains fail link-by-link
Correlation studies need controls before causal headlines earn belief

Unit 6: Poetry Analysis II4 concepts · ~18 min

▾

Tone mapping & shifts

Track attitude word by word; good poems move.
  • Name tone precisely on spectra: reverent-mocking, elegiac-celebratory, wistful-bitter
  • Mark shift signals: 'but/yet', stanza breaks, volta, image-field changes
  • Shift analysis answers WHY the poem needed to turn at that exact point
  • Ending tone often retroactively revises earlier lines — reread on finishing

Ambiguity as design

Great poems deliberately mean more than one thing at once.
  • Intentional ambiguity resists flattening into a single interpretation
  • Multiple readings coexist legitimately when each has textual grounding
  • Ambiguity differs from vagueness: precise words open multiple precise doors
  • Discuss competing readings, then argue which the text best supports

Extended metaphor & conceit

Conceits stretch comparison into load-bearing architecture.
  • Metaphysical conceit: startlingly intellectual parallels (lovers as compass legs)
  • Extended metaphor sustains one comparison across entire stanzas
  • Tracking every term of the conceit exposes the poem's argument structure
  • Mixed metaphors may be intentional — check whether context earns them

Comparative poetry essays

Comparison essays must argue about technique, not just summarize two poems.
  • Thesis compares TECHNIQUES serving purposes, never 'poem A is about X, poem B about Y'
  • Organize by element (tone shift / imagery / structure), alternating texts within paragraphs
  • Maintain balance: roughly equal attention to both poems throughout
  • Conclusion states what the comparison itself reveals — insight neither poem gives alone
Unbounded 'anything goes' readings ignore that text evidence bounds ambiguity
Summary sandwich isn't comparison — each paragraph needs an analytical claim
Modern lenses need period-aware framing plus textual anchors to persuade

Unit 7: Research & Citation (MLA)4 concepts · ~18 min

▾

Research question design

Good research starts with a question worth answering.
  • Scope: narrow enough to answer in essay length, broad enough to matter
  • Open beats closed: 'How did X affect Y?' invites analysis, not trivia recall
  • Preliminary searching tests whether sources actually exist
  • Thesis EVOLVES from evidence — never reverse-engineer sources to fit a fixed claim

Source evaluation (CRAAP)

Not every source deserves your citation.
  • Currency: date sensitivity varies by field — science fast-moving, history slower
  • Authority: author credentials, publisher reputation, peer-review markers
  • Accuracy: internal citations, verifiable data, absence of howler errors
  • Purpose/bias: advocacy sites disguise themselves — check About pages and funders

MLA in-text citations

MLA uses author-page parentheticals with graceful signal phrasing.
  • Basic form: (Fitzgerald 88) — no comma, no 'p.'
  • Vary signal verbs: argues, observes, counters, acknowledges
  • Prose attribution example: Fitzgerald suggests the parties mask loneliness (55).
  • No author → shortened title; web pages without pagination → author only

Works Cited & plagiarism

Every borrowed idea gets credit — not just direct quotes.
  • Alphabetical list, hanging indent at 0.5 inch on second lines
  • Container system: article inside journal inside database — nested titles
  • Cite paraphrases AND summaries; ideas carry ownership, not just wording
  • Common-knowledge exception is narrower than students hope: when unsure, cite
'I reworded it' does not erase the citation requirement — ideas are owned
Wikipedia is a source-finding tool, not itself a citable authority
Web sources without page numbers cite author alone or shortened title

Unit 8: Style & Syntax4 concepts · ~18 min

▾

Sentence variety architecture

Rhythm keeps readers awake; deliberate patterns create emphasis.
  • Mix lengths: short sentences punch; long ones chain logic
  • Vary openers — participial ('Exhausted, she…'), prepositional, adverbial starts
  • Cumulative sentences add detail after the main clause; periodic save the verb for suspense
  • Vary any run of three same-shape sentences — rhythm rules of thumb

Diction precision & connotation

Exact words earn their slot; near-synonyms betray fuzzy thinking.
  • Prefer concrete over abstract: 'walked heavily' beats 'moved'
  • Connotation audit: cheap / inexpensive / thrifty carry opposite judgments
  • Delete intensifiers (very, really) — stronger verbs do that work
  • Register must match: slang breaks academic tone; pomposity breaks trust

Active voice & concision

Strong subjects performing strong verbs read fastest.
  • Active: The committee rejected the proposal. Passive hides or delays the actor
  • Passive earns its keep when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or the receiver deserves emphasis
  • Wordiness patterns: there-is openers and nominalizations (make a decision → decide)
  • Redundancy cuts: advance planning, end result, absolutely essential

Revision hierarchy

Revise big-to-small: argument before paragraphs before words before commas.
  • Pass 1: thesis integrity and paragraph order serve the argument
  • Pass 2: PEEL completeness inside paragraphs plus transitions between moves
  • Pass 3: sentence concision, variety, active-voice audit
  • Pass 4: grammar, punctuation, MLA format — mechanics come last
Passive voice is a tool, not a crime — blanket bans misfire in methods sections
Thesaurus swaps produce wrong connotations: pensive is not interchangeable with sad
Fixing commas first wastes effort when the argument still needs restructuring
