Unit 1: Public Speaking Foundations3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Audience, Purpose and Occasion
Every effective speech starts by analyzing who is listening and why.
  • Purposes: to inform, to persuade, or to entertain/commemorate (special occasion)
  • Audience analysis: demographics (age, background) and attitudes (knowledge, beliefs, interest)
  • Adapt vocabulary, examples and length to the audience and setting
  • A specific purpose statement names what you want the audience to know, believe or do
  • The occasion (classroom, ceremony, competition) shapes tone and formality
Rhetorical Appeals
Aristotle identified three modes of persuasion.
  • Ethos: credibility, from expertise, trustworthiness and goodwill
  • Pathos: emotional appeal through stories, vivid language and shared values
  • Logos: logical appeal through evidence, statistics and reasoning
  • Strong speeches balance all three; overreliance on pathos can seem manipulative
  • Kairos: the timeliness or appropriateness of an argument for the moment
Managing Speech Anxiety
Nervousness is normal and manageable.
  • Most speakers feel anxious; audiences usually notice much less than the speaker feels
  • Preparation and repeated rehearsal out loud reduce anxiety the most
  • Slow breathing calms the physical stress response
  • Focus on the message and the audience rather than on yourself
  • Visualization: imagine delivering the speech successfully
Ethos isn't 'ethics'. It's the audience's perception of your credibility.
Informing and persuading are different goals; don't slip into advocacy in an informative speech.
Memorizing word for word often increases anxiety and sounds stiff.
Unit 2: Speech Organization3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Introductions
An introduction must win attention and prepare the audience for what follows.
  • Attention-getter: a question, startling statistic, story, quotation or vivid image
  • Reveal the topic and relate it to the audience's lives
  • Establish credibility: why should they listen to you?
  • Thesis statement: the central idea in one sentence
  • Preview the main points so listeners can follow
Body and Patterns
Main points should follow a clear pattern.
  • Chronological: time order (history, processes)
  • Spatial: physical layout (a tour of a building)
  • Topical: logical categories (three benefits of exercise)
  • Cause–effect and problem–solution patterns; Monroe's Motivated Sequence for persuasion: attention, need, satisfaction, visualization, action
  • Transitions and signposts ('First…', 'Now that we've seen the problem…') guide listeners
Conclusions and Outlines
End strongly and plan with outlines.
  • Signal the ending, summarize main points and restate the thesis
  • Close with a memorable clincher: call back to the opening, a quotation or a call to action
  • Don't introduce new main points in the conclusion
  • A preparation outline uses full sentences; a speaking outline uses brief key words
  • Most speeches work best with 2–5 main points
Don't open with 'Hi, my speech is about…'. Start with an attention-getter.
New main points in the conclusion confuse listeners.
A speaking outline shouldn't be a full script.
Unit 3: Delivery & Body Language3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Methods of Delivery
How a speech is delivered changes how natural it sounds.
  • Manuscript: reading word for word; precise but often monotone
  • Memorized: no notes, but risks forgetting and stiffness
  • Impromptu: little or no preparation
  • Extemporaneous: carefully prepared and practiced, delivered from brief notes; preferred for most speeches
  • Extemp competition gives speakers about 30 minutes to prepare a speech on a current-events question
Vocal Delivery
Your voice carries meaning beyond your words.
  • Volume: loud enough for the back row
  • Rate: vary speed; slow down for key ideas
  • Pitch and inflection: avoid monotone
  • Pauses create emphasis and replace filler words like 'um' and 'like'
  • Articulation and pronunciation make words clear and correct
Nonverbal Delivery
Body language can support or undercut your message.
  • Eye contact builds connection and credibility; scan the whole room
  • Gestures should be natural and purposeful, not repetitive
  • Posture: stand balanced and open; avoid swaying or leaning
  • Facial expressions should match the tone of your message
  • Movement can mark transitions but shouldn't be random pacing
Extemporaneous doesn't mean unprepared. That's impromptu.
Reading a manuscript word for word usually loses eye contact.
Constant hand-waving distracts; purposeful gestures help.
Unit 4: Argument Construction3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Claim, Warrant, Impact
A complete argument explains why it's true and why it matters.
  • Claim: the point you're making
  • Warrant: the reasoning that explains why the claim is true
  • Evidence (data): facts, statistics, expert testimony supporting the warrant
  • Impact: why the claim matters (magnitude, probability, timeframe)
  • Toulmin model: claim, data, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal
Types of Reasoning
Different reasoning patterns have different strengths and weaknesses.
  • Deductive reasoning moves from general principles to a specific conclusion (a syllogism)
  • Inductive reasoning generalizes from specific examples
  • Causal reasoning claims one thing causes another; watch for confounding factors
  • Analogical reasoning compares two similar cases
  • Sign reasoning infers a condition from observable signs
Logical Fallacies
Fallacies are errors in reasoning that can look persuasive.
  • Ad hominem: attacking the person instead of the argument
  • Straw man: misrepresenting an opponent's argument to attack it easily
  • Slippery slope: claiming one step leads inevitably to extreme consequences without evidence
  • False dilemma: presenting only two options when more exist
  • Hasty generalization, post hoc ('after this, therefore because of this'), bandwagon and appeal to authority outside expertise
Evidence isn't a warrant; you must explain how evidence proves the claim.
Correlation presented as causation is the post hoc fallacy.
Calling out a fallacy isn't enough. Explain why the reasoning fails.
Unit 5: Evidence & Research3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Finding Evidence
Strong arguments depend on reliable, relevant evidence.
  • Types: statistics, examples, expert testimony, studies, historical precedent
  • Use library databases, government sources, peer-reviewed journals and reputable news outlets
  • Search with specific keywords and operators (quotation marks, site:.gov)
  • Record full citation information as you research
  • Look for evidence on both sides so you can anticipate responses
Evaluating Sources
Not all sources are equally trustworthy.
  • Check the author's expertise and possible bias
  • Check the date: recent evidence matters for changing issues
  • Prefer primary sources and peer-reviewed studies over secondhand summaries
  • Lateral reading: check what other sources say about a site before trusting it
  • Be wary of evidence without a clear source, or statistics without context
Citing and Using Evidence
Using evidence honestly is both an ethical and a strategic requirement.
  • Orally cite sources: author, credentials, publication and date
  • Never distort a quotation's meaning by clipping out context
  • Explain the evidence: tell the audience what it proves
  • Plagiarism, presenting others' words or ideas as your own, violates ethics and competition rules
  • In debate, a card is a quoted piece of evidence with a full citation
A professional-looking website isn't automatically credible.
Clipping a quotation to change its meaning is an ethics violation.
Evidence without explanation doesn't persuade judges.
Unit 6: Resolution Analysis3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Types of Resolutions
A debate resolution is the statement the two sides argue about.
  • Resolutions of fact: is something true? ('Social media harms teen mental health')
  • Resolutions of value: which value is more important? (common in Lincoln-Douglas)
  • Resolutions of policy: should an actor take an action? ('The USFG should…')
  • The affirmative supports the resolution; the negative opposes it
  • Presumption often favors the status quo, so the affirmative carries the burden of proof
Defining Terms
Definitions frame the whole debate.
  • Identify every key term in the resolution and find credible definitions
  • Topicality: whether the affirmative's case actually falls within the resolution
  • Look at the actor, the action and the object (who does what to what)
  • Consider ground: definitions should leave fair arguments for both sides
  • Framework: the lens (value, criterion or weighing mechanism) judges should use
Stock Issues in Policy Debate
Policy affirmatives traditionally must prove several stock issues.
  • Harms: a significant problem exists
  • Inherency: the status quo won't solve it on its own
  • Solvency: the plan would solve the problem
  • Topicality: the plan fits the resolution
  • Significance and advantages: the benefits outweigh disadvantages
Value resolutions aren't settled by facts alone; they need a weighing framework.
Inherency isn't the harm itself. It's why the status quo won't fix the harm.
Definitions that leave the other side no ground are often rejected by judges.
Unit 7: Refutation & Crossfire3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Four-Step Refutation
Refutation directly answers an opponent's argument.
  • Step 1, 'They say': summarize the opponent's argument fairly
  • Step 2, 'But': state your response
  • Step 3, 'Because': give reasoning and evidence
  • Step 4, 'Therefore': explain the impact on the round
  • Signpost which argument you're answering so the judge can follow on the flow
Types of Responses
Different attacks weaken arguments in different ways.
  • Deny: the evidence or reasoning is false
  • Minimize: the impact is smaller than claimed
  • Turn: the argument actually supports your side (a link or impact turn)
  • Outweigh: even if true, your impacts are bigger, more likely or sooner
  • Point out dropped arguments: an argument not answered is usually treated as conceded
Cross-Examination and Crossfire
Questioning periods clarify arguments and expose weaknesses.
  • Ask short, focused questions with a clear goal
  • Use questions to set up arguments you'll make in your next speech
  • Don't make speeches during questioning or ask open-ended questions that let opponents repeat their case
  • When answering, stay calm, be concise and don't concede more than necessary
  • Public Forum 'crossfire' lets both speakers question each other; 'grand crossfire' involves all four
Misrepresenting the opponent's argument before refuting it is a straw man.
Cross-ex isn't a time for speeches.
Answering some arguments well can still lose if you drop a key one.
Unit 8: Event Formats (CX, LD, PF)3 concepts · ~15 min▾
Policy Debate (CX)
Two-person teams debate whether the government should adopt a specific plan.
  • Teams of two; a year-long policy resolution
  • Structure: four constructive speeches, each followed by cross-examination, then four rebuttals
  • Evidence-heavy; debaters often speak very quickly
  • Affirmative presents a plan; negative may run disadvantages, counterplans, topicality and kritiks
  • Named 'CX' for its cross-examination periods
Lincoln-Douglas (LD)
One-on-one debate focused on values and philosophy.
  • Named for the 1858 Lincoln–Douglas Senate debates
  • Resolutions usually ask a value question and change every two months
  • Each side presents a value (e.g. justice) and a criterion that measures it (e.g. protecting rights)
  • Speeches: AC, cross-ex, NC, cross-ex, 1AR, NR, 2AR
  • Philosophers such as Kant, Mill, Locke and Rawls often appear
Public Forum (PF)
Two-person teams debate current events for a general audience.
  • Created to be accessible to lay judges and audiences
  • Resolutions change monthly and address current events
  • A coin toss decides side and speaking order
  • Speeches: constructive, rebuttal, summary and final focus, with crossfire periods including grand crossfire
  • Emphasizes clear communication and evidence-based argument without a formal plan
LD is one-on-one; CX and PF are two-person teams.
PF teams don't present formal plans the way CX affirmatives do.
A criterion isn't a second value. It's how you measure the value.
