Unit 1: Period 1: 1491-16076 concepts · ~34 min

▾

Native American societies before contact

Diverse Native societies developed distinct economies and social structures shaped by regional environments.
  • Southwest (Pueblo, Hohokam): irrigated agriculture (maize, beans, squash), adobe/cliff-dwelling architecture, permanent villages
  • Great Plains: nomadic/semi-nomadic, hunted bison on foot before horses arrived, seasonal migration
  • Pacific Northwest: abundant salmon fishing supported large, complex, sedentary societies with social hierarchy (potlatch)
  • Mississippian culture (Cahokia): large agricultural surplus, mound-building, extensive trade networks, complex chiefdoms
  • Northeast (Iroquois Confederacy): matrilineal clans, longhouses, Great League of Peace united five (later six) nations

European motives for exploration

'God, Gold, and Glory' drove Spanish, Portuguese, French, and English exploration in the 15th-16th centuries.
  • Economic: search for new trade routes to Asia after Ottoman control of land routes raised prices
  • Religious: Catholic missionary zeal (Spain/France) to convert Native peoples; later Protestant motives for English colonization
  • Political/glory: national competition for empire and prestige among European monarchies
  • Technology: caravel ships, astrolabe, and compass made transoceanic voyages feasible
  • Portugal pioneered African coastal exploration; Spain funded Columbus's westward voyage (1492)

The Columbian Exchange

The transfer of plants, animals, people, diseases, and ideas between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres after 1492 remade both worlds.
  • To the Americas: horses, pigs, cattle, wheat, sugarcane, and devastating diseases (smallpox, measles, influenza)
  • To Europe/Old World: maize, potatoes, tomatoes, tobacco, cacao — potatoes and maize fueled European population growth
  • Demographic catastrophe: Native populations declined by an estimated 90% in some regions due to disease with no acquired immunity
  • Labor shortage from Native depopulation was a key cause of the trans-Atlantic African slave trade
  • Triggered a lasting shift in global diets, economies, and ecosystems (the 'Great Dying' and agricultural revolution)

Spanish colonization model

Spain built an extractive empire in the Americas based on conquest, labor exploitation, and religious conversion.
  • Conquistadors (Cortes over the Aztec, Pizarro over the Inca) used superior weaponry, disease, and Native rivalries to topple empires
  • Encomienda system: Crown granted colonists the right to Native labor in exchange for 'protection' and Christianization — functioned as forced labor
  • Casta system: rigid racial hierarchy (peninsulares, creoles, mestizos, Native peoples, enslaved Africans) structured colonial society
  • Missions (especially in Florida and the Southwest) aimed to convert and control Native populations
  • Spain extracted vast silver wealth (Potosi) that fueled European inflation (the 'Price Revolution')

Regional diversity of Native North America

Before contact, Indigenous peoples adapted to distinct environments, producing very different economies and social structures.
  • Southwest (Pueblo): irrigation-based maize agriculture, adobe towns, sedentary life.
  • Great Plains: mobile bison hunting on foot (before horses), small bands.
  • Northeast/Iroquois: mixed farming (the 'three sisters') plus hunting; matrilineal kinship and confederation.
  • Pacific Northwest: rich salmon fisheries supported large, hierarchical, permanent villages.
  • Mississippi River Valley (Cahokia): maize surplus fed a large, stratified, mound-building society.

Systems of labor and coerced conversion

Spain built its American empire on extracting Native labor and imposing Catholicism.
  • The encomienda granted colonists the labor and tribute of Native communities in exchange for 'protection' and Christianization.
  • The mission system concentrated Native peoples for conversion and labor, often by force.
  • Bartolome de las Casas condemned the abuses; debate over Native humanity shaped Spanish policy.
  • A rigid casta system ranked people by ancestry (peninsulares, creoles, mestizos, Native peoples, Africans).
  • Native resistance persisted, most dramatically in the Pueblo Revolt of 1680.

Unit 2: Period 2: 1607-17546 concepts · ~34 min

▾

Chesapeake colonies (Virginia, Maryland)

Founded for profit, the Chesapeake economy became dependent on tobacco and enslaved labor.
  • Jamestown (1607): first permanent English colony; near-failure from disease and starvation until tobacco cultivation (John Rolfe) made it profitable
  • Headright system: 50 acres granted per person whose passage was paid, encouraging indentured servitude and population growth
  • Shift from indentured servants to enslaved Africans accelerated after Bacon's Rebellion (1676) exposed risks of a free ex-servant class
  • House of Burgesses (1619): first representative assembly in English America
  • Tobacco monoculture led to large plantations, dispersed settlement, and a rigid social hierarchy with planter elites at the top

New England colonies

Settled largely by Puritans seeking religious purity, New England developed a distinct economy and tightly-knit town-based society.
  • Massachusetts Bay Colony (1630): Puritans sought to build a 'city upon a hill' (John Winthrop) — a model godly society, not full religious tolerance
  • Congregationalist church governance influenced town meeting democracy and relatively broad (male) political participation
  • Economy based on subsistence farming, fishing, shipbuilding, and trade (rocky soil unsuited to cash crops)
  • Roger Williams (banished) founded Rhode Island on principles of religious toleration and separation of church and state
  • Salem Witch Trials (1692) reflected social tensions, religious anxiety, and the dangers of mass hysteria

Middle and Southern colonies

The Middle colonies were marked by ethnic and religious diversity; the Southern colonies (beyond Chesapeake) built plantation slave societies.
  • Middle colonies (NY, PA, NJ, DE): diverse population (Dutch, Germans, Quakers, English), fertile land ('breadbasket'), religious tolerance especially in Pennsylvania (William Penn, Quakers)
  • Southern colonies (Carolinas, Georgia): rice and indigo cultivation in South Carolina relied heavily on enslaved labor and Africans' rice-growing expertise
  • Georgia founded (1733) as a buffer against Spanish Florida and briefly as a haven for debtors before adopting slavery
  • Growing reliance on slave labor across the South created stark regional economic and social divergence from the North

Atlantic slave trade & slavery's growth

The trans-Atlantic slave trade forcibly transported millions of Africans to the Americas, and slavery hardened into a racialized, hereditary institution.
  • Middle Passage: brutal ocean crossing with high mortality rates for enslaved Africans
  • Triangular trade: manufactured goods (Europe) → enslaved Africans (Africa) → raw materials/cash crops (Americas) → back to Europe
  • Colonial slave codes (Virginia, 1660s-1705) codified slavery as lifelong, hereditary, and race-based
  • Stono Rebellion (1739, South Carolina): largest slave uprising in the colonies, led to harsher slave codes
  • By the mid-1700s, enslaved Africans formed the majority of the population in parts of the Lower South

Mercantilism and colonial trade

England regulated colonial trade to benefit the mother country under the economic theory of mercantilism.
  • Mercantilism: colonies exist to enrich the mother country by supplying raw materials and buying finished goods
  • Navigation Acts (1651 onward): required colonial goods to be shipped on English ships and certain goods routed through England
  • 'Salutary neglect': before 1754, Britain loosely enforced trade regulations, allowing colonial self-governance and smuggling to flourish
  • Triangular trade routes linked New England rum, African slaves, and West Indian sugar/molasses

Enlightenment and the First Great Awakening

Two transatlantic intellectual currents reshaped colonial thought in the early 1700s.
  • The Enlightenment stressed reason, natural rights, and the social contract (Locke), influencing colonial elites.
  • The First Great Awakening (1730s-40s) was an emotional religious revival led by Jonathan Edwards and George Whitefield.
  • Itinerant preaching and 'New Light' congregations undercut established church authority.
  • The Awakening was one of the first shared experiences across colonial lines.
  • Both movements encouraged questioning of traditional authority, a seedbed for revolutionary ideas.

Unit 3: Period 3: 1754-18006 concepts · ~34 min

▾

French and Indian War (1754-1763)

Part of the global Seven Years' War, this conflict between Britain/colonists and France/Native allies reshaped North America and colonial-British relations.
  • Albany Congress (1754): Benjamin Franklin proposed the Albany Plan of Union for colonial cooperation — rejected by colonies but an early step toward unity
  • Treaty of Paris (1763): France ceded Canada and land east of the Mississippi to Britain; Spain gained Louisiana
  • Britain's war debt led to new taxation of colonies, ending the era of salutary neglect
  • Proclamation of 1763: forbade colonial settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains, angering land-hungry colonists
  • Pontiac's Rebellion: Native uprising against British expansion into the Ohio Valley after the war

Road to Revolution: taxation and resistance

A series of British revenue acts and colonial resistance escalated tensions from 1764 to 1776.
  • Stamp Act (1765): direct tax on printed materials; sparked 'no taxation without representation' and the Stamp Act Congress
  • Townshend Acts (1767) taxed imported goods; colonists responded with boycotts and non-importation agreements
  • Boston Massacre (1770): British soldiers killed five colonists; used as propaganda (Paul Revere's engraving) to inflame anti-British sentiment
  • Boston Tea Party (1773): Sons of Liberty dumped tea to protest the Tea Act's monopoly for the East India Company
  • Intolerable/Coercive Acts (1774): punished Massachusetts after the Tea Party, closing Boston Harbor and revoking self-government — prompted the First Continental Congress

Revolutionary ideology

Enlightenment political philosophy and colonial experience with self-government provided the intellectual foundation for independence.
  • John Locke's natural rights (life, liberty, property) and social contract theory heavily influenced the Declaration of Independence
  • Thomas Paine's Common Sense (1776) used plain language to make the case for independence and against monarchy
  • Declaration of Independence (1776, Jefferson): asserted natural rights, listed grievances against George III, declared separation
  • Republicanism: emphasis on civic virtue, the common good, and rejection of hereditary aristocracy
  • Salutary neglect had allowed colonial legislatures real power, so many colonists saw new British taxes as violating established rights

Articles of Confederation & its weaknesses

America's first national government (1781-1789) was deliberately weak, reflecting fear of centralized power after British rule.
  • No power to tax, no executive branch, no national judiciary, and each state had one vote regardless of size
  • Amendments required unanimous consent — made reform nearly impossible
  • Successes: Northwest Ordinance (1787) established an orderly process for territories to become states and banned slavery in the Northwest Territory
  • Shays' Rebellion (1786-87): armed uprising of indebted Massachusetts farmers exposed the Confederation's inability to maintain order or raise an army
  • Weaknesses (no power to regulate interstate commerce, no unified currency) directly led to calls for a new constitution

The Constitution and ratification debate

The Constitutional Convention of 1787 created a stronger federal government through compromise, sparking fierce debate over ratification.
  • Great (Connecticut) Compromise: bicameral legislature — House by population, Senate with equal representation (2 per state)
  • Three-Fifths Compromise: counted enslaved people as 3/5 of a person for representation and taxation purposes
  • Federalists (Hamilton, Madison, Jay — The Federalist Papers) supported ratification and a strong central government
  • Anti-Federalists opposed ratification, fearing tyranny and the lack of a bill of rights protecting individual liberties
  • Bill of Rights (1791): first ten amendments added to secure ratification and protect individual liberties from federal overreach

Washington's presidency and early partisan divide

The first administration set precedents and saw the emergence of America's first political parties over economic and foreign policy disputes.
  • Hamilton's financial plan: national bank, federal assumption of state debts, tariffs to protect industry — favored a strong central government
  • Jefferson and Madison opposed Hamilton's loose constitutional interpretation, favoring states' rights and an agrarian republic (strict construction)
  • Whiskey Rebellion (1794): Washington used federal troops to suppress a tax revolt, demonstrating federal authority
  • Federalists (Hamilton) vs. Democratic-Republicans (Jefferson) emerged as the first party system
  • Washington's Farewell Address warned against permanent foreign alliances and domestic political factions

Unit 4: Period 4: 1800-18486 concepts · ~34 min

▾

Jacksonian Democracy

Andrew Jackson's presidency (1829-1837) expanded suffrage for white men and reshaped the presidency, while deepening exclusion of Native Americans and enslaved people.
  • Expansion of white male suffrage (removal of property requirements) marked the rise of the 'common man' in politics
  • Spoils system: rewarding political supporters with government jobs, justified as democratizing government
  • Indian Removal Act (1830) and the Trail of Tears (1838): forced relocation of the Cherokee and other tribes despite Worcester v. Georgia ruling in the Cherokee's favor
  • Bank War: Jackson vetoed the re-charter of the Second Bank of the U.S., viewing it as an elite monopoly harming ordinary Americans
  • Nullification Crisis (1832-33): South Carolina claimed the right to nullify the federal tariff; Jackson threatened force, crisis resolved by compromise tariff

Market Revolution

A transportation and communication revolution linked regional economies into a national market, transforming labor and daily life.
  • Erie Canal (1825) connected the Great Lakes to the Hudson River, boosting trade and western settlement
  • Railroads and steamboats dramatically cut transportation time and costs, expanding markets
  • Lowell System: young women worked in textile mills under close supervision — early industrial wage labor
  • Rise of a distinct middle class and the 'cult of domesticity' idealizing women's role in the home as industrial work moved outside it
  • Regional economic specialization deepened: Northeast industry, Midwest grain, South cotton — increasing sectional interdependence and tension

Second Great Awakening & reform movements

A wave of religious revivalism emphasized personal salvation and human perfectibility, fueling a broad array of antebellum reform movements.
  • Revivalism (Charles Finney) stressed individual conversion and the ability to achieve salvation through good works
  • Temperance movement sought to reduce/ban alcohol consumption, tied to concerns about family and social order
  • Abolitionism: William Lloyd Garrison's The Liberator (1831) and Frederick Douglass demanded immediate emancipation
  • Seneca Falls Convention (1848): Elizabeth Cady Stanton's Declaration of Sentiments launched the organized women's rights movement
  • Education reform (Horace Mann) promoted common (public) schools; utopian communities (Oneida, Brook Farm) experimented with alternative social structures

Manifest Destiny and westward expansion

Americans increasingly believed it was their destiny to expand across the continent, driving territorial acquisition and conflict.
  • Manifest Destiny: belief that American expansion across North America was inevitable and divinely ordained (term coined 1845)
  • Louisiana Purchase (1803, Jefferson) doubled U.S. territory, despite Jefferson's strict-constructionist doubts about its constitutionality
  • Texas annexation (1845) and the Mexican-American War (1846-48) resulted in the Mexican Cession (California, New Mexico territory)
  • Wilmot Proviso (1846) proposed banning slavery in territory won from Mexico — failed to pass but intensified sectional conflict
  • Oregon Trail and Mormon migration to Utah reflected large-scale westward migration in this period

Antebellum politics: the Second Party System

By the 1830s a stable two-party rivalry organized national politics around economic questions.
  • Democrats (Jackson): limited federal government, hard money, opposition to the national bank, expansion.
  • Whigs (Clay, Webster): the 'American System' of a national bank, protective tariffs, and internal improvements.
  • The Nullification Crisis (1832-33): South Carolina claimed a right to void the 'Tariff of Abominations'; Jackson threatened force, and a compromise tariff defused it.
  • The Bank War: Jackson vetoed the Second Bank's recharter and moved deposits to 'pet banks'.
  • The Panic of 1837 followed, hurting Van Buren's presidency.

Slavery and sectional tension before 1848

Expansion repeatedly forced Congress to confront slavery's spread.
  • The Missouri Compromise (1820) admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as free, and banned slavery north of 36 degrees 30 minutes in the Louisiana Territory.
  • The 'gag rule' (1836-44) tabled antislavery petitions in the House, angering northerners like John Quincy Adams.
  • Abolitionism grew: William Lloyd Garrison's The Liberator, Frederick Douglass, the American Anti-Slavery Society.
  • Most white northerners were not abolitionists but increasingly opposed slavery's expansion.
  • Southern defenders shifted from calling slavery a 'necessary evil' to a 'positive good'.

Unit 5: Period 5: 1844-18776 concepts · ~34 min

▾

Sectionalism and the slavery debate

Territorial expansion after the Mexican-American War forced the question of slavery's expansion, and a series of failed compromises deepened North-South division.
  • Compromise of 1850: California admitted free, popular sovereignty in Utah/New Mexico territories, stronger Fugitive Slave Act, banned slave trade (not slavery) in D.C.
  • Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854): introduced popular sovereignty in territories north of the Missouri Compromise line, effectively repealing it and causing 'Bleeding Kansas' violence
  • Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857): Supreme Court ruled enslaved people were not citizens and Congress could not ban slavery in territories, inflaming the North
  • Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852, Harriet Beecher Stowe) galvanized Northern anti-slavery sentiment
  • Lincoln-Douglas debates (1858) and the election of Lincoln (1860) as a purely sectional (Republican, anti-expansion of slavery) candidate triggered Southern secession

Causes and course of the Civil War

Secession following Lincoln's election led to four years of war fought over slavery's expansion and, ultimately, the Union's survival.
  • Southern states seceded starting with South Carolina (Dec. 1860), citing states' rights to protect slavery; Confederacy formed (Jefferson Davis)
  • Fort Sumter (April 1861): first shots of the war, after which Lincoln called for troops and four more states seceded
  • Union advantages: larger population, industrial capacity, railroads; Confederate advantages: fighting defensively on home ground, strong military leadership early on
  • Emancipation Proclamation (Jan. 1863): freed enslaved people in Confederate-held territory, reframed the war as a fight to end slavery, and allowed Black enlistment in the Union Army
  • Turning points: Antietam (1862, enabled the Emancipation Proclamation), Gettysburg and Vicksburg (1863, turned the tide toward the Union)

Reconstruction era

After the war (1865-1877), the nation grappled with how to reintegrate the South and define citizenship and rights for formerly enslaved people.
  • 13th Amendment (1865) abolished slavery; 14th Amendment (1868) granted birthright citizenship and equal protection; 15th Amendment (1870) barred denying the vote based on race
  • Radical Republicans in Congress pushed for stronger protections for freedpeople than Lincoln's or Andrew Johnson's lenient plans, leading to Johnson's impeachment (1868, acquitted)
  • Freedmen's Bureau provided education, food, and legal aid to formerly enslaved people during Reconstruction
  • Black political participation surged (Black officeholders, participation in state constitutional conventions) during Radical Reconstruction
  • Compromise of 1877 ended Reconstruction: Republican Hayes won a disputed election in exchange for withdrawing federal troops from the South, ending federal protection of Black rights

Reconstruction's failures and legacy

White Southern resistance and the withdrawal of federal enforcement allowed a system of racial subordination to replace slavery.
  • Black Codes (immediately postwar) and later Jim Crow laws restricted freedpeople's rights and enforced segregation
  • Ku Klux Klan and other white supremacist violence terrorized Black communities and their political allies
  • Sharecropping trapped many freedpeople in cycles of debt, replacing slavery with a new form of economic dependency
  • Northern war-weariness and a economic depression (Panic of 1873) reduced political will to enforce Reconstruction
  • Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) later legalized 'separate but equal,' cementing the legal framework for segregation that outlived Reconstruction

The failed compromises of the 1850s

A decade of legislative and judicial attempts to settle the slavery question instead deepened the crisis.
  • Compromise of 1850: California free; stronger Fugitive Slave Act; popular sovereignty in the Mexican Cession.
  • The Fugitive Slave Act radicalized northern opinion and inspired Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852).
  • Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854) repealed the Missouri Compromise line, sparking 'Bleeding Kansas' and destroying the Whig Party.
  • The Republican Party formed (1854) to stop slavery's expansion.
  • Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857) held that Black people were not citizens and that Congress could not bar slavery from territories.

The Union and Confederate home fronts

Total war reshaped government, society, and the economy on both sides.
  • The Emancipation Proclamation (Jan. 1863) freed enslaved people in Confederate-held areas and allowed Black enlistment.
  • The Union passed the Homestead Act, Pacific Railway Act, Morrill Land-Grant Act, a national banking system, and an income tax.
  • Both sides used conscription; the 1863 New York City draft riots turned deadly.
  • Lincoln suspended habeas corpus in parts of the Union.
  • Women managed farms and businesses and served as nurses; the Confederacy suffered severe inflation and shortages.

Unit 6: Period 6: 1865-18986 concepts · ~34 min

▾

Industrialization and big business

Rapid industrial growth after the Civil War was driven by new technology, resources, and business consolidation, producing enormous wealth and inequality.
  • Bessemer process cut steel production costs, enabling railroads, skyscrapers, and industrial machinery on a massive scale
  • Andrew Carnegie (steel) used vertical integration (controlling all stages of production) to dominate his industry
  • John D. Rockefeller (oil) used horizontal integration and trusts (Standard Oil) to eliminate competitors and control an entire market
  • Social Darwinism (Herbert Spencer, 'survival of the fittest') was used to justify wealth inequality and oppose government regulation
  • Transcontinental Railroad (completed 1869) integrated national markets and relied heavily on Chinese and Irish immigrant labor

Gilded Age society: immigration and urbanization

Mark Twain's 'Gilded Age' term captured a period of glittering wealth masking widespread poverty, urban crowding, and political corruption.
  • 'New immigrants' (Southern/Eastern Europe — Italians, Poles, Jews) arrived in large numbers, differing in religion/culture from earlier 'old immigrants,' fueling nativism
  • Chinese Exclusion Act (1882): first major law restricting immigration based on nationality, driven by anti-Chinese sentiment in the West
  • Urbanization: rapid city growth led to overcrowded tenements, political machines (Tammany Hall) that traded services for votes amid corruption
  • Social Gospel movement and settlement houses (Jane Addams's Hull House) sought to address urban poverty through Christian social action
  • Political machines and bosses (like Boss Tweed) provided real services to immigrants but were rife with graft and corruption

Labor movements

Workers organized in response to dangerous conditions, low wages, and long hours in the new industrial economy, often facing violent suppression.
  • Knights of Labor: sought to include all workers (skilled/unskilled, women, Black workers) and broad reforms like the 8-hour day
  • American Federation of Labor (Samuel Gompers): focused narrowly on 'bread and butter' issues (wages, hours) for skilled workers through collective bargaining
  • Haymarket Affair (1886): a bombing at a Chicago labor rally led to a public backlash against labor unions, linking them to radicalism/anarchism
  • Homestead Strike (1892) and Pullman Strike (1894): major strikes crushed by private security and federal troops, showing government often sided with business
  • Labor's overall gains were limited in this period by court injunctions, an unsympathetic judiciary, and employer strategies like blacklisting

Populism and agrarian discontent

Farmers, squeezed by falling crop prices, high railroad rates, and debt, organized politically to demand economic reform.
  • Grange movement and Farmers' Alliances organized farmers cooperatively before evolving into the Populist (People's) Party
  • Populist Party platform (1892, Omaha Platform): called for free coinage of silver, a graduated income tax, direct election of senators, and government regulation of railroads
  • Free silver debate: Populists/silverites wanted silver-backed currency to inflate the money supply and ease farmers' debt burden; gold-standard advocates wanted stable currency
  • William Jennings Bryan's 'Cross of Gold' speech (1896) championed free silver and won him the Democratic (and Populist) nomination, but he lost to McKinley
  • Populism's decline after 1896 nonetheless left a legacy of reform ideas later adopted during the Progressive Era

The New South and Southern agriculture

After Reconstruction the South promoted industry but remained poor, rural, and racially stratified.
  • Boosters like Henry Grady promoted textile mills, tobacco, and railroads, but the region stayed largely agricultural.
  • Sharecropping and tenant farming trapped Black and poor white farmers in debt through the crop-lien system.
  • Jim Crow laws mandated segregation; Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) upheld 'separate but equal'.
  • Disenfranchisement via poll taxes, literacy tests, and grandfather clauses stripped Black men of the vote.
  • Ida B. Wells led anti-lynching journalism and activism.

Politics of the Gilded Age

National politics featured close elections, high turnout, machine patronage, and few policy differences.
  • Party loyalty ran high but the parties rarely differed on major issues; the tariff and 'the money question' (gold vs. silver) dominated.
  • Political machines like Tammany Hall traded services for votes and skimmed graft.
  • The Pendleton Civil Service Act (1883) created merit-based hiring after Garfield's assassination.
  • The Interstate Commerce Act (1887) and Sherman Antitrust Act (1890) were early, weak federal regulation.
  • Farmers and reformers denounced government as captured by railroads and trusts.

Unit 7: Period 7: 1890-19456 concepts · ~34 min

▾

Progressivism

Progressives responded to the problems of industrialization and urbanization with government activism at the local, state, and federal levels.
  • Muckrakers (Upton Sinclair's The Jungle, Ida Tarbell on Standard Oil) exposed corruption and unsafe conditions, spurring reform legislation like the Pure Food and Drug Act (1906)
  • Political reforms: initiative, referendum, recall, direct primary, and the 17th Amendment (direct election of senators) aimed to increase direct democracy
  • Theodore Roosevelt's Square Deal: trust-busting (regulating rather than eliminating big business), consumer protection, and conservation of natural resources
  • Woodrow Wilson's New Freedom: lowered tariffs, created the Federal Reserve System (1913) for banking stability, and the Federal Trade Commission to police unfair business practices
  • 19th Amendment (1920) granted women's suffrage, the culmination of decades of activism by suffragists like Susan B. Anthony and Alice Paul

American imperialism

The U.S. expanded overseas influence in the late 19th/early 20th century, debating its role as an emerging world power.
  • Spanish-American War (1898): U.S. gained the Philippines, Puerto Rico, and Guam; Cuba became a U.S. protectorate under the Platt Amendment
  • Anti-Imperialist League opposed annexing the Philippines, arguing it contradicted American democratic principles; imperialists argued for markets, strategic bases, and 'civilizing' missions
  • Theodore Roosevelt's 'Big Stick' diplomacy and the Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine asserted U.S. right to intervene in Latin America
  • Panama Canal (completed 1914): U.S. backed Panamanian independence from Colombia to secure canal rights, boosting naval and trade power
  • Open Door Policy (China, 1899-1900): sought equal trade access for all nations in China rather than territorial division

World War I

The U.S. entered WWI in 1917 after years of neutrality, and the war's aftermath reshaped domestic society and foreign policy debates.
  • Causes of U.S. entry: unrestricted German submarine warfare (sinking of the Lusitania, 1915) and the Zimmermann Telegram (1917, Germany proposed alliance with Mexico)
  • Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points proposed self-determination and a League of Nations for lasting peace
  • Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles and League of Nations membership, reflecting a return to isolationist sentiment
  • Wartime mobilization: Great Migration of African Americans to Northern industrial cities; expanded roles for women in the workforce
  • Wartime repression: Espionage Act (1917) and Sedition Act (1918) restricted anti-war speech, upheld in Schenck v. United States (1919, 'clear and present danger')

The 1920s

The postwar decade saw cultural conflict between traditionalism and modernity, economic boom, and rising nativism.
  • Economic boom fueled by mass production (assembly line), consumer credit, and new consumer goods (automobiles, radios)
  • Harlem Renaissance: flourishing of African American art, literature, and music (jazz), centered in New York
  • Cultural clashes: Scopes Trial (1925, evolution vs. fundamentalism), Prohibition (18th Amendment) and its widespread evasion, and a revived KKK targeting immigrants, Catholics, Jews, and Black Americans
  • Immigration restriction: National Origins Act (1924) set strict quotas favoring Northern/Western Europe, reflecting nativism
  • Republican pro-business policies (Harding, Coolidge, Hoover): low taxes, high tariffs (Fordney-McCumber), minimal regulation

The Great Depression and New Deal

The 1929 stock market crash triggered a decade-long economic collapse, met by Franklin Roosevelt's unprecedented federal response.
  • Causes of the Depression: stock market speculation/margin buying, bank failures, overproduction, uneven income distribution, and the crash of Oct. 1929
  • Hoover's limited response (voluntary cooperation, Reconstruction Finance Corporation) was seen as inadequate; contrasted with FDR's activist New Deal
  • New Deal relief/recovery/reform: Social Security Act (1935, retirement pensions), FDIC (bank deposit insurance), TVA (regional development), Wagner Act (protected union organizing/collective bargaining)
  • Critics from the left (Huey Long) argued the New Deal did too little; critics from the right and the Supreme Court (which struck down NIRA, AAA) argued it did too much and was unconstitutional overreach
  • The New Deal did not end the Depression (which persisted until WWII mobilization) but permanently expanded the federal government's role in the economy and social welfare

World War II

The U.S. entered WWII after Pearl Harbor, mobilizing fully for a two-front war and emerging as a global superpower.
  • Pearl Harbor attack (Dec. 7, 1941) by Japan brought the U.S. into the war against the Axis powers (Germany, Italy, Japan)
  • Wartime mobilization: full employment, rationing, war bond drives, and massive industrial conversion to war production ('Arsenal of Democracy')
  • Japanese American internment (Executive Order 9066): forced relocation of ~120,000 Japanese Americans, later upheld in Korematsu v. United States (1944) and widely condemned in retrospect
  • Women (Rosie the Riveter) and African Americans entered war-industry jobs in unprecedented numbers, laying groundwork for later civil rights and gender equity movements
  • Atomic bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki (Aug. 1945) ended the war with Japan and opened the nuclear/Cold War era

Origins and course of the Cold War

Ideological conflict between the U.S. (capitalism/democracy) and USSR (communism) defined global politics without direct military conflict between the superpowers.
  • Containment (George Kennan): core U.S. strategy to prevent the spread of communism without direct war with the USSR
  • Truman Doctrine (1947) pledged support to nations resisting communism; Marshall Plan (1948) provided economic aid to rebuild Western Europe
  • NATO (1949) formed a Western military alliance; Warsaw Pact (1955) was the Soviet counter-alliance — divided Europe into blocs
  • Korean War (1950-53): first major 'hot' proxy conflict of containment, ending in stalemate near the original 38th parallel border
  • Cuban Missile Crisis (1962): closest the U.S. and USSR came to direct nuclear war, resolved through negotiation (Soviet missile removal for a secret U.S. pledge on Turkey missiles)

Civil Rights Movement

African Americans and allies used legal challenges, nonviolent protest, and grassroots organizing to dismantle legal segregation and expand civil rights.
  • Brown v. Board of Education (1954): overturned Plessy's 'separate but equal,' ruling school segregation unconstitutional
  • Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955-56, sparked by Rosa Parks) launched Martin Luther King Jr.'s leadership and the nonviolent direct-action strategy
  • Civil Rights Act (1964): banned discrimination in public accommodations and employment based on race, sex, religion, or national origin
  • Voting Rights Act (1965): eliminated literacy tests and authorized federal oversight of voter registration in discriminatory jurisdictions
  • Shift toward Black Power (Malcolm X, Stokely Carmichael) in the late 1960s reflected frustration with the pace of change and emphasized Black self-determination

Great Society and 1960s liberalism

President Lyndon Johnson expanded the New Deal's legacy with an ambitious set of programs targeting poverty and inequality.
  • Medicare (health coverage for the elderly) and Medicaid (health coverage for the poor) created lasting federal health programs (1965)
  • War on Poverty included Head Start (early education), Job Corps, and food stamp expansion
  • Immigration and Nationality Act (1965) abolished the national-origins quota system, dramatically reshaping U.S. immigration patterns
  • Environmental and consumer protection laws (EPA created 1970) grew out of 1960s activism (Rachel Carson's Silent Spring)
  • Great Society programs expanded the federal government's role in social welfare far beyond the New Deal's scope

Vietnam War and its domestic impact

U.S. involvement in Vietnam, justified by containment logic, escalated into a costly, divisive war that fractured domestic consensus on Cold War policy.
  • Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964) gave President Johnson broad authority to escalate U.S. military involvement without a formal declaration of war
  • Tet Offensive (1968): though a military defeat for the Viet Cong/North Vietnam, it shocked American public opinion and undermined confidence in official war reports
  • Anti-war movement grew on college campuses and among the broader public, including incidents like the Kent State shootings (1970)
  • Vietnamization (Nixon): gradual withdrawal of U.S. troops while transferring combat duties to South Vietnamese forces
  • War Powers Act (1973) sought to limit the president's ability to commit troops without congressional approval, a direct legacy of Vietnam

Social movements and the 1970s

The era's activism expanded beyond civil rights to feminism, environmentalism, and other identity- and rights-based movements, amid economic difficulty and political scandal.
  • Second-wave feminism (Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique, NOW) pushed for workplace equality; Roe v. Wade (1973) established a constitutional right to abortion
  • Environmental movement gained momentum (first Earth Day, 1970) alongside new federal regulatory agencies
  • Watergate scandal (1972-74): break-in and cover-up led to Nixon's resignation (1974), deepening public distrust of government
  • Stagflation (simultaneous inflation and unemployment) in the 1970s challenged existing Keynesian economic policy and hurt Democratic political fortunes
  • Détente (Nixon): a policy of easing Cold War tensions with the USSR and opening relations with Communist China (1972 visit)

Postwar affluence and suburbanization

Sustained prosperity after 1945 transformed where and how Americans lived.
  • The GI Bill funded college and low-cost mortgages for millions of veterans.
  • Levittown-style mass-produced suburbs, the interstate highway system (1956), and cheap cars drove suburban growth.
  • Consumer culture, television, and a postwar baby boom reshaped family life.
  • Population and industry shifted to the Sun Belt (South and West).
  • Federal mortgage policy and restrictive covenants excluded most Black families from the suburbs, deepening residential segregation.

The Reagan Revolution

Ronald Reagan's presidency (1981-1989) marked a conservative shift in American politics, emphasizing tax cuts, deregulation, and a renewed Cold War posture.
  • Reaganomics ('supply-side'/trickle-down economics): large tax cuts intended to spur investment and growth, paired with deep domestic spending cuts
  • Deregulation reduced federal oversight of industries (banking, airlines), continuing a trend begun under Carter
  • Massive defense spending increases (including the Strategic Defense Initiative, 'Star Wars') pressured the Soviet economy
  • Federal budget deficits and national debt grew substantially despite the goal of smaller government
  • Conservative resurgence included the rise of the religious right (Moral Majority) as a political force

End of the Cold War

A combination of Soviet economic weakness, reform efforts, and Western pressure led to the collapse of Soviet communism by the early 1990s.
  • Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms — glasnost (openness) and perestroika (economic restructuring) — unintentionally accelerated the USSR's collapse
  • Fall of the Berlin Wall (1989) symbolized the end of Soviet control over Eastern Europe
  • Soviet Union formally dissolved in 1991, ending the Cold War and leaving the U.S. as the world's sole superpower
  • Persian Gulf War (1991): U.S.-led coalition expelled Iraq from Kuwait, showcasing post-Cold War U.S. military leadership
  • End of the Cold War prompted debates over America's new global role and the 'peace dividend' of reduced defense spending

Globalization and the new economy

The late 20th/early 21st century saw accelerating economic integration, technological change, and debate over free trade's costs and benefits.
  • NAFTA (1994) created a free trade zone among the U.S., Canada, and Mexico, sparking debate over job outsourcing versus lower consumer costs
  • Rise of the internet and information technology transformed communication, business, and the economy (dot-com boom of the late 1990s)
  • Deindustrialization: decline of traditional manufacturing jobs, particularly in the 'Rust Belt,' as production shifted overseas
  • Income inequality widened as wages for many workers stagnated while wealth concentrated among high earners
  • Financial deregulation and complex mortgage-backed securities contributed to the 2008 financial crisis and Great Recession

Post-9/11 America and contemporary issues

Terrorism, prolonged wars, economic crisis, and deep political polarization have defined the 21st century so far.
  • September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks led to the War on Terror, the USA PATRIOT Act (expanded surveillance/law enforcement powers), and creation of the Department of Homeland Security
  • U.S. invasions of Afghanistan (2001) and Iraq (2003) became the longest wars in U.S. history, with debated justifications and outcomes
  • 2008 financial crisis triggered the Great Recession; federal response included TARP bailouts and later the Affordable Care Act (2010) expanding health coverage
  • Political polarization intensified, marked by deep partisan divides over immigration, healthcare, race, and the role of government
  • Ongoing debates over climate change, technology's societal role, racial justice movements (e.g., following George Floyd's murder in 2020), and America's global leadership continue to shape contemporary politics

Culture wars and political polarization

From the 1980s on, cultural and religious conflicts increasingly divided the parties.
  • The Religious Right (Moral Majority) mobilized evangelical voters around abortion, school prayer, and family issues.
  • Debates over affirmative action, gun rights, LGBTQ rights, and immigration hardened partisan lines.
  • Talk radio, cable news, and later social media reinforced ideological divisions.
  • The 2000 election was decided by the Supreme Court in Bush v. Gore.
  • Congressional gridlock and party-line voting became the norm.

The 2008 financial crisis and its aftermath

The worst downturn since the Great Depression reshaped economic policy and politics.
  • A housing bubble built on subprime mortgages and complex securities collapsed in 2007-08.
  • Major financial firms failed or were rescued; Congress passed the TARP bailout.
  • Unemployment neared 10%; the Obama administration passed a large stimulus and the Dodd-Frank regulatory law.
  • The Affordable Care Act (2010) expanded health coverage and became a lasting partisan flashpoint.
  • The Tea Party and later movements on the left reflected deep distrust of elites and government.

Conquistador
Spanish soldier-explorer (e.g., Cortes, Pizarro) who conquered Native American empires using weaponry, disease, and Native rivalries.
Triangular trade origins
Early Atlantic trade networks linking Europe, Africa, and the Americas began forming in this period, setting the stage for the slave trade.
'God, Gold, and Glory'
Shorthand for the three main motives — religious conversion, wealth, and national prestige — driving European exploration.

Term for the catastrophic Native American population collapse (up to ~90% in places) from Old World diseases after 1492.

Unit 2: Period 2: 1607-1754

Headright system
Granted 50 acres of land per person whose passage to the colony was paid — encouraged indentured servitude and population growth in Virginia.
House of Burgesses
1619 — first representative legislative assembly in English America, established in Virginia.
Bacon's Rebellion (1676)
Uprising of poor Virginia farmers/ex-servants that accelerated the shift from indentured servitude to racial slavery as the main labor source.
Middle Passage
The brutal, high-mortality ocean voyage transporting enslaved Africans across the Atlantic as part of the triangular trade.
Mercantilism
Economic theory holding that colonies exist to enrich the mother country; enforced in the colonies via the Navigation Acts.
Salutary neglect
Britain's loose enforcement of trade laws before 1754, which allowed colonial self-government and smuggling to flourish.
Congregationalism
Puritan church governance model in New England where individual congregations were self-governing, influencing town meeting democracy.
Indentured servitude
System where a person worked for a fixed term (often 4-7 years) in exchange for passage to the colonies; common in the Chesapeake before slavery expanded.
Mercantilism / Navigation Acts
The idea that colonies exist to enrich the mother country; the Navigation Acts required key colonial trade to pass through England on English ships.
First Great Awakening
A 1730s-40s religious revival (Edwards, Whitefield) that spread emotional faith, challenged church authority, and created a shared colonial experience.
Halfway Covenant (1662)
A Puritan compromise granting partial church membership to the unconverted children of members, responding to declining piety.

Unit 3: Period 3: 1754-1800

Proclamation of 1763
British order forbidding colonial settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains, issued after the French and Indian War — angered colonists.
Common Sense
Thomas Paine's 1776 pamphlet that used plain language to argue for American independence and against monarchy.
Three-Fifths Compromise
Constitutional Convention deal counting enslaved people as 3/5 of a person for purposes of representation and taxation.
Federalists vs. Anti-Federalists
Federalists supported ratifying the Constitution and a strong central government; Anti-Federalists opposed it, fearing tyranny without a bill of rights.
Articles of Confederation
First U.S. national government (1781-89); deliberately weak — no power to tax, no executive, no national judiciary.
Shays' Rebellion
1786-87 armed uprising of indebted Massachusetts farmers that exposed the Confederation government's weakness and spurred the Constitutional Convention.
Stamp Act Congress (1765)
Meeting of colonial delegates to organize resistance to the Stamp Act, an early step toward intercolonial cooperation.
Coercive/Intolerable Acts (1774)
Punitive British laws closing Boston Harbor and revoking Massachusetts self-government after the Boston Tea Party; prompted the First Continental Congress.
'No taxation without representation'
The colonial principle that Parliament could regulate trade but not impose internal revenue taxes on colonists who elected no members to it.
Common Sense (1776)
Thomas Paine's widely read pamphlet arguing in plain language for immediate independence and republican government.
Articles of Confederation weaknesses
Congress could not tax or regulate commerce, there was no executive or national court, and amendments required unanimous consent.
Northwest Ordinance (1787)
A Confederation success: it set an orderly path for territories to become equal states and banned slavery in the Northwest Territory.
Federalist No. 10
Madison's argument that a large republic controls faction by multiplying interests so no single majority can dominate.
Alien and Sedition Acts (1798)
Federalist laws restricting immigrants and criminalizing criticism of the government; opposed by the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions.
Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions
Statements by Madison and Jefferson asserting that states could judge the constitutionality of federal laws (an early nullification argument).

Manifest Destiny
Mid-1800s belief that American expansion across the continent was inevitable and divinely sanctioned.
Market Revolution
Early-19th-century transportation/communication boom (canals, railroads) that linked regional economies into one national market.
Second Great Awakening
Religious revival movement emphasizing personal salvation and human perfectibility; fueled abolitionism, temperance, and other reforms.
Seneca Falls Convention (1848)
First women's rights convention in the U.S.; produced the Declaration of Sentiments modeled on the Declaration of Independence.
Indian Removal Act (1830)
Authorized the forced relocation of Native American tribes from the Southeast; led to the Trail of Tears.
Bank War
Andrew Jackson's political battle against and veto of the re-charter of the Second Bank of the United States, which he saw as favoring elites.
Utopian communities
Experimental antebellum societies (e.g., Oneida, Brook Farm) that tested alternative social, economic, or religious arrangements.
Nullification Crisis
1832-33 dispute in which South Carolina claimed the right to nullify a federal tariff, testing federal versus state authority.
Jacksonian Democracy
The expansion of white male suffrage and a political culture celebrating the 'common man,' alongside Indian removal and the spoils system.
Nullification Crisis (1832-33)
South Carolina's attempt to void a federal tariff; Jackson threatened force and a compromise tariff resolved it, foreshadowing secession arguments.
Missouri Compromise (1820)
Admitted Missouri (slave) and Maine (free) and banned slavery north of 36 degrees 30 minutes in the Louisiana Territory.
Cult of domesticity
The antebellum ideal that middle-class women belonged to a private sphere of home and moral life, separate from men's world of work and politics.

Unit 5: Period 5: 1844-1877

Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854)
Allowed popular sovereignty to decide slavery in those territories, effectively repealing the Missouri Compromise line and causing 'Bleeding Kansas.'
Emancipation Proclamation
1863 order freeing enslaved people in Confederate-held territory; reframed the Civil War as a fight against slavery.
Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)
Supreme Court ruling that enslaved people were not citizens and Congress could not ban slavery in the territories.
Reconstruction Amendments
13th (abolished slavery), 14th (citizenship & equal protection), 15th (voting rights regardless of race) Amendments.
Compromise of 1877
Resolved the disputed 1876 election by giving Hayes the presidency in exchange for withdrawing federal troops from the South, ending Reconstruction.
Fort Sumter
Site of the first shots of the Civil War (April 1861), after Confederate forces fired on the Union-held fort in South Carolina.
Radical Reconstruction
Period when Radical Republicans in Congress imposed stricter Reconstruction terms on the South, including military districts and expanded Black civil rights.
Black Codes
Restrictive laws passed by Southern states immediately after the Civil War to limit freedpeople's rights and control their labor.
Compromise of 1850
California free; a tougher Fugitive Slave Act; popular sovereignty in the Mexican Cession; slave trade ended in Washington, D.C.
Emancipation Proclamation (1863)
Freed enslaved people in Confederate-held areas, made the war a fight against slavery, and allowed Black enlistment.
Sharecropping / crop-lien system
A postwar tenancy arrangement that kept many Black and poor white farmers landless and trapped in debt.

Unit 6: Period 6: 1865-1898

Vertical integration
Business strategy of controlling every stage of production, used by Andrew Carnegie in the steel industry.
Horizontal integration
Business strategy of buying out or merging with competitors in the same industry, used by John D. Rockefeller in oil (Standard Oil).
Social Darwinism
Application of 'survival of the fittest' to society/economics, used to justify Gilded Age wealth inequality and oppose regulation.
Populist (People's) Party
1890s agrarian political party demanding free silver, income tax, and railroad regulation to help struggling farmers.
Knights of Labor vs. AFL
Knights welcomed all workers and broad reform; the American Federation of Labor (Gompers) focused narrowly on wages/hours for skilled workers.
Gilded Age
Mark Twain's term for the late 19th century — a period of glittering wealth masking widespread poverty, corruption, and inequality.
Settlement house
Community center (e.g., Jane Addams's Hull House) offering services to poor urban immigrants as part of the Social Gospel movement.
Free silver
Populist-backed monetary policy calling for unlimited coinage of silver to expand the money supply and ease farmers' debt burdens.
Vertical vs. horizontal integration
Vertical (Carnegie): control every stage of production. Horizontal (Rockefeller): dominate one stage by absorbing competitors.
Gospel of Wealth
Carnegie's argument that the rich had a duty to act as trustees of their fortunes for the public good.
Plessy v. Ferguson (1896)
Upheld 'separate but equal,' giving constitutional cover to Jim Crow segregation for over 50 years.
Dawes Severalty Act (1887)
Broke up tribal lands into individual allotments to force assimilation; tribes lost most of their remaining land.
Disenfranchisement tools
Poll taxes, literacy tests, and grandfather clauses used to strip Black men of the vote while evading the 15th Amendment's wording.

Unit 7: Period 7: 1890-1945

Muckrakers
Progressive-era investigative journalists (Sinclair, Tarbell) who exposed corruption and unsafe conditions, spurring reform legislation.
Federal Reserve System (1913)
Created under Wilson's New Freedom to provide a stable, elastic national currency and oversee banking.
Fourteen Points
Woodrow Wilson's WWI peace plan proposing self-determination and a League of Nations.
Harlem Renaissance
1920s flowering of African American literature, art, and music (jazz) centered in New York City.
New Deal
FDR's set of relief, recovery, and reform programs (Social Security, FDIC, TVA, Wagner Act) responding to the Great Depression.
Japanese American internment
WWII forced relocation of ~120,000 Japanese Americans under Executive Order 9066, later upheld in Korematsu v. United States.
Square Deal
Theodore Roosevelt's domestic program of trust-busting, consumer protection, and conservation of natural resources.
Open Door Policy
U.S. policy (1899-1900) seeking equal trade access to China for all nations rather than territorial division among imperial powers.
Progressive Era amendments
16th (income tax), 17th (direct election of senators), 18th (Prohibition), 19th (woman suffrage).
Roosevelt Corollary
Theodore Roosevelt's assertion of a U.S. right to intervene in Latin America as an 'international police power.'
Causes of U.S. entry into WWI
Unrestricted German submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram proposing a German-Mexican alliance.
Great Migration
The movement of African Americans from the rural South to northern cities for jobs and to escape Jim Crow, beginning around WWI.
New Deal's Three Rs
Relief (jobs and aid), Recovery (spending and price supports), Reform (Social Security, the SEC, the Wagner Act).
Schenck v. United States (1919)
Allowed limits on speech that posed a 'clear and present danger,' upholding punishment for obstructing the draft.

Unit 8: Period 8: 1945-1980

Containment
Core U.S. Cold War strategy of preventing the spread of communism without direct war with the USSR.
Brown v. Board of Education (1954)
Supreme Court ruling that overturned Plessy v. Ferguson and declared public school segregation unconstitutional.
Great Society
Lyndon Johnson's 1960s programs (Medicare, Medicaid, War on Poverty) expanding the federal social safety net.
Gulf of Tonkin Resolution
1964 congressional resolution giving Johnson broad authority to escalate U.S. military involvement in Vietnam.
Watergate
1972-74 scandal involving a break-in and cover-up that led to Richard Nixon's resignation.
Truman Doctrine
1947 policy pledging U.S. support to nations resisting communist takeover, a foundational piece of the containment strategy.
Marshall Plan
1948 U.S. program of economic aid to rebuild Western Europe after WWII, intended partly to prevent the spread of communism.
NATO
1949 Western military alliance formed to collectively defend against Soviet expansion, countered later by the Warsaw Pact.
Civil Rights Act of 1964 / Voting Rights Act of 1965
Banned discrimination in public accommodations and jobs (1964); ended literacy tests and added federal oversight of registration (1965).
Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964)
Gave the president broad authority to use force in Vietnam without a formal declaration of war.
Tet Offensive (1968)
A large North Vietnamese/Viet Cong assault; a military defeat for the North but a psychological turning point that eroded U.S. support for the war.
Redlining
Federal and private practices denying mortgages in Black neighborhoods, excluding Black families from the suburbs and building a wealth gap.

Unit 9: Period 9: 1980-present

Reaganomics
Supply-side economic policy under Reagan combining large tax cuts, deregulation, and increased defense spending.
Glasnost and perestroika
Gorbachev's policies of political openness and economic restructuring that unintentionally hastened the Soviet Union's collapse.
NAFTA (1994)
Free trade agreement among the U.S., Canada, and Mexico, debated for both lowering costs and outsourcing manufacturing jobs.
USA PATRIOT Act
Post-9/11 law expanding government surveillance and law enforcement powers to combat terrorism.
2008 Financial Crisis
Triggered by the collapse of the housing/mortgage market and financial deregulation; led to the Great Recession and TARP bailouts.
Strategic Defense Initiative
Reagan-era proposed missile defense system ('Star Wars') that increased pressure on the Soviet economy during the arms race.
Department of Homeland Security
Federal department created after the September 11 attacks to coordinate domestic security and counterterrorism efforts.
Great Recession
Severe global economic downturn following the 2008 financial crisis, prompting the TARP bailout and later stimulus measures.
End of the Cold War
1989-1991: the fall of the Berlin Wall, Eastern European revolutions, and the dissolution of the Soviet Union.
USA PATRIOT Act (2001)
Post-9/11 law expanding surveillance, records access, and detention powers, raising civil-liberties concerns.
Bush v. Gore (2000)
The Supreme Court halted the Florida recount, effectively deciding the presidential election for George W. Bush.
Affordable Care Act (2010)
Expanded health coverage through mandates, subsidies, and Medicaid expansion; passed on party lines and became a partisan flashpoint.

Unit 1: Period 1: 1491-1607

Native American societies before contact
Diverse Native societies developed distinct economies and social structures shaped by regional environments.
European motives for exploration
'God, Gold, and Glory' drove Spanish, Portuguese, French, and English exploration in the 15th-16th centuries.
The Columbian Exchange
The transfer of plants, animals, people, diseases, and ideas between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres after 1492 remade both worlds.
Spanish colonization model
Spain built an extractive empire in the Americas based on conquest, labor exploitation, and religious conversion.
Regional diversity of Native North America
Before contact, Indigenous peoples adapted to distinct environments, producing very different economies and social structures.
Systems of labor and coerced conversion
Spain built its American empire on extracting Native labor and imposing Catholicism.

Unit 2: Period 2: 1607-1754

Chesapeake colonies (Virginia, Maryland)
Founded for profit, the Chesapeake economy became dependent on tobacco and enslaved labor.
New England colonies
Settled largely by Puritans seeking religious purity, New England developed a distinct economy and tightly-knit town-based society.
Middle and Southern colonies
The Middle colonies were marked by ethnic and religious diversity; the Southern colonies (beyond Chesapeake) built plantation slave societies.
Atlantic slave trade & slavery's growth
The trans-Atlantic slave trade forcibly transported millions of Africans to the Americas, and slavery hardened into a racialized, hereditary institution.
Mercantilism and colonial trade
England regulated colonial trade to benefit the mother country under the economic theory of mercantilism.
Enlightenment and the First Great Awakening
Two transatlantic intellectual currents reshaped colonial thought in the early 1700s.

Unit 3: Period 3: 1754-1800

French and Indian War (1754-1763)
Part of the global Seven Years' War, this conflict between Britain/colonists and France/Native allies reshaped North America and colonial-British relations.
Road to Revolution: taxation and resistance
A series of British revenue acts and colonial resistance escalated tensions from 1764 to 1776.
Revolutionary ideology
Enlightenment political philosophy and colonial experience with self-government provided the intellectual foundation for independence.
Articles of Confederation & its weaknesses
America's first national government (1781-1789) was deliberately weak, reflecting fear of centralized power after British rule.
The Constitution and ratification debate
The Constitutional Convention of 1787 created a stronger federal government through compromise, sparking fierce debate over ratification.
Washington's presidency and early partisan divide
The first administration set precedents and saw the emergence of America's first political parties over economic and foreign policy disputes.

Unit 4: Period 4: 1800-1848

Jacksonian Democracy
Andrew Jackson's presidency (1829-1837) expanded suffrage for white men and reshaped the presidency, while deepening exclusion of Native Americans and enslaved people.
Market Revolution
A transportation and communication revolution linked regional economies into a national market, transforming labor and daily life.
Second Great Awakening & reform movements
A wave of religious revivalism emphasized personal salvation and human perfectibility, fueling a broad array of antebellum reform movements.
Manifest Destiny and westward expansion
Americans increasingly believed it was their destiny to expand across the continent, driving territorial acquisition and conflict.
Antebellum politics: the Second Party System
By the 1830s a stable two-party rivalry organized national politics around economic questions.
Slavery and sectional tension before 1848
Expansion repeatedly forced Congress to confront slavery's spread.

Unit 5: Period 5: 1844-1877

Sectionalism and the slavery debate
Territorial expansion after the Mexican-American War forced the question of slavery's expansion, and a series of failed compromises deepened North-South division.
Causes and course of the Civil War
Secession following Lincoln's election led to four years of war fought over slavery's expansion and, ultimately, the Union's survival.
Reconstruction era
After the war (1865-1877), the nation grappled with how to reintegrate the South and define citizenship and rights for formerly enslaved people.
Reconstruction's failures and legacy
White Southern resistance and the withdrawal of federal enforcement allowed a system of racial subordination to replace slavery.
The failed compromises of the 1850s
A decade of legislative and judicial attempts to settle the slavery question instead deepened the crisis.
The Union and Confederate home fronts
Total war reshaped government, society, and the economy on both sides.

Unit 6: Period 6: 1865-1898

Industrialization and big business
Rapid industrial growth after the Civil War was driven by new technology, resources, and business consolidation, producing enormous wealth and inequality.
Gilded Age society: immigration and urbanization
Mark Twain's 'Gilded Age' term captured a period of glittering wealth masking widespread poverty, urban crowding, and political corruption.
Labor movements
Workers organized in response to dangerous conditions, low wages, and long hours in the new industrial economy, often facing violent suppression.
Populism and agrarian discontent
Farmers, squeezed by falling crop prices, high railroad rates, and debt, organized politically to demand economic reform.
The New South and Southern agriculture
After Reconstruction the South promoted industry but remained poor, rural, and racially stratified.
Politics of the Gilded Age
National politics featured close elections, high turnout, machine patronage, and few policy differences.

Unit 7: Period 7: 1890-1945

Progressivism
Progressives responded to the problems of industrialization and urbanization with government activism at the local, state, and federal levels.
American imperialism
The U.S. expanded overseas influence in the late 19th/early 20th century, debating its role as an emerging world power.
World War I
The U.S. entered WWI in 1917 after years of neutrality, and the war's aftermath reshaped domestic society and foreign policy debates.
The 1920s
The postwar decade saw cultural conflict between traditionalism and modernity, economic boom, and rising nativism.
The Great Depression and New Deal
The 1929 stock market crash triggered a decade-long economic collapse, met by Franklin Roosevelt's unprecedented federal response.
World War II
The U.S. entered WWII after Pearl Harbor, mobilizing fully for a two-front war and emerging as a global superpower.

Unit 8: Period 8: 1945-1980

Origins and course of the Cold War
Ideological conflict between the U.S. (capitalism/democracy) and USSR (communism) defined global politics without direct military conflict between the superpowers.
Civil Rights Movement
African Americans and allies used legal challenges, nonviolent protest, and grassroots organizing to dismantle legal segregation and expand civil rights.
Great Society and 1960s liberalism
President Lyndon Johnson expanded the New Deal's legacy with an ambitious set of programs targeting poverty and inequality.
Vietnam War and its domestic impact
U.S. involvement in Vietnam, justified by containment logic, escalated into a costly, divisive war that fractured domestic consensus on Cold War policy.
Social movements and the 1970s
The era's activism expanded beyond civil rights to feminism, environmentalism, and other identity- and rights-based movements, amid economic difficulty and political scandal.
Postwar affluence and suburbanization
Sustained prosperity after 1945 transformed where and how Americans lived.

Unit 9: Period 9: 1980-present

The Reagan Revolution
Ronald Reagan's presidency (1981-1989) marked a conservative shift in American politics, emphasizing tax cuts, deregulation, and a renewed Cold War posture.
End of the Cold War
A combination of Soviet economic weakness, reform efforts, and Western pressure led to the collapse of Soviet communism by the early 1990s.
Globalization and the new economy
The late 20th/early 21st century saw accelerating economic integration, technological change, and debate over free trade's costs and benefits.
Post-9/11 America and contemporary issues
Terrorism, prolonged wars, economic crisis, and deep political polarization have defined the 21st century so far.
Culture wars and political polarization
From the 1980s on, cultural and religious conflicts increasingly divided the parties.
The 2008 financial crisis and its aftermath
The worst downturn since the Great Depression reshaped economic policy and politics.

The Articles of Confederation failed due to WEAKNESS (no taxing/military power), not because it was too strong.

Watch out
The Wilmot Proviso never passed Congress, but it foreshadowed the sectional slavery debate of the 1850s.
Watch out
Manifest Destiny is a mid-1840s term/ideology, not a formal government policy from the Founding era.

Watch out
The Emancipation Proclamation freed enslaved people only in Confederate-held territory — not in the loyal border states.

Watch out

The 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments were passed DURING Reconstruction, not immediately in 1865 all at once.

Watch out

Watch out

Watch out
'New immigrants' (Southern/Eastern Europe, after ~1880s) differ from earlier 'old immigrants' (Northern/Western Europe) — nativist reaction targeted the newer group.
Watch out
Populism was primarily an agrarian (farmer) movement, distinct from the urban, worker-focused labor movement, though both arose from industrial-era grievances.

The Tet Offensive was a military defeat for the communist forces but a psychological/political turning point against U.S. public support for the war.
Watch out
Détente eased tensions with the USSR/China but did NOT end the Cold War — it continued into the 1980s.
