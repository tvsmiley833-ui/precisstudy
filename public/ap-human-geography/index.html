Unit 1: Thinking Geographically4 concepts · ~16 min▾
Spatial Concepts
Geographers use a common toolkit of spatial ideas to describe where things are and why they are there.
  • Absolute location: exact position (coordinates, address); relative location: position described in relation to other places
  • Distance decay: interaction between two places decreases as the distance between them increases
  • Space-time compression: technology (transportation, communication) shrinks the effective distance between places
  • Distribution: the arrangement of a phenomenon across Earth's surface (density, concentration, pattern)
Types of Regions
A region is an area with one or more unifying characteristics; geographers classify regions by how that unity is defined.
  • Formal region: shared, official/measurable trait (e.g., a country, a climate zone)
  • Functional region: organized around a focal point/node (e.g., a cell-phone tower's coverage area)
  • Perceptual (vernacular) region: defined by people's shared feelings/perceptions rather than official data (e.g., 'the Midwest')
Maps, Scale, and GIS
How geographers represent and analyze spatial data shapes what patterns they can see.
  • Map scale: the ratio between distance on a map and distance on Earth; large scale = zoomed in/small area, small scale = zoomed out/large area
  • GIS (Geographic Information Systems): software layering multiple data sets onto a map for spatial analysis
  • Choropleth map: uses shading/color to show a measured value across regions; a common source of the modifiable areal unit problem
Human-Environment Interaction
Geography examines how people shape, and are shaped by, their physical environment.
  • Environmental determinism: the (largely discredited) view that the physical environment alone dictates human behavior/culture
  • Possibilism: the environment sets constraints, but humans choose among possible responses
  • Sustainability: meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs
Don't confuse absolute location (fixed coordinates) with relative location (context-dependent, can change).
A 'large-scale' map is zoomed IN (shows a small area in detail) — this is the opposite of everyday usage of 'large.'
Unit 2: Population and Migration Patterns and Processes4 concepts · ~16 min▾
Population Distribution and Density
Population is distributed unevenly across the globe, concentrated in a few dense clusters.
  • Arithmetic density: total population divided by total land area
  • Physiological density: population divided by arable (farmable) land — a better measure of pressure on farmland
  • Agricultural density: number of farmers divided by arable land
  • Major population clusters: East Asia, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Western Europe
The Demographic Transition Model (DTM)
The DTM describes how birth and death rates change as a country develops economically.
  • Stage 1 (Low Growth): high birth and death rates, little natural increase (no country remains here today)
  • Stage 2 (High Growth): death rates fall (sanitation, medicine) while birth rates stay high — rapid population growth
  • Stage 3 (Moderating Growth): birth rates start falling (urbanization, education, family planning) — growth slows
  • Stage 4 (Low Growth): birth and death rates both low, population stable
  • Stage 5 (Decline): birth rate falls below death rate, population shrinks (e.g., Japan, parts of Europe)
Population Pyramids
Age-sex population pyramids visualize a country's stage in the demographic transition.
  • A wide-based, triangular pyramid indicates high birth rates (Stage 2, rapid growth)
  • A rectangular/column shape indicates low, stable birth and death rates (Stage 4)
  • An inverted (top-heavy) shape indicates population decline (Stage 5)
Migration: Push and Pull Factors
Migration is driven by factors that push people away from a place and pull them toward another.
  • Push factors: conditions that drive people to leave (war, famine, persecution, lack of jobs)
  • Pull factors: conditions that attract people to a destination (jobs, safety, family, climate)
  • Ravenstein's Laws of Migration: most migrants move short distances, and most migration is step-wise/rural-to-urban
  • Intervening obstacles: barriers (cost, distance, borders, physical terrain) that hinder migration
A country can have a high population (large total) but low density (spread thin) — India and Canada are useful contrasts here.
Emigration is leaving a place; immigration is entering a place — from the perspective of the origin vs. the destination.
Unit 3: Cultural Patterns and Processes4 concepts · ~16 min▾
Culture, Cultural Traits, and Cultural Landscape
Culture is the shared beliefs, practices, and material objects of a group, expressed visibly on the landscape.
  • Cultural trait: a single element of culture (a food, a belief, a style of dress)
  • Cultural landscape: the visible imprint of human activity on the land (buildings, fields, signage)
  • Material culture: physical objects a group produces (tools, clothing, architecture)
  • Nonmaterial culture: intangible beliefs, values, and norms
Cultural Diffusion
Cultural traits spread from their point of origin (hearth) outward through several distinct processes.
  • Relocation diffusion: a trait spreads because people who carry it physically move
  • Expansion diffusion: a trait spreads outward from its hearth while also staying strong there
  • — Contagious diffusion: spreads rapidly through a population with little regard to status (like a rumor)
  • — Hierarchical diffusion: spreads from people/places of power or influence downward (e.g., a trend starting in major cities)
  • — Stimulus diffusion: the underlying idea spreads and is adapted, even if the specific practice does not transfer directly
Language
Language distribution and diffusion is one of the clearest ways geographers trace cultural history.
  • Language family: a group of languages descended from a common ancestral tongue (e.g., Indo-European)
  • Lingua franca: a common language used for communication between groups who don't share a native language
  • Dialects and isoglosses: regional variation within a language; an isogloss maps the boundary of a particular usage
  • Toponyms often preserve traces of earlier language groups even after a population has been displaced
Religion
Religions are classified by how they spread and how universal their appeal is intended to be.
  • Universalizing religion: seeks converts everywhere, appeals to all people (Christianity, Islam, Buddhism)
  • Ethnic religion: tied to a specific ethnic group or place, does not typically seek converts (Hinduism, Judaism)
  • Religions spread through both relocation diffusion (migration) and hierarchical/contagious diffusion (missionary work, conversion)
Don't confuse hierarchical diffusion (top-down, through influential nodes) with contagious diffusion (spreads to everyone nearby regardless of status).
A universalizing religion isn't automatically the largest — ethnic religions like Hinduism can still have enormous populations.
Unit 4: Political Patterns and Processes4 concepts · ~16 min▾
States, Nations, and Sovereignty
Political geography distinguishes the legal entity of a state from the cultural identity of a nation.
  • State: an independent political unit with defined territory, permanent population, and sovereignty
  • Nation: a group of people with a shared culture/identity, who may or may not have their own state
  • Nation-state: a state whose territory corresponds closely with a single nation's cultural boundaries
  • Sovereignty: the recognized right of a state to govern its own territory without outside interference
Boundaries
Political boundaries are classified by how and when they were drawn relative to the cultural landscape.
  • Antecedent boundary: drawn before the cultural landscape developed
  • Subsequent boundary: drawn to accommodate existing cultural differences after settlement
  • Superimposed boundary: forcibly drawn over an existing cultural landscape, often ignoring it (common in former colonies)
  • Relic boundary: no longer functions but leaves a visible cultural mark (e.g., a former Berlin Wall route)
  • Geometric boundary: a straight line following latitude/longitude, ignoring physical or cultural features
Devolution and Supranationalism
Political power can shift both away from and beyond the traditional state.
  • Devolution: central government grants increased autonomy to regions within the state (e.g., Scotland within the UK)
  • Centripetal forces: unify a state and strengthen its cohesion (shared language, national symbols, transportation networks)
  • Centrifugal forces: divide a state and weaken its cohesion (ethnic conflict, uneven development, separatist movements)
  • Supranationalism: states voluntarily give up some sovereignty to a larger organization (e.g., the European Union)
Electoral Geography and Gerrymandering
How voting districts are drawn can itself become a spatial political tool.
  • Gerrymandering: manipulating district boundaries for political advantage
  • 'Packing': concentrating opposition voters into one district to reduce their influence elsewhere
  • 'Cracking': splitting opposition voters across many districts so they can't form a majority anywhere
'Nation' and 'state' are NOT interchangeable on this exam — a nation is cultural identity, a state is the political/legal entity.
Devolution grants MORE autonomy to a region; it is a centrifugal force that can either relieve or encourage separatist pressure.
Unit 5: Agricultural and Rural Land-Use Patterns and Processes4 concepts · ~16 min▾
Agricultural Origins and Revolutions
Agriculture has undergone several major transformations that reshaped population and land use.
  • First Agricultural Revolution: domestication of plants and animals, shift from hunting/gathering to farming
  • Second Agricultural Revolution: mechanization and new techniques (crop rotation, seed drill) alongside the Industrial Revolution
  • Green Revolution: mid-20th-century spread of high-yield seeds, fertilizers, and irrigation to the developing world
  • Third Agricultural Revolution: biotechnology, GMOs, and precision farming
Von Thünen's Model
This model explains how distance from a market shapes what farmers choose to grow.
  • Land closer to the market is used for perishable or bulky/heavy goods (dairy, market gardening) due to high transport cost/urgency
  • Land farther from the market is used for goods that travel well or need more space (grain, then livestock ranching)
  • The model assumes an isolated state with a single market and uniform land — real-world transportation networks distort the rings
Subsistence vs. Commercial Agriculture
Farming systems differ in whether they aim to feed the farmer's household or to sell for profit.
  • Subsistence agriculture: grown primarily to feed the farmer's family, common in less developed regions
  • Commercial agriculture: grown for sale in a market, often large-scale and mechanized
  • Intensive agriculture: high input of labor/capital per unit of land (e.g., paddy rice farming)
  • Extensive agriculture: low input per unit of land, larger area (e.g., shifting cultivation, ranching)
Rural Settlement Patterns
The way rural populations arrange themselves on the landscape reflects history, land division, and farming type.
  • Dispersed settlement: farmsteads spread out, each surrounded by its own land (common in the U.S. Midwest)
  • Clustered settlement: farmhouses grouped closely, with farmland surrounding the cluster (common in parts of Europe)
  • Long-lot survey pattern: narrow lots stretching back from a river or road, giving each farm access to transportation/water
Von Thünen's rings order goods by transport cost and perishability relative to the market, not simply by how 'valuable' a crop is.
Intensive agriculture is about input per unit of AREA, not total farm size — a small, heavily worked plot can be intensive.
Unit 6: Cities and Urban Land-Use Patterns and Processes4 concepts · ~16 min▾
Urbanization and Site/Situation
Cities grow at locations chosen for their physical characteristics and their connections to other places.
  • Urbanization: the growing share of a population living in cities rather than rural areas
  • Site: the physical characteristics of a city's exact location (terrain, water access, resources)
  • Situation: a city's location relative to other places and transportation networks
  • Break-of-bulk point: a location where goods are transferred from one mode of transport to another, often spurring city growth (e.g., a port)
Models of Urban Structure
Several classic models describe how land use is organized within a city.
  • Concentric Zone Model (Burgess): the city grows outward in rings from a central business district (CBD)
  • Sector Model (Hoyt): land uses extend outward from the CBD in wedge-shaped sectors along transportation routes
  • Multiple Nuclei Model (Harris & Ullman): a city grows around several separate nodes/centers, not just one CBD
  • Galactic (Peripheral) City Model: a CBD is surrounded by suburban development and an outer beltway with edge cities
Urban Challenges
Rapid or uneven urban growth creates distinct spatial and social problems.
  • Urban sprawl: unplanned, low-density outward growth of a city into surrounding rural land
  • Redlining: historic discriminatory practice of denying services/loans to residents of certain neighborhoods, based on race
  • Gentrification: an influx of wealthier residents into a lower-income neighborhood, raising property values and often displacing original residents
  • Squatter settlements (informal settlements): unplanned housing areas, often lacking basic services, common on the edges of cities in less developed countries
Rank-Size Rule and Primate Cities
The relative sizes of a country's cities reveal something about its economic development and history.
  • Rank-size rule: a country's nth-largest city is approximately 1/n the population of its largest city — common in more developed countries
  • Primate city: a country's largest city that is disproportionately larger than all others, often dominating politics/culture/economy
  • Primate city distributions are common in less developed and formerly colonized countries
The Concentric Zone Model predicts rings; the Sector Model predicts wedges along transport lines — don't mix up which shape goes with which model.
A primate city being large doesn't automatically mean the whole country is highly urbanized — it can just mean urban population is unevenly concentrated.
Unit 7: Industrial and Economic Development Patterns and Processes4 concepts · ~16 min▾
Measuring Development
Geographers use multiple indicators, not just income, to compare how developed different countries are.
  • GDP/GNI per capita: economic output per person — a common but incomplete development measure
  • Human Development Index (HDI): combines life expectancy, education, and income into a single composite score
  • Gender Inequality Index (GII) and Gender Development Index (GDI): measure disparities between men and women in health, education, and economic participation
  • Core-periphery model (World Systems Theory): divides the world into wealthy 'core' countries, developing 'semi-periphery,' and less developed 'periphery' countries dependent on the core
Rostow's Stages of Growth
This model outlines a linear sequence countries pass through as they industrialize.
  • Traditional society: subsistence-based, little trade or industry
  • Preconditions for takeoff: early infrastructure and investment begin
  • Takeoff: rapid industrialization in a few leading sectors
  • Drive to maturity: industry diversifies and modernizes broadly
  • Age of high mass consumption: production shifts toward consumer goods and services
The Industrial Revolution and Its Diffusion
Industrialization began in one region and spread outward, reshaping global trade and migration.
  • The Industrial Revolution began in Great Britain in the late 1700s, tied to coal, steam power, and textile manufacturing
  • It diffused to continental Europe, North America, and eventually much of the world
  • Industrialization drove massive rural-to-urban migration as factory jobs pulled workers into cities
Globalization and Manufacturing Location
Modern industry is organized around global supply chains, and firms weigh several factors when choosing a location.
  • Break-of-bulk points and transportation costs still shape where factories locate, alongside labor cost and access to markets
  • Outsourcing/offshoring: shifting production to other countries, typically to reduce labor costs
  • Special Economic Zones (SEZs): areas with reduced regulation/tax incentives designed to attract foreign investment
  • Deindustrialization: the decline of manufacturing in a formerly industrial region (e.g., the U.S. 'Rust Belt'), often accompanied by job loss and population decline
Rostow's model is often criticized as too linear/Western-centric — know it as a model, not a guaranteed real-world path.
A high GDP per capita doesn't guarantee high HDI — a country can have high average income but weaker education/health outcomes, and vice versa.
