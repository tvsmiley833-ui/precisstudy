Unit 1: The Global Tapestry 1200-14506 concepts▾
Islamic Empires & the Dar al-Islam
By 1200, the Islamic world stretched from Iberia to Southeast Asia, unified by trade, faith, and Arabic scholarship even as political power fragmented after the Abbasid Caliphate's decline.
  • Abbasid Caliphate (750-1258) centered in Baghdad, a hub of science, math, and Greek text preservation until the 1258 Mongol sack
  • Delhi Sultanate (1206-1526) brought Islamic rule to South Asia, taxing (jizya) non-Muslims and blending Persian-Islamic and Hindu culture
  • Sufism (mystical Islam) and traveling scholars like Ibn Battuta spread Islamic practice into West Africa and Southeast Asia
  • Mansa Musa's Mali (r. 1312-1337) tied West Africa into the Dar al-Islam via his famous 1324 hajj through Cairo
  • Cordoba in Islamic Spain (al-Andalus) was a center of learning where Muslim, Jewish, and Christian scholars translated classical texts
China: Song, Yuan, and Ming Dynasties
China led the world in technology, bureaucracy, and trade, alternating between periods of dynastic strength and Mongol conquest.
  • Song Dynasty (960-1279) had the civil service exam based on Confucian classics, movable type printing, and gunpowder weapons
  • Champa rice from Vietnam, adopted under the Song, allowed double-cropping and fueled population growth
  • Yuan Dynasty (1271-1368) was founded by Kublai Khan after the Mongol conquest of China, using foreigners (like Marco Polo) in administration
  • Ming Dynasty (1368-1644) overthrew Mongol rule and restored Confucian bureaucracy and the civil service exam
  • Zheng He's treasure fleet voyages (1405-1433) under the Ming projected Chinese power across the Indian Ocean before China turned inward
Sub-Saharan African States
West and East African states built wealth through trans-Saharan gold-salt trade and Indian Ocean commerce, developing distinct political and religious systems.
  • Mali Empire controlled trans-Saharan trade routes, taxing gold and salt exchanged at Timbuktu and Gao
  • Great Zimbabwe (c. 1100-1450) was a stone-built trading state linked to Indian Ocean gold and ivory trade with Swahili coast cities
  • Swahili Coast city-states (Kilwa, Mombasa, Mogadishu) blended Bantu and Islamic culture, speaking Swahili and trading gold, ivory, and slaves for Indian Ocean goods
  • Axum/Ethiopia remained a Christian kingdom, isolated but resilient amid surrounding Islamic states
  • Griots (oral historians) preserved West African political and cultural history absent widespread written records
European Christendom & Byzantium
Medieval Europe was politically fragmented under feudalism while the Byzantine Empire preserved Roman-Christian tradition in the east until its final decline.
  • Feudalism organized medieval Europe: land-for-loyalty relationships between lords, vassals, and serfs
  • The Catholic Church was the unifying institution of Western Europe, controlling education and legitimizing monarchs
  • The Byzantine Empire (capital Constantinople) preserved Greco-Roman learning and Orthodox Christianity, acting as a buffer against Islamic expansion
  • The Great Schism (1054) formally split Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox Christianity
  • The Crusades (1095-1291) were religious wars for the Holy Land that increased European contact with Islamic and Byzantine goods and ideas
The Americas: Mexica (Aztec) and Inca
In the Western Hemisphere, isolated from Afro-Eurasian networks, the Mexica and Inca built large tribute-based empires through conquest and engineering.
  • The Mexica (Aztec) Empire, capital Tenochtitlan (founded 1325), ruled central Mexico through a tribute system extracted from conquered peoples
  • Aztec religion demanded human sacrifice to sustain the sun god Huitzilopochtli, justifying wars to capture victims
  • The Inca Empire ran along the Andes with the capital at Cuzco, using the mit'a labor tax system instead of currency
  • Inca administration used quipu (knotted cords) for record-keeping and an extensive road system for control without writing
  • Both empires lacked large draft animals, wheeled transport, and steel, unlike contemporary Afro-Eurasian states
Trans-Regional Connections & State-Building Methods
States across the globe 1200-1450 used comparable strategies—bureaucracy, religion, military, and tribute—to organize diverse populations and legitimize rule.
  • Bureaucracies (Chinese civil service, Islamic caliphate administration) recruited talent and enforced central control
  • Religion legitimized rulers everywhere: Mandate of Heaven in China, divine right of Islamic caliphs, Aztec sun worship
  • Military conquest and tribute/tax systems (Mongol tribute, Aztec tribute, mit'a) extracted resources from subject peoples
  • Trade networks (Silk Roads, Indian Ocean, trans-Saharan) linked empires and spread technology, religion, and disease before 1450
  • Comparison is key on the AP exam: know similarities/differences in how states justified and maintained power
The Crusades were primarily religious/military campaigns for Jerusalem, not simple 'trade expeditions' — though they did increase European exposure to Asian goods.
The Delhi Sultanate ruled over a Hindu-majority population; it did not convert all of South Asia to Islam, and Hindu-Muslim syncretism (e.g., Sikhism later) emerged from this contact.
Feudalism in Europe was decentralized power-sharing, not a strong centralized monarchy like Song China or the Mali Empire.
The Inca used the mit'a labor/tax system, not currency-based taxation — don't confuse it with European feudal dues.
Unit 2: Networks of Exchange6 concepts▾
The Silk Roads
Overland trade routes connecting East Asia to the Mediterranean carried goods, technology, religions, and disease across Eurasia, reaching a peak under the Mongol-enforced Pax Mongolica.
  • Connected China, Central Asia, the Middle East, and Europe, exchanging silk, spices, paper, and gunpowder technology
  • Buddhism spread from India into China and Central Asia via Silk Road merchants and monks
  • Caravanserais (roadside inns) supported long-distance overland merchants across arid Central Asian terrain
  • The Pax Mongolica (13th-14th century) secured Silk Road routes under unified Mongol control, boosting trade volume and safety
  • The Black Death (bubonic plague) traveled along the Silk Roads from Central Asia into Europe and the Middle East in the 1340s
Indian Ocean Trade Network
Monsoon winds enabled a vast maritime trading system linking East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China in the era's largest and most lucrative trade network.
  • Predictable monsoon wind patterns allowed merchants to sail reliably between regions in different seasons
  • Key port cities included Calicut and Malacca (Melaka), major transshipment hubs for spices and goods
  • Dhows (Arab/Indian ships) and Chinese junks were the dominant vessels, larger and more advanced than most contemporary European ships
  • Goods traded included spices (pepper, cloves), textiles (Indian cotton), porcelain, and gold
  • Swahili Coast cities and Gujarati merchants were key intermediaries connecting African, Middle Eastern, and Asian markets
Trans-Saharan Trade Network
Camel caravans crossed the Sahara Desert linking West African gold and salt producers to North African and Mediterranean markets, spreading Islam southward.
  • Gold from West Africa (Mali, Ghana, Songhai) was exchanged for salt mined in the Sahara at cities like Taghaza
  • The camel saddle and caravan organization made regular desert crossings possible starting centuries earlier and intensifying in this era
  • Timbuktu and Gao grew into wealthy trade and Islamic scholarly centers due to their position on these routes
  • Islam spread into West Africa largely through Muslim merchants engaged in trans-Saharan trade, not military conquest
  • The Mali Empire taxed trade passing through its territory, becoming enormously wealthy, as shown by Mansa Musa's hajj
The Mongol Empire and Its Effects
The Mongols under Chinggis (Genghis) Khan built history's largest contiguous land empire, which unified Eurasian trade routes despite immense destruction during conquest.
  • Chinggis Khan united Mongol tribes and began conquests that eventually stretched from China to Eastern Europe
  • The empire split into four khanates: the Yuan (China), Golden Horde (Russia), Ilkhanate (Persia/Middle East), and Chagatai Khanate (Central Asia)
  • Mongol rule enforced relative safety and stability along the Silk Roads (Pax Mongolica), facilitating merchant travel like Marco Polo's
  • Mongols were generally religiously tolerant, adopting elements of the cultures they conquered (e.g., Yuan rulers adopted some Chinese customs)
  • The Mongols also facilitated the spread of the bubonic plague along trade routes, contributing to demographic collapse in Eurasia
Technology & Innovation in Exchange
Trade networks spread transformative technologies—papermaking, printing, gunpowder, and navigation tools—that reshaped societies far from their point of origin.
  • Papermaking technology spread from China westward through the Islamic world and eventually to Europe
  • Gunpowder, invented in China, spread via trade and Mongol conquests, eventually revolutionizing warfare in the Islamic world and Europe
  • The magnetic compass, developed in China, improved maritime navigation, aiding Indian Ocean and later Atlantic voyages
  • Astrolabes, refined by Islamic scholars building on Greek models, helped sailors determine latitude at sea
  • Printing technology (woodblock, then movable type) spread from China, influencing later developments including the Gutenberg press in Europe
Disease, Demography & Cultural Diffusion
Expanded trade networks were double-edged: they spread religions and ideas that reshaped societies, but also carried the Black Death, which devastated populations across Afro-Eurasia.
  • The Black Death (1340s-1350s) killed an estimated one-third of Europe's population and caused similar devastation in the Middle East and China
  • Labor shortages after the plague weakened the manorial/feudal system in Europe, raising the bargaining power of surviving peasants
  • Buddhism, Islam, and Christianity all spread significantly via trade networks and merchant/missionary activity in this period
  • Cultural diffusion via trade also spread artistic styles, culinary crops, and scientific knowledge (e.g., Hindu-Arabic numerals) across regions
  • Trade networks fostered diaspora communities, such as Muslim merchant communities settling in Indian Ocean port cities
The Indian Ocean trade network relied on monsoon winds, not constant winds like the Atlantic — sailing seasons were dictated by wind direction reversal.
Islam spread into West Africa mainly through trans-Saharan trade contact and voluntary conversion by elites, not primarily through military jihad.
The Mongols did not destroy all trade — while conquest was brutal, the resulting empire's stability (Pax Mongolica) actually increased Silk Road trade volume.
The Black Death originated in Central Asia and spread via trade routes; it was not a European-only phenomenon — it also devastated the Middle East and China.
Unit 3: Land-Based Empires6 concepts▾
The Ottoman Empire
The Ottoman Turks built a powerful Islamic empire spanning three continents, famously capturing Constantinople in 1453 and ruling a diverse population through the millet system.
  • Mehmed II ('the Conqueror') captured Constantinople in 1453, ending the Byzantine Empire and renaming the city Istanbul
  • The Ottomans used gunpowder cannons effectively to breach Constantinople's walls, showcasing the era's 'gunpowder empire' military edge
  • The millet system allowed religious minorities (Christians, Jews) to govern their own communities under Ottoman rule, paying a jizya tax
  • The devshirme system conscripted Christian boys from the Balkans, converting and training them as elite Janissary soldiers or administrators
  • Suleiman the Magnificent (r. 1520-1566) presided over the empire's peak, expanding into the Balkans and reforming law
The Safavid Empire
The Safavid Empire made Twelver Shia Islam the official state religion of Persia, creating a lasting Sunni-Shia divide with its Ottoman rivals.
  • Founded by Ismail I in 1501, the Safavids forcibly converted Persia to Shia Islam, distinguishing it from Sunni neighbors
  • The Ottoman-Safavid rivalry was partly religious (Sunni vs. Shia) and partly a struggle for control of trade routes and territory
  • Shah Abbas I (r. 1588-1629) reached the empire's height, reforming the military and promoting trade at the capital Isfahan
  • Isfahan became a dazzling center of Persian art, architecture, and carpet-weaving under Safavid patronage
  • The Safavids used gunpowder weapons and a professional standing army to consolidate central power, another 'gunpowder empire'
The Mughal Empire
The Mughal Empire united most of South Asia under Muslim rule, blending Persian, Islamic, and Hindu traditions and reaching a cultural and administrative peak under Akbar.
  • Founded by Babur in 1526 after the Battle of Panipat, the Mughal Empire ruled most of the Indian subcontinent
  • Akbar the Great (r. 1556-1605) promoted religious tolerance, abolishing the jizya tax on Hindus and marrying into Hindu Rajput families
  • Akbar created a policy of Sulh-i-kul ('universal peace/tolerance') blending Hindu and Muslim administrative elites
  • Shah Jahan commissioned the Taj Mahal (completed 1653) as a mausoleum for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, showcasing Mughal architectural grandeur
  • Aurangzeb (r. 1658-1707) reversed tolerant policies, reinstating the jizya and enforcing stricter Islamic law, straining Hindu-Muslim relations
Ming and Qing China
China alternated between the ethnically Chinese Ming Dynasty and the Manchu-led Qing Dynasty, both maintaining centralized bureaucratic rule and periods of relative isolation from the West.
  • The Ming Dynasty (1368-1644) restored Confucian bureaucracy and briefly projected naval power via Zheng He before turning inward
  • The Ming built and restored the Great Wall of China as defense against northern nomadic incursions (notably the Mongols)
  • The Qing Dynasty (1644-1912) was founded by the Manchus, who conquered Ming China while maintaining the civil service examination system
  • Kangxi and Qianlong emperors expanded Qing territory significantly, doubling China's size and stabilizing long-term rule
  • Both dynasties limited European trade contact, restricting foreign merchants largely to controlled ports like Canton (Guangzhou) under the Qing
Russia Under the Tsars
Moscow's grand princes threw off Mongol ('Tatar') control and built a centralized, autocratic Russian state that expanded eastward across Siberia.
  • Ivan III ('the Great') ended Mongol/Tatar dominance over Moscow in 1480, asserting Russian independence
  • Ivan IV ('the Terrible') became the first to take the title Tsar (Caesar), centralizing power and expanding Russian territory
  • The Russian Orthodox Church legitimized tsarist rule, with Moscow proclaimed the 'Third Rome' after Constantinople's fall
  • Serfdom bound Russian peasants to the land, becoming a defining and long-lasting feature of the Russian social/economic system
  • Peter the Great (r. 1682-1725) later westernized Russia's military, bureaucracy, and culture, founding St. Petersburg as a 'window to the West'
Comparing Land-Based (Gunpowder) Empires
Historians group the Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, Ming/Qing, and Russian states as 'gunpowder empires' whose rulers used firearms and cannon to centralize power over vast land territories.
  • All these empires used gunpowder weaponry to defeat rivals and consolidate centralized, absolutist rule over diverse populations
  • Religious legitimacy (Sunni Islam, Shia Islam, Confucianism, Orthodox Christianity) underpinned each ruler's claim to authority
  • Bureaucracies (Ottoman devshirme-trained officials, Chinese scholar-officials, Mughal mansabdars) administered these vast territories
  • Religious diversity management varied: Ottomans and Mughals under Akbar tolerated minorities, while Safavids and later Aurangzeb enforced religious conformity
  • Trade and taxation of agricultural land (not maritime commerce alone) were the primary revenue sources for these land-based empires
The Ottoman millet system granted religious communities self-governance, not equal political power — it was tolerance for social order, not democracy.
The Safavid-Ottoman rivalry was about Shia vs. Sunni Islam and territorial control, not a simple 'Christian vs. Muslim' conflict.
Akbar's religious tolerance was a personal policy, not a permanent Mughal norm — Aurangzeb later reversed many of these tolerant practices.
Russia's tsarist system developed by rejecting Mongol overlordship, not by adopting Mongol governance structures — 'Third Rome' ideology ties Russia to Byzantine Orthodoxy, not the Mongols.
Unit 4: Transoceanic Connections 1450-17506 concepts▾
European Age of Exploration
Portugal and Spain pioneered transoceanic voyages driven by the search for direct routes to Asian spices, Christian missionary zeal, and new navigational technology.
  • Prince Henry the Navigator sponsored Portuguese voyages down the West African coast starting in the 1420s, seeking a sea route to Asia
  • Vasco da Gama reached India by sailing around Africa in 1498, opening a direct sea route to Indian Ocean spice markets
  • Christopher Columbus, sailing for Spain, reached the Caribbean in 1492 seeking a westward route to Asia, initiating sustained contact with the Americas
  • The caravel ship design, combining European and Islamic/Chinese navigational features, enabled longer ocean voyages against the wind
  • Motives for exploration are often summarized as 'God, Gold, and Glory' — religious conversion, economic profit, and national/personal prestige
The Columbian Exchange
Contact between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres after 1492 triggered a massive global transfer of crops, animals, people, and diseases with transformative and often devastating effects.
  • New World crops (maize, potatoes, tomatoes, cacao) transformed diets and supported population growth in Afro-Eurasia
  • Old World crops and animals (wheat, sugar, horses, cattle, pigs) were introduced to the Americas, reshaping agriculture and Indigenous life
  • Old World diseases (smallpox, measles, influenza) devastated Indigenous American populations, which lacked immunity, killing an estimated 80-90% in some regions
  • The horse, reintroduced to the Americas, transformed the mobility and warfare of Plains Indigenous peoples
  • Silver from Spanish American mines (Potosí) entered global trade circuits, notably fueling trade with Ming/Qing China via the Manila Galleon
Spanish and Portuguese Colonization
Spain and Portugal established the first major European colonial empires in the Americas, extracting resources through forced labor systems and imposing Catholicism on Indigenous populations.
  • Hernán Cortés conquered the Aztec Empire (1519-1521) with the aid of Indigenous allies, disease, and superior weaponry
  • Francisco Pizarro conquered the Inca Empire (1532-1533), exploiting a civil war and capturing the emperor Atahualpa
  • The encomienda system granted Spanish colonists the right to Indigenous labor and tribute in exchange for 'protection' and conversion
  • The mita system was adapted by Spain from Inca labor practices to force Indigenous workers into silver mines like Potosí under brutal conditions
  • Portuguese colonization focused more on coastal trading posts and Brazil, developing large sugar plantations reliant on enslaved labor
The Atlantic Slave Trade
As Indigenous populations collapsed from disease and overwork, European colonizers turned to the forced transportation of enslaved Africans to the Americas, creating a brutal, large-scale system of racialized slavery.
  • An estimated 12+ million enslaved Africans were forcibly transported to the Americas between the 16th and 19th centuries
  • The Middle Passage refers to the horrific transatlantic voyage; mortality rates were extremely high due to disease, abuse, and overcrowding
  • The triangular trade linked Europe (manufactured goods), West Africa (enslaved people), and the Americas (raw materials like sugar and tobacco)
  • African states and merchants (such as the Kingdom of Kongo, Dahomey, and Asante) participated in supplying captives, often obtained through warfare
  • Racial hierarchies and chattel slavery ideology developed to justify the permanent, hereditary enslavement of Africans specifically
Labor Systems in the Americas
Colonizing powers developed varied coerced labor systems—encomienda, mita, chattel slavery, and indentured servitude—to extract wealth from American colonies.
  • Chattel slavery treated enslaved Africans and their descendants as permanent, inheritable property, distinct from earlier forms of bondage
  • Cash crop plantation agriculture (sugar in Brazil/Caribbean, tobacco in Virginia) relied heavily on enslaved labor for profitability
  • Silver mining in Spanish America (Potosí, Zacatecas) used forced Indigenous (mita) and later some enslaved labor under brutal conditions
  • Indentured servitude bound mostly poorer Europeans to a fixed term of labor in exchange for passage to the colonies
  • These labor systems created deeply hierarchical, race-based colonial societies (e.g., the casta system in Spanish America)
State Consolidation & Absolutism in Europe
European monarchs used the wealth and administrative demands of overseas empire to strengthen centralized, often absolutist state power at home.
  • Absolutism concentrated political power in monarchs like Louis XIV of France, who claimed divine right and reduced noble political influence
  • Mercantilism, the dominant economic theory, held that colonies existed to enrich the mother country through favorable trade balances and raw material extraction
  • Joint-stock companies (Dutch and British East India Companies) pooled investor capital to fund risky, profitable overseas trading ventures
  • Growing maritime empires funded standing armies and navies, reinforcing monarchical power and enabling further imperial competition
  • Religious wars (e.g., following the Protestant Reformation) intertwined with state consolidation as rulers used religion to unify or control territory
The 'discovery' of the Americas by Columbus is more accurately framed as sustained contact between two hemispheres that had been separate — millions of Indigenous people already lived there.
Disease, not superior European weaponry alone, was the primary cause of Indigenous population collapse in the Americas.
The Atlantic slave trade differed from earlier African slavery systems in that it was racialized, hereditary, and permanent (chattel slavery), not simply captivity from warfare.
Mercantilism, not free trade, governed colonial economic policy in this era — colonies existed to benefit the mother country's balance of trade, not to develop independently.
Unit 5: Revolutions 1750-19006 concepts▾
Enlightenment Ideas & the American Revolution
Enlightenment philosophers challenged divine-right monarchy with reason, natural rights, and social-contract theory, and their ideas directly inspired the first successful colonial independence movement.
  • John Locke argued government exists to protect natural rights (life, liberty, property) and derives authority from the consent of the governed
  • Jean-Jacques Rousseau's 'general will' and Montesquieu's separation of powers shaped constitutional thinking across the Atlantic world
  • American colonists protested 'taxation without representation' after the Stamp Act (1765) and Tea Act (1773), invoking natural rights language
  • The Declaration of Independence (1776), drafted by Thomas Jefferson, used Lockean ideas to justify separation from Britain
  • The U.S. Constitution (1787) created a federal republic with checks and balances, though it preserved slavery and excluded women and Native Americans from full citizenship
  • The American Revolution became a model (and financial strain on France) that helped spark later revolutions
The French Revolution (1789-1799)
Financial crisis, Enlightenment ideas, and deep social inequality under the Old Regime combined to topple the French monarchy and produce a radical, then reactionary, revolution.
  • The Old Regime's three estates (clergy, nobility, commoners) left the Third Estate paying most taxes while lacking political power
  • Deficit spending (partly from aiding the American Revolution) and poor harvests pushed Louis XVI to call the Estates-General in 1789
  • The Third Estate formed the National Assembly and stormed the Bastille (July 14, 1789), issuing the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen
  • The Reign of Terror (1793-1794) under Robespierre and the Committee of Public Safety executed thousands, including Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette, to defend the revolution from internal and external enemies
  • Napoleon Bonaparte seized power in 1799, later crowning himself emperor (1804) and codifying legal equality for men in the Napoleonic Code while curbing political liberty
  • The Congress of Vienna (1814-1815) restored conservative monarchies after Napoleon's defeat but could not erase revolutionary nationalism and liberalism
The Haitian Revolution (1791-1804)
Enslaved and free people of color in France's wealthiest colony, Saint-Domingue, turned revolutionary ideals into the only successful large-scale slave revolt in history, founding the first Black republic.
  • Saint-Domingue's plantation economy relied on brutal sugar-plantation slavery, producing immense wealth for France on the backs of an enslaved majority
  • News of the French Declaration of the Rights of Man inspired free people of color and enslaved Africans to demand equality and freedom
  • Toussaint Louverture, a formerly enslaved general, unified rebel forces, defeated Spanish, British, and French armies, and abolished slavery on the island
  • Napoleon sent troops to restore slavery and French control; disease and fierce resistance under Jean-Jacques Dessalines defeated them
  • Haiti declared independence in 1804, becoming the first nation founded by a successful slave revolt and the second republic in the Americas
  • France and other powers diplomatically isolated Haiti and forced it to pay a crushing indemnity (1825) for 'lost property,' crippling its economy for over a century
Latin American Independence Movements
Creole elites, inspired by Enlightenment ideas and emboldened by Spain's and Portugal's weakness during the Napoleonic Wars, led independence movements across most of Latin America by the 1820s.
  • Creoles (American-born Europeans) resented peninsulares (Spanish-born officials) who monopolized top colonial offices under the casta system
  • Napoleon's 1808 invasion of Spain and Portugal removed the legitimate monarchs, creating a crisis of authority that colonial elites exploited
  • Simón Bolívar ('the Liberator') led independence campaigns across Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru, envisioning a unified Gran Colombia
  • José de San Martín liberated Argentina, Chile, and helped free Peru, coordinating a pincer strategy with Bolívar's forces
  • Miguel Hidalgo's 1810 Grito de Dolores began Mexico's independence struggle, which succeeded in 1821 under a more conservative creole-led coalition
  • Independence rarely improved conditions for Indigenous peoples, enslaved people, or the poor — creole elites simply replaced peninsulares atop existing racial and economic hierarchies
Origins of the Industrial Revolution
Beginning in Britain around 1750-1800, a cluster of agricultural, technological, and economic changes shifted production from hand tools and human/animal power to machines and fossil fuels, reshaping the global economy.
  • Britain's advantages included abundant coal and iron, a stable banking/credit system, a large colonial market, and a labor supply freed by agricultural enclosure
  • The Agricultural Revolution (crop rotation, selective breeding, enclosure of common lands) raised food output and pushed rural workers into cities as wage laborers
  • James Watt's improved steam engine (1769) powered factories, mines, and eventually railroads and steamships, decoupling production from rivers and human muscle
  • The textile industry mechanized first: the spinning jenny, water frame, and power loom moved cloth production from cottage industry into centralized factories
  • Urbanization accelerated as factory towns like Manchester grew rapidly, often with overcrowding, pollution, and dangerous working conditions
  • Britain's head start let it dominate global manufacturing and trade through the 19th century, exporting factory-made goods and importing raw materials from colonies
Reactions & Consequences of the Age of Revolutions
The political revolutions of 1750-1848 spread nationalism and liberalism across Europe and the Americas, while early industrialization generated new ideologies responding to its social costs.
  • Nationalism — the belief that people sharing language, culture, and history deserve their own sovereign state — spread from revolutionary France across Europe
  • Conservatives (favoring monarchy and tradition) and liberals (favoring constitutional government and individual rights) clashed throughout the early 19th century, notably in the failed Revolutions of 1848
  • Industrialization produced a new industrial working class (proletariat) facing long hours, child labor, and unsafe factories, sparking labor organizing
  • Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels's Communist Manifesto (1848) argued industrial capitalism's class conflict between bourgeoisie and proletariat would end in revolution
  • Women's rights advocates, building on revolutionary rhetoric of equality, began organizing (e.g., Olympe de Gouges's Declaration of the Rights of Woman, 1791; Seneca Falls Convention, 1848)
  • These revolutions collectively established Enlightenment-based political ideals — popular sovereignty, natural rights, nationalism — as enduring global forces even where they initially failed
Don't equate the American Revolution with a full social revolution — it changed political rule but left slavery and existing social hierarchies largely intact.
The French Revolution was not a single event: the moderate constitutional phase (1789-1792), the radical Terror (1793-1794), and Napoleon's authoritarian empire were distinct, shifting phases.
Latin American 'independence' from Spain and Portugal did not mean liberation for enslaved people or Indigenous peoples — creole elites largely replaced peninsulares at the top of the same system.
The Industrial Revolution did not happen everywhere at once — it began in Britain and spread unevenly to Belgium, the U.S., and Germany over decades, and most of the world remained agricultural into the 20th century.
Unit 6: Imperialism & Migration6 concepts▾
Causes of New Imperialism
Industrialized nations built overseas empires from roughly 1850-1914 for a mix of economic, political, and ideological reasons, using technology that gave them decisive military and administrative advantages.
  • Economic motives included demand for raw materials (rubber, cotton, palm oil, minerals) and new markets for factory-made goods
  • Nationalist competition among European powers turned colonies into prestige symbols, fueling rivalries like the 'Scramble for Africa'
  • Social Darwinism and the 'civilizing mission' (mission civilisatrice) provided pseudo-scientific and religious justifications for domination, epitomized by Rudyard Kipling's 'White Man's Burden'
  • Technological advantages — the Maxim gun, steamships, quinine (to prevent malaria), and the telegraph — allowed small European forces to conquer and administer vast territories
  • Strategic motives included controlling naval coaling stations and canal routes (e.g., the Suez Canal, opened 1869) to protect trade and military reach
The Scramble for Africa
European powers partitioned nearly the entire African continent in a few decades, formalizing their claims at the Berlin Conference without African representation.
  • The Berlin Conference (1884-1885) set rules for European powers to divide Africa, drawing borders with no regard for existing ethnic, linguistic, or political boundaries
  • King Leopold II of Belgium ran the Congo Free State as a personal possession, forcing rubber production through mutilation and mass violence that killed millions
  • Britain pursued a 'Cape to Cairo' vision under figures like Cecil Rhodes, while France expanded across West and North Africa
  • The Anglo-Boer (South African) War (1899-1902) saw Britain defeat Dutch-descended Boer settlers, using concentration camps that killed thousands of civilians
  • By 1914, only Ethiopia (which defeated Italy at the Battle of Adwa, 1896) and Liberia remained independent of European colonial rule
Imperialism in Asia & the Pacific
European powers, along with the United States and a rapidly modernizing Japan, extended formal and informal control over much of Asia through trade concessions, treaty ports, and outright colonization.
  • Britain ruled India directly after the 1857 Sepoy Rebellion (Indian Rebellion) ended East India Company rule, replacing it with the British Raj
  • China avoided full colonization but was carved into 'spheres of influence' after the Opium Wars (1839-1842, 1856-1860) forced unequal treaties (e.g., Treaty of Nanjing) opening treaty ports and ceding Hong Kong
  • The Meiji Restoration (1868) modernized Japan's military, industry, and government, allowing Japan to avoid colonization and instead become an imperial power itself (annexing Korea in 1910)
  • The United States acquired the Philippines, Guam, and Puerto Rico after the Spanish-American War (1898), and fought the Philippine-American War against Filipino independence fighters
  • The Open Door Policy (1899), pushed by the U.S., sought equal trading access to China for all foreign powers rather than exclusive colonial control
Colonial Rule: Direct and Indirect Systems
Imperial powers used varying administrative strategies to control colonized peoples, extract resources, and maintain order with minimal European personnel.
  • Direct rule (used by France) governed through European officials and aimed at assimilation, imposing French language, law, and culture
  • Indirect rule (used by Britain in much of Africa and India) governed through existing local leaders and institutions, which was cheaper but often entrenched or invented ethnic divisions
  • Cash-crop and extraction economies (cotton in Egypt and India, rubber in the Congo, sugar in the Caribbean) reoriented colonial economies to serve the metropole rather than local needs
  • Settler colonies (Kenya, Algeria, South Africa) saw large numbers of Europeans take land, displacing and often violently subjugating Indigenous populations
  • Missionaries spread Christianity and Western education alongside colonization, sometimes providing literacy and healthcare while also undermining Indigenous religions and cultures
Forced & Coerced Labor Migration
Imperial economies depended on massive movements of coerced and semi-coerced labor after the abolition of the Atlantic slave trade, reshaping the demographics of the Americas, Africa, and Asia.
  • Indentured servitude replaced much of the enslaved labor force after Britain abolished slavery (1833); Indian and Chinese laborers were sent under contract to Caribbean sugar plantations, South Africa, and Southeast Asian rubber/tin operations
  • Conditions for indentured workers were often brutal and closely resembled slavery, with long contracts, debt bondage, and high mortality
  • Forced labor systems like Congo's rubber quotas and French corvée labor compelled colonized Africans to work on infrastructure and cash-crop production under threat of violence
  • Chinese migration (the 'coolie trade') sent laborers to build railroads in the Americas (e.g., the U.S. transcontinental railroad) and work in mines under exploitative contracts
  • These migrations created lasting diaspora communities (South Asians in East Africa and the Caribbean, Chinese in Southeast Asia and the Americas) that reshaped colonial and postcolonial societies
Resistance to Imperialism
Colonized peoples resisted imperial rule through armed rebellion, cultural/religious revitalization movements, and early nationalist political organizing, with mixed success before 1900.
  • The Sepoy Rebellion (1857) in India began over religious grievances (greased cartridges) but grew into a broader revolt against East India Company rule, leading Britain to impose direct crown rule
  • The Boxer Rebellion (1899-1901) in China, led by anti-foreign 'Boxers,' targeted foreign missionaries and Christians but was crushed by an international coalition, deepening foreign control
  • Ethiopia's victory over Italy at the Battle of Adwa (1896) under Emperor Menelik II preserved its independence and stood as a powerful symbol of successful African resistance
  • The Zulu Kingdom under Cetshwayo won a notable early victory at Isandlwana (1879) against Britain before ultimately being defeated and absorbed into the British Empire
  • Early nationalist and reform movements — the Indian National Congress (founded 1885) and Chinese reformers like the Self-Strengthening Movement — began building political frameworks for later 20th-century independence struggles
Don't call New Imperialism (c. 1850-1914) the same as earlier mercantile colonialism (1450-1750) — New Imperialism was driven by industrial capitalism, nationalism, and pseudo-scientific racism, and involved formal territorial annexation on a far larger scale.
Indirect rule was not 'gentler' than direct rule — it often deepened ethnic divisions (e.g., colonial favoritism in Rwanda) that had lasting, violent consequences after independence.
Indentured servitude was not the same as free wage labor — many indentured workers faced coercion, debt bondage, and conditions resembling slavery.
Resistance to imperialism was common, not rare — movements like the Sepoy Rebellion, Boxer Rebellion, and Adwa show colonized peoples consistently resisted, even though most rebellions before 1900 were ultimately suppressed.
Unit 7: Global Conflict 1900-present6 concepts▾
Causes of World War I
A tangle of long-term structural tensions and a single assassination combined to plunge Europe's great powers into a war of unprecedented scale by August 1914.
  • MAIN causes: Militarism (arms races, especially Anglo-German naval rivalry), Alliances (Triple Alliance vs. Triple Entente), Imperialism (colonial rivalries), and Nationalism (especially in the Balkans)
  • The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary by Serbian nationalist Gavrilo Princip (June 1914) triggered a diplomatic crisis
  • Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia activated a chain of alliance obligations, pulling Russia, Germany, France, and Britain into war within weeks
  • Trench warfare on the Western Front produced horrific stalemate and casualties, with new technologies (machine guns, poison gas, tanks, airplanes) making offense far deadlier than in prior wars
  • The war became 'total war,' mobilizing entire economies and civilian populations, including women who entered factories and support roles in unprecedented numbers
The End of WWI and Its Aftermath
The war's end reshaped the map of Europe and the Middle East, while its punitive peace settlement and economic destruction sowed seeds for future conflict.
  • The Treaty of Versailles (1919) forced Germany to accept sole guilt for the war (War Guilt Clause), pay massive reparations, and lose territory and its overseas colonies
  • The collapse of the Ottoman, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, and German empires created new nations in Eastern Europe and the Middle East, often drawn without regard to ethnic realities (e.g., the Sykes-Picot Agreement dividing the Middle East)
  • The Russian Revolution (1917) toppled the Tsar and later brought the Bolsheviks under Lenin to power, taking Russia out of the war via the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk and founding the Soviet Union
  • Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points called for self-determination and a League of Nations, but the League lacked enforcement power and the U.S. never joined
  • War debts, reparations, and economic dislocation contributed to hyperinflation in Weimar Germany and set the stage for the Great Depression's global impact
Interwar Totalitarianism
Economic crisis, humiliation from WWI, and fear of communism enabled totalitarian regimes to seize and consolidate absolute control over politics, economy, and society in Italy, Germany, and the Soviet Union.
  • Benito Mussolini's Fascist Party took power in Italy (1922), emphasizing extreme nationalism, a single-party state, and suppression of political opposition
  • Adolf Hitler's Nazi Party exploited the Great Depression, resentment over Versailles, and hyperinflation to win power legally in 1933, then dismantled German democracy
  • Totalitarian regimes used propaganda, secret police, and mass mobilization (youth organizations, rallies) to control both public and private life
  • Joseph Stalin's Soviet Union pursued forced collectivization of agriculture and rapid industrialization through Five-Year Plans, causing mass famine (notably the Ukrainian Holodomor, c. 1932-1933) and millions of deaths
  • Stalin's Great Purge (1936-1938) eliminated real and perceived political rivals through show trials, executions, and the Gulag labor camp system
Causes and Course of World War II
Failed collective security, appeasement, and aggressive expansion by Axis powers in Europe and Asia led to the deadliest conflict in human history.
  • Germany's remilitarization of the Rhineland, annexation of Austria (Anschluss), and the Munich Agreement's appeasement over the Sudetenland emboldened Hitler's expansion
  • The Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact (1939) between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union secretly divided Eastern Europe, and Germany's invasion of Poland (September 1939) triggered British and French declarations of war
  • In Asia, Japan's invasion of Manchuria (1931) and full-scale invasion of China (1937), including the Nanjing Massacre, expanded into a broader Pacific War after the attack on Pearl Harbor (December 1941)
  • Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union (Operation Barbarossa, 1941) opened a massive Eastern Front, ultimately draining German resources decisively at battles like Stalingrad (1942-1943)
  • The war ended in Europe with Germany's surrender (May 1945) and in the Pacific after the U.S. dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki (August 1945)
The Holocaust and Wartime Atrocities
Nazi Germany's racial ideology culminated in the systematic genocide of six million Jews and millions of other victims, while atrocities occurred on multiple fronts of the war.
  • Nazi racial ideology targeted Jews as the primary victims, alongside Roma, disabled people, homosexuals, Slavic peoples, political dissidents, and Jehovah's Witnesses
  • Nuremberg Laws (1935) stripped German Jews of citizenship; Kristallnacht (1938) marked an escalation into open state-sponsored violence
  • The 'Final Solution,' formalized at the Wannsee Conference (1942), industrialized mass murder through death camps like Auschwitz-Birkenau, killing roughly six million Jews
  • Japan committed wartime atrocities including the Nanjing Massacre (1937-1938) and forced sexual slavery of 'comfort women' across occupied Asia
  • Postwar Nuremberg Trials and Tokyo Trials established precedents for prosecuting individuals for war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide
Global Consequences of the World Wars
The two world wars devastated Europe and Asia, accelerated the decline of European colonial empires, and created new international institutions meant to prevent future catastrophic conflict.
  • The wars bankrupted European colonial powers, weakening their ability and legitimacy to maintain empires, which fueled decolonization after 1945
  • The United Nations (1945) replaced the failed League of Nations, aiming for collective security, with a Security Council giving veto power to major wartime allies
  • The United States and Soviet Union emerged as the two dominant superpowers, setting the stage for Cold War rivalry
  • Colonial troops from Africa, India, and other territories fought for imperial powers in both wars, and many returned home demanding independence in exchange for their service and sacrifice
  • The Holocaust's revelation fueled postwar Jewish demands for a homeland, contributing to the creation of the state of Israel (1948) and ongoing regional conflict
Don't treat WWI's assassination of Franz Ferdinand as the sole cause — it was a trigger, not the underlying cause; militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism had built tension for decades.
Fascism (Italy, Germany) and communism (Soviet Union) are not the same ideology — both were totalitarian and used similar tools of control, but fascism emphasized ultranationalism and private property, while communism aimed to abolish class and private property.
Appeasement at Munich (1938) was intended to prevent war by conceding to Hitler's demands, but it is now widely seen as having emboldened further aggression rather than stopping it.
The Holocaust targeted multiple groups, not only Jews — Roma, disabled people, Slavic civilians, political prisoners, and others were also systematically persecuted and murdered, though Jews were the primary and most numerous target of the genocide.
Unit 8: Cold War & Decolonization6 concepts▾
Origins of the Cold War
The wartime alliance between the U.S. and USSR collapsed after 1945 into a decades-long ideological, political, and military rivalry that divided much of the world into competing blocs.
  • Ideological conflict pitted American capitalism and democracy against Soviet communism and one-party rule, with each side viewing the other's system as an existential threat
  • The 'Iron Curtain' (Churchill's term) divided Europe between Soviet-dominated Eastern Bloc states and Western democracies allied with the U.S.
  • The Truman Doctrine (1947) pledged U.S. support for nations resisting communist takeover, formalizing a policy of 'containment'
  • The Marshall Plan (1948) provided massive U.S. economic aid to rebuild Western Europe, partly to prevent communism from gaining appeal amid postwar devastation
  • NATO (1949) and the Warsaw Pact (1955) formalized opposing military alliances, and Germany itself was divided into a communist East and capitalist West, symbolized by the Berlin Wall (built 1961)
Cold War Conflicts & Proxy Wars
Direct U.S.-Soviet military conflict never occurred, but the superpowers fought for influence through proxy wars, arms races, and crises across the globe.
  • The Korean War (1950-1953) saw communist North Korea (backed by China and the USSR) fight U.S.-backed South Korea to a stalemate near the original border
  • The Vietnam War (U.S. involvement escalating through the 1960s, ending 1975) pitted communist North Vietnam against the U.S.-backed South, ending in communist victory and reunification under Ho Chi Minh's successors
  • The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) brought the world to the brink of nuclear war after the USSR placed missiles in Cuba, resolved through negotiation and mutual concessions
  • The Soviet-Afghan War (1979-1989) saw the USSR intervene to prop up a communist government, facing U.S.-backed mujahideen resistance in a costly, decade-long quagmire
  • The nuclear arms race produced the doctrine of 'mutually assured destruction' (MAD), a deterrent logic based on the certainty of mutual annihilation in any full-scale nuclear exchange
Decolonization in Asia
Weakened European empires and rising nationalist movements led to rapid decolonization across Asia in the decades after WWII, though independence often came with partition and violence.
  • India gained independence from Britain in 1947 after decades of nonviolent resistance led by Mohandas Gandhi and the Indian National Congress under Jawaharlal Nehru
  • Partition (1947) split British India into Hindu-majority India and Muslim-majority Pakistan, triggering mass migration and horrific communal violence that killed hundreds of thousands to over a million people
  • Vietnam's independence struggle against France (ending with French defeat at Dien Bien Phu, 1954) was followed by Cold War-driven division and the Vietnam War
  • Indonesia declared independence from the Netherlands in 1945, achieving full recognition after years of armed and diplomatic struggle by 1949
  • Ho Chi Minh and other Asian nationalist leaders often blended anti-colonial nationalism with communist ideology, linking decolonization to Cold War alignment
Decolonization in Africa
Nearly all of Africa achieved independence between the late 1950s and mid-1970s, through both negotiated transitions and violent liberation struggles, often leaving fragile postcolonial states.
  • Ghana, led by Kwame Nkrumah, became the first sub-Saharan African colony to gain independence (1957), inspiring pan-Africanist movements across the continent
  • The Algerian War of Independence (1954-1962) against France was exceptionally brutal, involving guerrilla warfare, terrorism, and torture on both sides before Algeria won independence
  • The Mau Mau Uprising in Kenya (1952-1960) against British rule involved harsh British repression, including detention camps, before Kenyan independence in 1963
  • The Congo's rapid, poorly prepared transition to independence (1960) led to political chaos, the assassination of Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba, and Cold War intervention by both superpowers
  • Many postcolonial African states struggled with arbitrary colonial borders that grouped rival ethnic groups together, weak institutions, and continued economic dependence on former colonizers (neocolonialism)
The Non-Aligned Movement & Global South Solidarity
Newly independent nations sought to avoid becoming pawns in the Cold War by forming a coalition committed to independence from both superpower blocs and mutual economic development.
  • The Bandung Conference (1955) in Indonesia brought together African and Asian leaders (including Nehru, Sukarno, Nasser, and Zhou Enlai) to promote decolonization, economic cooperation, and neutrality in the Cold War
  • The Non-Aligned Movement (formally founded 1961), led by figures like Josip Broz Tito (Yugoslavia), Jawaharlal Nehru (India), and Gamal Abdel Nasser (Egypt), rejected formal alignment with either the U.S. or USSR
  • Nasser's nationalization of the Suez Canal (1956) and the resulting Suez Crisis demonstrated growing Global South assertiveness against former colonial powers
  • Non-alignment did not mean isolation — many members still received aid or arms from both superpowers while formally avoiding alliance commitments
  • The movement highlighted shared Global South concerns: ending colonialism, promoting economic development, and resisting neocolonial economic dependency on former imperial powers
The End of the Cold War
Economic stagnation, reform movements, and the collapse of Soviet political control over Eastern Europe brought the Cold War to a rapid and largely peaceful end between 1989 and 1991.
  • Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms — glasnost (openness) and perestroika (economic restructuring) — aimed to revitalize the stagnant Soviet system but instead accelerated its unraveling
  • Eastern European states threw off Soviet-backed communist governments in 1989 (the 'Revolutions of 1989'), symbolized by the fall of the Berlin Wall in November 1989
  • Germany reunified in 1990, and the Warsaw Pact dissolved as former Soviet satellite states pursued independent, often pro-Western paths
  • The Soviet Union itself dissolved in December 1991, splitting into Russia and fourteen other independent states, ending nearly 70 years of communist rule
  • The Cold War's end left the United States as the sole global superpower and prompted debates over a 'new world order,' globalization, and the spread of market economies and democratic governance
The Cold War was not a 'hot' direct war between the U.S. and USSR — the superpowers never fought each other directly, instead waging proxy wars (Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan) and an arms/space race.
Decolonization did not happen uniformly — some transitions were largely peaceful and negotiated (Ghana, India), while others involved prolonged, brutal violence (Algeria, Kenya, Congo).
Non-alignment did not mean total neutrality or isolation from the Cold War — many Non-Aligned Movement members still accepted aid or weapons from the U.S. or USSR while avoiding formal alliance commitments.
Gorbachev's reforms (glasnost and perestroika) were intended to save and strengthen the Soviet system, not deliberately dismantle it — their unintended consequence was accelerating the USSR's collapse.

The Mongol Empire's unification of Eurasian trade routes (Pax Mongolica) is a prime example of how political stability boosted long-distance commerce.
Key fact
The Black Death, spread via Silk Road and Mongol trade contacts, killed roughly a third of Europe's population and caused major social and economic change.
Key fact
Religions (Buddhism, Islam) spread primarily through peaceful means — merchants, missionaries, and Sufi mystics — along these trade networks, not solely through conquest.

Unit 3: Land-Based Empires

The Ottoman Empire
The Ottoman Turks built a powerful Islamic empire spanning three continents, famously capturing Constantinople in 1453 and ruling a diverse population through the millet system.
The Safavid Empire
The Safavid Empire made Twelver Shia Islam the official state religion of Persia, creating a lasting Sunni-Shia divide with its Ottoman rivals.
The Mughal Empire
The Mughal Empire united most of South Asia under Muslim rule, blending Persian, Islamic, and Hindu traditions and reaching a cultural and administrative peak under Akbar.
Ming and Qing China
China alternated between the ethnically Chinese Ming Dynasty and the Manchu-led Qing Dynasty, both maintaining centralized bureaucratic rule and periods of relative isolation from the West.
Russia Under the Tsars
Moscow's grand princes threw off Mongol ('Tatar') control and built a centralized, autocratic Russian state that expanded eastward across Siberia.
Comparing Land-Based (Gunpowder) Empires
Historians group the Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, Ming/Qing, and Russian states as 'gunpowder empires' whose rulers used firearms and cannon to centralize power over vast land territories.
Key fact
The 'gunpowder empires' — Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal — used cannon and firearms to centralize power over large land empires between roughly 1450-1750.
Key fact
The Ottoman capture of Constantinople in 1453 ended the Byzantine Empire and marked a major shift in Mediterranean and European power.
Key fact
Religious tolerance policy varied dramatically by ruler: Akbar's tolerance contrasts sharply with Aurangzeb's later enforcement of Islamic orthodoxy in Mughal India.
Key fact
The Qing Dynasty, founded by the non-Chinese Manchus, nonetheless preserved the Confucian civil service exam system to legitimize and staff its bureaucracy.

Unit 4: Transoceanic Connections 1450-1750

European Age of Exploration
Portugal and Spain pioneered transoceanic voyages driven by the search for direct routes to Asian spices, Christian missionary zeal, and new navigational technology.
The Columbian Exchange
Contact between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres after 1492 triggered a massive global transfer of crops, animals, people, and diseases with transformative and often devastating effects.
Spanish and Portuguese Colonization
Spain and Portugal established the first major European colonial empires in the Americas, extracting resources through forced labor systems and imposing Catholicism on Indigenous populations.
The Atlantic Slave Trade
As Indigenous populations collapsed from disease and overwork, European colonizers turned to the forced transportation of enslaved Africans to the Americas, creating a brutal, large-scale system of racialized slavery.
Labor Systems in the Americas
Colonizing powers developed varied coerced labor systems—encomienda, mita, chattel slavery, and indentured servitude—to extract wealth from American colonies.
State Consolidation & Absolutism in Europe
European monarchs used the wealth and administrative demands of overseas empire to strengthen centralized, often absolutist state power at home.
Key fact
The Columbian Exchange transferred crops, animals, people, and diseases between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres, with catastrophic demographic effects on Indigenous Americans.
Key fact
An estimated 12+ million enslaved Africans were transported across the Atlantic in the Middle Passage as part of the triangular trade system.
Key fact
Spanish silver from Potosí, moved via the Manila Galleon trade, connected the Americas directly to Ming/Qing China's economy for the first time.
Key fact
Labor systems (encomienda, mita, chattel slavery, indentured servitude) varied by colonizer and region but all extracted forced labor to generate colonial wealth.

Unit 5: Revolutions 1750-1900

Enlightenment Ideas & the American Revolution
Enlightenment philosophers challenged divine-right monarchy with reason, natural rights, and social-contract theory, and their ideas directly inspired the first successful colonial independence movement.
The French Revolution (1789-1799)
Financial crisis, Enlightenment ideas, and deep social inequality under the Old Regime combined to topple the French monarchy and produce a radical, then reactionary, revolution.
The Haitian Revolution (1791-1804)
Enslaved and free people of color in France's wealthiest colony, Saint-Domingue, turned revolutionary ideals into the only successful large-scale slave revolt in history, founding the first Black republic.
Latin American Independence Movements
Creole elites, inspired by Enlightenment ideas and emboldened by Spain's and Portugal's weakness during the Napoleonic Wars, led independence movements across most of Latin America by the 1820s.
Origins of the Industrial Revolution
Beginning in Britain around 1750-1800, a cluster of agricultural, technological, and economic changes shifted production from hand tools and human/animal power to machines and fossil fuels, reshaping the global economy.
Reactions & Consequences of the Age of Revolutions
The political revolutions of 1750-1848 spread nationalism and liberalism across Europe and the Americas, while early industrialization generated new ideologies responding to its social costs.
Key fact
The Haitian Revolution (1791-1804) was the only successful large-scale slave revolt in world history and produced the first Black-led republic.
Key fact
Enlightenment natural-rights language (Locke, Rousseau) provided the ideological justification for the American, French, Haitian, and Latin American revolutions alike.
Key fact
Latin American independence was led overwhelmingly by creole elites, not Indigenous or enslaved populations, and left existing racial/economic hierarchies largely intact.
Key fact
Britain industrialized first because of coal/iron reserves, capital and credit markets, colonial markets and raw materials, and a labor force freed up by agricultural enclosure.

Unit 6: Imperialism & Migration

Causes of New Imperialism
Industrialized nations built overseas empires from roughly 1850-1914 for a mix of economic, political, and ideological reasons, using technology that gave them decisive military and administrative advantages.
The Scramble for Africa
European powers partitioned nearly the entire African continent in a few decades, formalizing their claims at the Berlin Conference without African representation.
Imperialism in Asia & the Pacific
European powers, along with the United States and a rapidly modernizing Japan, extended formal and informal control over much of Asia through trade concessions, treaty ports, and outright colonization.
Colonial Rule: Direct and Indirect Systems
Imperial powers used varying administrative strategies to control colonized peoples, extract resources, and maintain order with minimal European personnel.
Forced & Coerced Labor Migration
Imperial economies depended on massive movements of coerced and semi-coerced labor after the abolition of the Atlantic slave trade, reshaping the demographics of the Americas, Africa, and Asia.
Resistance to Imperialism
Colonized peoples resisted imperial rule through armed rebellion, cultural/religious revitalization movements, and early nationalist political organizing, with mixed success before 1900.
Key fact
By 1914, European powers controlled roughly 90% of Africa, a partition formalized at the 1884-1885 Berlin Conference without any African representation.
Key fact
Japan's Meiji Restoration (1868) was the major non-Western exception: rapid modernization let Japan avoid colonization and become an imperial power itself.
Key fact
Indentured servitude (especially of Indian and Chinese laborers) replaced much of the enslaved labor force after Britain's 1833 abolition, creating lasting diasporas worldwide.
Key fact
Ethiopia's victory at the Battle of Adwa (1896) made it, along with Liberia, one of only two African states to remain independent through the imperial era.

Unit 7: Global Conflict 1900-present

Causes of World War I
A tangle of long-term structural tensions and a single assassination combined to plunge Europe's great powers into a war of unprecedented scale by August 1914.
The End of WWI and Its Aftermath
The war's end reshaped the map of Europe and the Middle East, while its punitive peace settlement and economic destruction sowed seeds for future conflict.
Interwar Totalitarianism
Economic crisis, humiliation from WWI, and fear of communism enabled totalitarian regimes to seize and consolidate absolute control over politics, economy, and society in Italy, Germany, and the Soviet Union.
Causes and Course of World War II
Failed collective security, appeasement, and aggressive expansion by Axis powers in Europe and Asia led to the deadliest conflict in human history.
The Holocaust and Wartime Atrocities
Nazi Germany's racial ideology culminated in the systematic genocide of six million Jews and millions of other victims, while atrocities occurred on multiple fronts of the war.
Global Consequences of the World Wars
The two world wars devastated Europe and Asia, accelerated the decline of European colonial empires, and created new international institutions meant to prevent future catastrophic conflict.
Key fact
World War I's MAIN causes (Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism) turned a regional Balkan crisis into a continent-wide, then global, war.
Key fact
The Treaty of Versailles's punitive terms (War Guilt Clause, reparations, territorial losses) fueled German resentment that Hitler and the Nazis later exploited.
Key fact
The Holocaust systematically murdered approximately six million Jews, alongside millions of other targeted groups, through the Nazi 'Final Solution.'
Key fact
Both world wars bankrupted and delegitimized European colonial empires, directly accelerating the decolonization movements of the mid-20th century.

Unit 8: Cold War & Decolonization

Origins of the Cold War
The wartime alliance between the U.S. and USSR collapsed after 1945 into a decades-long ideological, political, and military rivalry that divided much of the world into competing blocs.
Cold War Conflicts & Proxy Wars
Direct U.S.-Soviet military conflict never occurred, but the superpowers fought for influence through proxy wars, arms races, and crises across the globe.
Decolonization in Asia
Weakened European empires and rising nationalist movements led to rapid decolonization across Asia in the decades after WWII, though independence often came with partition and violence.
Decolonization in Africa
Nearly all of Africa achieved independence between the late 1950s and mid-1970s, through both negotiated transitions and violent liberation struggles, often leaving fragile postcolonial states.
The Non-Aligned Movement & Global South Solidarity
Newly independent nations sought to avoid becoming pawns in the Cold War by forming a coalition committed to independence from both superpower blocs and mutual economic development.
The End of the Cold War
Economic stagnation, reform movements, and the collapse of Soviet political control over Eastern Europe brought the Cold War to a rapid and largely peaceful end between 1989 and 1991.
Key fact
The Truman Doctrine (1947) and Marshall Plan (1948) established the U.S. policy of 'containment' aimed at stopping the spread of communism, especially in Europe.
Key fact
India's 1947 partition into India and Pakistan triggered one of the largest and most violent mass migrations in history, killing hundreds of thousands.
Key fact
The Non-Aligned Movement, formalized in 1961 out of the 1955 Bandung Conference, represented newly independent nations' attempt to avoid Cold War bloc alignment.
Key fact
The Soviet Union's dissolution in December 1991 ended the Cold War, leaving the United States as the world's sole superpower.

The Crusades were primarily religious/military campaigns for Jerusalem, not simple 'trade expeditions' — though they did increase European exposure to Asian goods.

Unit 2: Networks of Exchange

Watch out
The Indian Ocean trade network relied on monsoon winds, not constant winds like the Atlantic — sailing seasons were dictated by wind direction reversal.
Watch out
Islam spread into West Africa mainly through trans-Saharan trade contact and voluntary conversion by elites, not primarily through military jihad.
Watch out
The Mongols did not destroy all trade — while conquest was brutal, the resulting empire's stability (Pax Mongolica) actually increased Silk Road trade volume.
Watch out
The Black Death originated in Central Asia and spread via trade routes; it was not a European-only phenomenon — it also devastated the Middle East and China.

Unit 3: Land-Based Empires

Watch out
The Ottoman millet system granted religious communities self-governance, not equal political power — it was tolerance for social order, not democracy.
Watch out
The Safavid-Ottoman rivalry was about Shia vs. Sunni Islam and territorial control, not a simple 'Christian vs. Muslim' conflict.
Watch out
Akbar's religious tolerance was a personal policy, not a permanent Mughal norm — Aurangzeb later reversed many of these tolerant practices.
Watch out
Russia's tsarist system developed by rejecting Mongol overlordship, not by adopting Mongol governance structures — 'Third Rome' ideology ties Russia to Byzantine Orthodoxy, not the Mongols.

Unit 4: Transoceanic Connections 1450-1750

Watch out
The 'discovery' of the Americas by Columbus is more accurately framed as sustained contact between two hemispheres that had been separate — millions of Indigenous people already lived there.
Watch out
Disease, not superior European weaponry alone, was the primary cause of Indigenous population collapse in the Americas.
Watch out
The Atlantic slave trade differed from earlier African slavery systems in that it was racialized, hereditary, and permanent (chattel slavery), not simply captivity from warfare.
Watch out
Mercantilism, not free trade, governed colonial economic policy in this era — colonies existed to benefit the mother country's balance of trade, not to develop independently.

Unit 5: Revolutions 1750-1900

Watch out
Don't equate the American Revolution with a full social revolution — it changed political rule but left slavery and existing social hierarchies largely intact.
Watch out
The French Revolution was not a single event: the moderate constitutional phase (1789-1792), the radical Terror (1793-1794), and Napoleon's authoritarian empire were distinct, shifting phases.
Watch out
Latin American 'independence' from Spain and Portugal did not mean liberation for enslaved people or Indigenous peoples — creole elites largely replaced peninsulares at the top of the same system.
Watch out
The Industrial Revolution did not happen everywhere at once — it began in Britain and spread unevenly to Belgium, the U.S., and Germany over decades, and most of the world remained agricultural into the 20th century.

Unit 6: Imperialism & Migration

Watch out
Don't call New Imperialism (c. 1850-1914) the same as earlier mercantile colonialism (1450-1750) — New Imperialism was driven by industrial capitalism, nationalism, and pseudo-scientific racism, and involved formal territorial annexation on a far larger scale.
Watch out
Indirect rule was not 'gentler' than direct rule — it often deepened ethnic divisions (e.g., colonial favoritism in Rwanda) that had lasting, violent consequences after independence.
Watch out
Indentured servitude was not the same as free wage labor — many indentured workers faced coercion, debt bondage, and conditions resembling slavery.
Watch out
Resistance to imperialism was common, not rare — movements like the Sepoy Rebellion, Boxer Rebellion, and Adwa show colonized peoples consistently resisted, even though most rebellions before 1900 were ultimately suppressed.

Unit 7: Global Conflict 1900-present

Watch out
Don't treat WWI's assassination of Franz Ferdinand as the sole cause — it was a trigger, not the underlying cause; militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism had built tension for decades.
Watch out
Fascism (Italy, Germany) and communism (Soviet Union) are not the same ideology — both were totalitarian and used similar tools of control, but fascism emphasized ultranationalism and private property, while communism aimed to abolish class and private property.
Watch out
Appeasement at Munich (1938) was intended to prevent war by conceding to Hitler's demands, but it is now widely seen as having emboldened further aggression rather than stopping it.
Watch out
The Holocaust targeted multiple groups, not only Jews — Roma, disabled people, Slavic civilians, political prisoners, and others were also systematically persecuted and murdered, though Jews were the primary and most numerous target of the genocide.

Unit 8: Cold War & Decolonization

Watch out
The Cold War was not a 'hot' direct war between the U.S. and USSR — the superpowers never fought each other directly, instead waging proxy wars (Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan) and an arms/space race.
Watch out
Decolonization did not happen uniformly — some transitions were largely peaceful and negotiated (Ghana, India), while others involved prolonged, brutal violence (Algeria, Kenya, Congo).
Watch out
Non-alignment did not mean total neutrality or isolation from the Cold War — many Non-Aligned Movement members still accepted aid or weapons from the U.S. or USSR while avoiding formal alliance commitments.
Watch out
Gorbachev's reforms (glasnost and perestroika) were intended to save and strengthen the Soviet system, not deliberately dismantle it — their unintended consequence was accelerating the USSR's collapse.
