Unit 1: The Rhetorical Situation6 concepts · ~34 min

▾

SOAPSTone

Every piece of writing happens inside a specific situation — SOAPSTone is a checklist for reading that situation before you analyze anything else.
  • Speaker: who is writing or speaking, and what do we know about their background, authority, or perspective?
  • Occasion: what event, moment, or context prompted this piece of writing?
  • Audience: who is the writer specifically trying to reach or persuade?
  • Purpose: what does the writer want the audience to think, feel, or do as a result?
  • Subject: what is the piece actually about, stated as specifically as possible?
  • Tone: the writer's attitude toward the subject, revealed through word choice and style.

Exigence

Exigence is the specific problem, need, or urgency that makes a piece of writing necessary right now.
  • Every argument responds to something — a controversy, an event, a gap in public understanding.
  • Identifying the exigence helps explain WHY the writer chose this moment and this approach.
  • Exigence is different from subject: the subject is what the text is about; the exigence is why it needed to be written.

Context and constraints

Writers don't create in a vacuum — the situation itself shapes what choices are even available to them.
  • Context includes the historical moment, medium (speech, essay, ad), and cultural setting a text is produced in.
  • Constraints are limits on the writer: word count, audience expectations, what is considered appropriate for the occasion.
  • A skilled writer works within constraints rather than ignoring them — this is part of what you analyze.

Purpose vs. subject

Confusing what a text is ABOUT with what it is trying to DO is one of the most common early mistakes in rhetorical reading.
  • Subject answers 'what is this about?' Purpose answers 'what does the writer want to happen because of this?'
  • Two texts can share a subject (e.g., climate change) but have completely different purposes (to alarm, to reassure, to mobilize, to inform).
  • Always state purpose as an infinitive verb phrase: 'to persuade,' 'to criticize,' 'to celebrate' — not as a noun.

The rhetorical triangle and kairos

Every text is shaped by the relationship among the writer, the audience, and the subject, at a particular moment.
  • The rhetorical triangle links speaker (ethos), audience (pathos), and message/subject (logos).
  • Kairos is the opportune moment: the right argument delivered at the right time to the right people.
  • A response ignores kairos when it is technically sound but poorly timed or aimed.
  • Exigence supplies the 'why now'; kairos supplies the 'why this approach, at this moment'.
  • On the exam, name the rhetorical situation precisely before analyzing choices.

Audience analysis

Effective writers shape appeals around what a specific audience knows, values, and needs.
  • Identify the audience's likely assumptions, values, and level of prior knowledge.
  • A hostile audience calls for concession and shared premises before disagreement.
  • A sympathetic audience can be moved to action rather than merely persuaded of a position.
  • Diction, examples, and tone should all be calibrated to that audience.
  • Ask: what does this audience need to believe or feel to accept the claim?
Confusing the SUBJECT of a text (what it's about) with its PURPOSE (what it wants to accomplish) — these are always separate questions.
Assuming the audience is 'everyone' — strong rhetorical analysis identifies a SPECIFIC intended audience, even if the text is public.
Describing tone with a vague word like 'negative' instead of a precise one like 'scornful,' 'wistful,' or 'indignant.'
Treating 'audience' as 'anyone who reads it' — analysis must name the specific intended audience.

Unit 2: Rhetorical Appeals & Logical Fallacies6 concepts · ~34 min

▾

Ethos, pathos, logos

Aristotle's three appeals are the foundation of persuasion — almost every argument leans on some combination of the three.
  • Ethos appeals to credibility and character — why should we trust this speaker? Built through expertise, fairness, or shared values.
  • Pathos appeals to emotion — the writer wants the audience to FEEL something (fear, hope, anger, sympathy) that moves them toward the writer's position.
  • Logos appeals to logic and reason — facts, statistics, examples, and clear chains of reasoning.

Building ethos

Ethos isn't just 'the writer is famous' — it's actively constructed within the text itself.
  • Citing credentials or firsthand experience builds ethos by establishing authority.
  • Acknowledging the opposing side fairly builds ethos by showing the writer is reasonable, not one-sided.
  • Using precise, careful language (rather than sloppy or inflammatory language) builds ethos through demonstrated competence.

Common logical fallacies

A fallacy is a flaw in reasoning that makes an argument persuasive-SOUNDING without actually being logically sound.
  • Ad hominem: attacking the person making an argument instead of the argument itself.
  • Straw man: misrepresenting an opponent's argument as weaker than it really is, then attacking that weak version.
  • False dilemma (either/or): presenting only two options when more actually exist.
  • Slippery slope: claiming one small step will inevitably lead to an extreme, unlikely outcome without justifying the chain.
  • Hasty generalization: drawing a broad conclusion from too small or unrepresentative a sample.

Identifying appeals in a passage

On the exam, you must be able to point to a SPECIFIC line and name which appeal it uses and why it works.
  • Look for statistics, expert citations, and cause-effect reasoning as signals of logos.
  • Look for vivid imagery, personal anecdotes, and charged language as signals of pathos.
  • Look for the writer establishing shared values or referencing their own credibility as signals of ethos.
  • A single passage often blends more than one appeal — strong analysis explains HOW they work together, not just which is present.

Logos: evidence and forms of reasoning

Logical appeal rests on the type and arrangement of evidence and inference.
  • Deductive reasoning moves from a general principle to a specific conclusion.
  • Inductive reasoning builds from specific cases to a general claim (always somewhat provisional).
  • Evidence types: facts and statistics, expert testimony, anecdotes, analogies, hypothetical examples.
  • Analogical reasoning is only as strong as the similarity between the two things compared.
  • Weak logos often shows up as hasty generalization or a false cause (post hoc).

Persona and the projected self

Ethos includes the character a writer constructs on the page, not just real-world credentials.
  • Persona is the version of the writer the text projects (reasonable, outraged, humble, authoritative).
  • Word choice, sentence rhythm, and level of formality all build persona.
  • A shift in persona mid-text is a deliberate rhetorical move worth analyzing.
  • Self-deprecation or admitting limits can strengthen ethos by signaling honesty.
  • Analyze persona by asking how the writer wants to be seen and how the language creates that impression.
Simply LABELING an appeal ('this is pathos') without explaining HOW it functions and WHY it's effective earns little credit — always connect device to effect.

Unit 3: Rhetorical Devices & Figurative Language6 concepts · ~34 min

▾

Devices of repetition

Repeating words or structures is one of the most common ways writers create emphasis and rhythm.
  • Anaphora: repetition of a word or phrase at the BEGINNING of successive clauses or sentences ('I have a dream that...').
  • Epistrophe: repetition at the END of successive clauses ('of the people, by the people, for the people').
  • Parallelism: repeating a grammatical structure (e.g., a series of matching phrases) to create balance and rhythm.
  • Anadiplosis: repeating the last word of one clause at the start of the next, linking ideas together.

Devices of contrast and balance

Placing ideas in tension with each other is a powerful way to make a point memorable.
  • Antithesis: placing two contrasting ideas in a parallel grammatical structure ('Ask not what your country can do for you—ask what you can do for your country').
  • Juxtaposition: placing two things side by side to highlight their differences.
  • Chiasmus: a reversal of grammatical structure in successive phrases ('ask not what your country can do for you, ask what you can do for your country' is also chiastic in structure).

Figurative language

Figurative language asks the reader to understand one thing in terms of another, creating a vivid or persuasive image.
  • Metaphor: a direct comparison without 'like' or 'as' (e.g., 'the classroom was a zoo').
  • Simile: a comparison using 'like' or 'as.'
  • Personification: giving human qualities to something nonhuman.
  • Hyperbole: deliberate exaggeration for effect, not meant to be taken literally.
  • Understatement: deliberately downplaying something for ironic or persuasive effect.

Rhetorical questions and irony

Some devices work by NOT stating things directly, forcing the reader to fill in the meaning themselves.
  • A rhetorical question is asked for effect, not to get a literal answer — it leads the reader to a conclusion the writer wants them to reach.
  • Verbal irony says one thing but means another, often to criticize or add wit.
  • Sarcasm is a sharp, often mocking form of verbal irony.
  • Understanding irony requires paying attention to CONTEXT — the same sentence can be sincere or ironic depending on the situation.

Sound, rhythm, and syntactic pacing

How language sounds and moves shapes emphasis and emotion.
  • Alliteration and assonance bind phrases and make lines memorable.
  • Asyndeton (omitting conjunctions) speeds a list and can feel urgent or overwhelming.
  • Polysyndeton (repeating conjunctions) slows a list and can feel weighty or exhausting.
  • Cadence and sentence length create a rhythm that reinforces tone.
  • Analyze the effect of the sound, not just its name.

Allusion, analogy, and extended metaphor

Comparisons and references import meaning and feeling from elsewhere.
  • Allusion is a brief reference to a shared text, event, or figure, relying on the audience to recognize it.
  • An effective allusion transfers the authority or emotion of its source to the present argument.
  • An extended metaphor develops a single comparison across a passage, structuring the reader's understanding.
  • Analogy argues that because two situations are alike in some ways, they are alike in another.
  • Always connect the comparison to the writer's purpose.

Unit 4: Style: Diction, Syntax & Tone6 concepts · ~34 min

▾

Diction

Diction is a writer's specific word choice — and word choice is never neutral; every word carries connotation.
  • Denotation is a word's literal dictionary definition; connotation is the emotional association attached to it.
  • 'Slender' and 'skinny' can denote the same thing but carry very different connotations (positive vs. negative).
  • Formal diction uses elevated, precise vocabulary; colloquial diction uses casual, everyday language — the choice signals audience and tone.
  • Concrete diction (specific, sensory) creates vivid images; abstract diction (general, conceptual) creates a more analytical or distanced feel.

Syntax

Syntax is how a writer arranges words and constructs sentences — sentence structure itself is a rhetorical choice.
  • Short, simple sentences create emphasis, urgency, or a blunt tone.
  • Long, complex sentences with multiple clauses can build a sense of accumulation, complexity, or breathless momentum.
  • A sentence fragment used deliberately can create dramatic emphasis on a single idea.
  • Sentence variety (mixing short and long sentences) controls pacing and keeps the reader engaged.

Tone and shifts in tone

Tone is the writer's attitude toward the subject — and tone can, and often does, shift over the course of a passage.
  • Tone words must be specific and precise: not just 'angry' but 'indignant,' 'contemptuous,' or 'exasperated.'
  • A tone shift is often signaled by a transition word ('but,' 'however,' 'yet') or a change in sentence structure.
  • Identifying WHERE a tone shift happens and WHY the writer made that choice is a common analysis task.

Point of view and style

Who is telling the story or making the argument, and how, shapes the entire piece.
  • First person ('I') creates intimacy and personal authority; third person creates distance and can seem more objective.
  • An author's overall 'voice' — the combination of diction, syntax, and tone that makes their writing recognizable — is called style.
  • Register refers to how formal or informal the language is relative to the occasion.

Detail, imagery, and figurative diction

Concrete detail and sensory language work together to shape the reader's experience and judgment.
  • Imagery is language that appeals to the senses; it makes an abstraction vivid and felt.
  • Selection of detail is itself an argument: what a writer chooses to show reveals a stance.
  • Figurative diction (metaphor woven into word choice) colors a subject positively or negatively.
  • Juxtaposing images can imply a comparison or contrast without stating it.
  • Ask what impression the accumulated details create and why the writer wants it.

Periodic and cumulative (loose) sentences

Where a writer places the main clause changes emphasis and suspense.
  • A periodic sentence delays the main idea until the end, building suspense and emphasis.
  • A cumulative (loose) sentence states the main idea first, then adds modifying detail.
  • Long, complex sentences can suggest careful reasoning; short sentences land with force.
  • A single short sentence after several long ones creates emphasis by contrast.
  • Analyze syntax by describing the pattern and its effect on pace and emphasis.
Using a vague, overused tone word like 'happy' or 'sad' instead of a precise word that matches the passage's actual nuance.

Unit 5: Claims, Evidence & Commentary6 concepts · ~34 min

▾

Types of claims

Every argument is built on a claim — but not all claims are the same kind.
  • A claim of fact asserts something is true or false and can be verified.
  • A claim of value argues something is good, bad, right, or wrong.
  • A claim of policy argues that something should or should not be done.
  • A strong thesis usually combines these — it takes a clear, specific, arguable position, not just a statement of topic.

Types of evidence

Evidence is what makes a claim more than just an opinion — different kinds of evidence serve different purposes.
  • Statistical evidence (data, numbers, studies) supports logos-based arguments.
  • Anecdotal evidence (a specific story or example) supports pathos and makes an abstract point concrete.
  • Expert testimony borrows credibility (ethos) from an authority on the subject.
  • Historical or literary evidence draws parallels or precedent to support a claim.

Commentary vs. summary

The single most common weakness in student writing is substituting summary for commentary — commentary is what actually earns credit.
  • Summary restates WHAT the evidence says; commentary explains WHY that evidence matters and HOW it supports the argument.
  • A good ratio is roughly one sentence of evidence to two or more sentences of commentary/analysis.
  • Commentary should answer 'so what?' — why does this evidence prove the claim, and why should the reader care?

Line of reasoning

A line of reasoning is the logical thread that connects a thesis through each piece of evidence to a coherent overall argument.
  • Each body paragraph should advance a DIFFERENT piece of the argument, not repeat the same point with new evidence.
  • Reasoning should flow logically: claim → evidence → commentary that connects the evidence back to the claim.
  • A strong essay's paragraphs build on each other in a deliberate order, not just a random list of separate points.

Qualifying a claim

Strong arguments limit their claims to what the evidence can actually support.
  • Qualifiers (often, in most cases, under these conditions) make a claim more defensible.
  • A concession acknowledges a limit or an opposing point without abandoning the thesis.
  • Overstated claims ('this proves', 'everyone knows') invite easy refutation.
  • Scope words (all, some, many) should match the strength of the evidence.
  • On the exam, a qualified, precise thesis usually earns more than a sweeping one.

Sufficiency and relevance of evidence

Evidence must be both on-point and adequate in amount and kind to support the claim.
  • Relevant evidence bears directly on the specific claim, not a loosely related one.
  • Sufficient evidence is enough, and varied enough, to make the claim credible.
  • One vivid anecdote is rarely sufficient for a broad claim.
  • Cherry-picked data is relevant but insufficient (and misleading).
  • Evaluate a source by asking: does this actually support the point, and is it enough?

Unit 6: Organization, Structure & Counterargument6 concepts · ~34 min

▾

Introduction and thesis

A strong introduction orients the reader and stakes out a clear, defensible position.
  • An effective thesis is specific and arguable — someone could reasonably disagree with it.
  • Avoid a thesis that simply restates the prompt or announces intent ('In this essay I will discuss...').
  • The introduction should establish context briefly before arriving at the thesis — it doesn't need to be long.

Body paragraph structure

Each body paragraph should function as a self-contained argument that supports the overall thesis.
  • A topic sentence should state the paragraph's specific claim, not just announce a topic.
  • Evidence should be integrated smoothly, not simply dropped in as an unexplained quotation.
  • Commentary should close the paragraph by tying the evidence explicitly back to the thesis.

Counterargument and concession

Addressing the other side of an argument — rather than ignoring it — actually strengthens a writer's credibility and position.
  • A concession acknowledges a valid point on the opposing side before pivoting back to your own argument.
  • A rebuttal directly refutes an opposing point, explaining why it's flawed or less compelling.
  • Ignoring the strongest counterarguments makes an essay seem one-sided or naive, weakening ethos.

Conclusions and transitions

Transitions and conclusions are what make an essay feel unified rather than like a list of disconnected paragraphs.
  • Transitions should show the LOGICAL relationship between ideas (contrast, cause-effect, addition), not just be a filler word.
  • An effective conclusion does more than restate the thesis — it can broaden the argument's significance or implications.
  • A conclusion that introduces a brand-new, unsupported argument is a structural weakness, not a strength.

Modes of arrangement

Writers organize material in patterns that fit their purpose.
  • Chronological / narrative order suits process, history, or personal experience.
  • Cause and effect traces why something happened or what it will produce.
  • Problem-solution names a problem, then argues for a response.
  • Comparison-contrast sets two things side by side to clarify or evaluate.
  • Order of importance (climactic) builds to the strongest point.

Coherence and cohesion

Well-organized prose guides the reader from one idea to the next without gaps.
  • The 'given-new' principle: start sentences with familiar information, end with the new point.
  • Repeating key terms (rather than elegant variation) keeps a thread visible.
  • Pointing words (this, that, such, these) must have a clear referent.
  • Transitions should name the logical relationship (however, therefore, for instance), not just decorate.
  • Paragraph breaks signal a shift in subtopic or stage of the argument.

Using transition words ('also,' 'another thing') that show ADDITION but not actual logical relationship between ideas.

Unit 7: The Synthesis Essay6 concepts · ~34 min

▾

What the synthesis essay requires

The synthesis essay asks you to develop your OWN argument on a topic, using multiple provided sources as support.
  • You must take and defend a clear position on the prompt's issue — the sources are tools for YOUR argument, not the point of the essay.
  • You must cite at least three of the provided sources to support your line of reasoning.
  • The essay is not a summary of what each source says — that earns little credit on its own.

Selecting and using sources

Choosing which sources to use — and how to use them — is a strategic part of the synthesis essay.
  • Pick sources that best support the specific argument you're making, not just the first three in the packet.
  • You can use a source to support your point, or strategically use one to acknowledge and rebut a counterargument.
  • Cite sources by their letter (e.g., 'Source B') or a clear paraphrase — don't just quote at length without analysis.

Avoiding the summary trap

The most common way to lose points on the synthesis essay is turning it into a source-by-source book report.
  • Never organize the essay source-by-source ('Source A says... Source B says...') — organize it by YOUR own points, drawing on sources as needed within each.
  • Every citation needs commentary explaining how it supports YOUR specific claim.
  • A strong synthesis essay could be outlined using only the writer's own claims — the sources fill in the support.

Building a defensible position

Because the synthesis prompt is often a genuinely debatable issue, a defensible, specific position is essential.
  • A qualified position (agreeing with conditions or nuance) is completely acceptable and often stronger than an all-or-nothing stance.
  • Avoid a wishy-washy thesis that doesn't actually commit to anything — pick a specific, defensible lane.
  • Anticipate the strongest counterargument to your position and address it using one of the sources.

Synthesis vs. summary vs. compare-contrast

The synthesis essay asks you to build your own argument using sources, not to report on them.
  • Summary reports what each source says; synthesis uses sources as evidence for your claims.
  • Compare-contrast weighs sources against each other; synthesis subordinates them to your line of reasoning.
  • Organize body paragraphs around your sub-claims, bringing in whichever sources support each.
  • Sources that disagree are useful: use one to complicate or answer another.
  • Every citation should be doing a job in your argument.

Attribution and citation conventions

Sources must be integrated smoothly and credited clearly.
  • A signal phrase names the source in the sentence ('Source A argues that ...').
  • A parenthetical citation places the reference at the end ((Source A)).
  • Quote sparingly and only when the wording matters; otherwise paraphrase.
  • Introduce a quotation; never drop it in without framing.
  • The AP synthesis prompt requires citing at least three of the provided sources.

Unit 8: The Rhetorical Analysis Essay6 concepts · ~34 min

▾

What rhetorical analysis requires

This essay asks you to explain HOW a writer builds their argument, not whether you agree with it.
  • You are analyzing the WRITER'S choices (rhetorical strategies) and their effect on the intended audience — not arguing the topic yourself.
  • Start by identifying the rhetorical situation: speaker, audience, purpose, occasion.
  • A strong thesis identifies the writer's overall purpose AND previews the strategies you'll analyze to achieve it.

Selecting strategies to analyze

You can't analyze everything in a passage — choosing a few strong, well-supported strategies is more effective than a shallow list.
  • Choose 2–3 rhetorical strategies (appeals, devices, structure, diction/syntax) that are clearly present and significant.
  • Pick strategies you can trace across MULTIPLE parts of the passage, not just a single isolated line.
  • Organize by strategy or by the passage's structure (chronological) — either is valid if the reasoning is clear.

Connecting device to effect

The core skill of rhetorical analysis is always the same move: identify a specific choice, then explain its effect on the audience.
  • Never just quote a line and label a device — always follow with 'this creates the effect of...' or 'this persuades the audience to...'
  • Effects should tie back to the writer's overall purpose established in your thesis.
  • Strong analysis explains WHY a specific word or structure was chosen over alternatives, not just that it exists.

Common structural approaches

There's no single required structure, but successful essays usually follow one of two reliable patterns.
  • Strategy-based structure: one body paragraph per rhetorical strategy, tracing it through the whole passage.
  • Chronological structure: body paragraphs follow the passage's own organization (beginning, middle, end), analyzing shifts in strategy along the way.
  • Whichever structure you choose, each paragraph needs its own clear claim about a specific strategy's effect.

Building the analytical thesis

A rhetorical analysis thesis makes a defensible claim about the writer's choices and their purpose.
  • A weak thesis lists devices ('the author uses ethos, pathos, and logos').
  • A strong thesis states what the writer is doing and to what end ('to build urgency, the writer...').
  • It should be arguable: someone could reasonably read the choices differently.
  • It previews a line of reasoning, not a checklist.
  • The body then shows HOW specific choices create the effect the thesis names.

Analyzing tone and tonal shifts

Tone is the writer's attitude; changes in tone are deliberate and meaningful.
  • Name tone precisely (measured, scornful, wistful, urgent), not just 'serious' or 'emotional'.
  • Diction, syntax, imagery, and figurative language together create tone.
  • A shift in tone (marked by 'but', a new paragraph, a change in sentence length) signals a turn in the argument.
  • Explain what the shift accomplishes for the writer's purpose.
  • Track tone across the passage rather than assigning one label to the whole.

Unit 9: The Argument Essay6 concepts · ~34 min

▾

What the argument essay requires

The argument essay gives you a general claim or issue and asks YOU to develop and defend your own position using evidence from your own knowledge.
  • Unlike synthesis, there are no provided sources — evidence comes from your own reading, knowledge, and experience.
  • You may agree, disagree, or agree with qualifications — any defensible position is acceptable if well-supported.
  • The evidence you choose should be specific and relevant, not vague generalities.

Choosing strong evidence

Specific, well-explained evidence is far more persuasive than a long list of vague examples.
  • Historical examples, current events, literature, and personal experience are all valid evidence sources.
  • One or two well-developed examples with real commentary beat five examples mentioned in passing.
  • Evidence should be verifiably true or at least reasonably plausible — invented 'facts' undermine credibility.

Structuring the argument

A clear structure with acknowledged complexity signals sophisticated, college-level thinking.
  • State a clear, specific, defensible thesis early — avoid restating the prompt as your thesis.
  • Address a counterargument to show you've considered the issue's complexity, then explain why your position still holds.
  • A qualified thesis ('X is true, except when...') often demonstrates more sophisticated thinking than an absolute claim.

Sophistication of thought

The highest-scoring argument essays go beyond a basic thesis-evidence-conclusion structure to show real intellectual engagement.
  • Explaining the broader implications or stakes of your argument demonstrates sophistication.
  • Making nuanced distinctions (this is true in some contexts but not others) shows sophistication over an all-or-nothing stance.
  • A vivid, effective writing style alone does not equal sophistication — it must be paired with genuinely complex reasoning.

Line of reasoning, refutation, and concession

An argument essay needs a clear through-line and an honest treatment of opposing views.
  • The line of reasoning is the logical sequence connecting thesis, sub-claims, and evidence.
  • Refutation directly answers an opposing argument and shows why it fails.
  • Concession grants that an opposing point has some merit, then limits its importance.
  • Concede-then-counter ('while X is true, it does not follow that Y') strengthens ethos.
  • Every body paragraph should visibly advance the thesis.

Sophistication of thought

  • Vivid, purposeful style contributes to the impression of sophistication.

Common sentence errors

The multiple-choice section tests your ability to spot and fix grammar and structure problems in real writing.
  • A comma splice joins two independent clauses with only a comma — fix with a period, semicolon, or coordinating conjunction.
  • A sentence fragment is missing a subject, verb, or complete thought and cannot stand alone.
  • A run-on sentence joins independent clauses with no punctuation or connector at all.

Parallel structure and agreement

Consistency in grammatical form is tested constantly — mismatched structures are one of the most common 'fix this' question types.
  • Items in a list or series must share the same grammatical form ('to run, to jump, and to swim' — not 'to run, jumping, and swim').
  • Subject-verb agreement: a singular subject takes a singular verb, even when other words come between them.
  • Pronoun-antecedent agreement: a pronoun must match its antecedent in number ('everyone' is singular, so it takes 'his or her,' not 'their' in the strictest formal usage tested).

Multiple-choice reading questions

Reading-based multiple choice questions test close reading of nonfiction passages, similar to the skills used in rhetorical analysis.
  • Questions ask you to identify a passage's main claim, a specific rhetorical strategy's function, or how a paragraph relates to the whole.
  • Always go back to the passage to verify an answer choice rather than relying on memory or general impression.
  • Wrong answer choices are often 'half-right' — true in general but not actually supported by THIS specific passage.

Multiple-choice writing questions

Writing-based multiple choice questions ask you to improve a student-written passage, testing revision skills directly.
  • Questions may ask you to choose the best transition, the most effective word choice, or where to place a sentence for better organization.
  • 'Which choice best maintains the tone established in the passage?' questions require checking the surrounding sentences' tone, not just grammar.
  • Some questions ask which revision would be LEAST effective or should be deleted — read the question direction carefully before answering.

Punctuation for effect

Beyond correctness, punctuation shapes emphasis and rhythm.
  • A colon introduces an explanation, list, or appositive that completes the preceding clause.
  • A dash sets off an interruption or emphatic addition, more informal than parentheses.
  • A semicolon joins two closely related independent clauses without a conjunction.
  • Overusing dashes weakens their emphatic force.
  • On MC 'writing' questions, choose the punctuation that best serves clarity and the intended emphasis.

Revising for concision and precision

Multiple-choice writing questions and your essays reward economical, exact language.
  • Cut redundancy ('past history', 'end result', 'in order to' when 'to' works).
  • Replace vague words ('things', 'stuff', 'a lot') with specific nouns.
  • Prefer active voice unless the actor is unknown or unimportant.
  • Remove filler ('it is important to note that', 'the fact that').
  • In an MC revision item, the best answer is usually the clearest, not the longest.

"Rip Van Winkle" (Irving, 1819)

A comic folk tale from The Sketch Book, framed as papers left by the fictional historian Diedrich Knickerbocker. A lazy, good-natured villager sleeps for twenty years and wakes up in a new country.
  • Intro: Before the American Revolution, in an old Dutch village at the foot of the Kaatskill (Catskill) Mountains on the Hudson River, the colony still belongs to Great Britain. Rip Van Winkle, a descendant of brave Dutch settlers, is "a simple, good-natured fellow": he plays with the village children, tells them stories, helps any neighbor with any job, and is loved by every wife and dog in town. But he has "an insuperable aversion to all kinds of profitable labor", so his own farm is "the most pestilent little piece of ground", his fences fall down, his cow wanders off, his land shrinks, and his children run wild (his son Rip is a ragged "urchin"). His wife, Dame Van Winkle, nags him morning, noon and night. Rip escapes to the club of idle men who sit on a bench outside the village inn, under a portrait of King George the Third, where schoolmaster Derrick Van Brummel reads old newspapers aloud and silent landlord Nicholas Vedder shows his opinion with puffs of his pipe. When Dame Van Winkle breaks up even that, Rip's only escape is to take his gun and his dog Wolf, who is as henpecked as he is, into the woods.
  • Middle: One autumn day Rip goes squirrel shooting high in the Kaatskills. Late in the afternoon, tired and dreading going home to his wife, he rests on a green knoll overlooking the Hudson. A voice calls "Rip Van Winkle! Rip Van Winkle!" and Wolf bristles and growls. A short, square-built old man with a grizzled beard, dressed in old-fashioned Dutch clothes, climbs toward him carrying a heavy keg and signals for help. Rip helps him carry it up a ravine, hearing rolling noises like distant thunder, to a hollow shaped like an amphitheater. There a company of odd-looking men in old Dutch outfits, with long beards and strange faces, is silently playing ninepins; the rolling balls are the "thunder". No one speaks. They stare at Rip, and the stranger makes him serve drinks from the keg. When they aren't looking, Rip tastes the liquor (Hollands), likes it, drinks again and again, and finally falls into a deep sleep. The sleep lasts TWENTY YEARS, and during it the American Revolution happens.
  • End: Rip wakes on the same green knoll on a bright, sunny morning, thinking he slept only one night and dreading what his wife will say. His gun is rusted and falling apart, his joints are stiff, Wolf is gone, and the ravine he climbed is now a rushing mountain stream with no way through. In the village he knows no one, and his beard has grown a foot long. People stare and stroke their chins, children hoot at him, and dogs bark. His house is decayed, the roof has fallen in, and it is empty. The old inn has become "The Union Hotel, by Jonathan Doolittle", with a flagpole flying the stars and stripes, and King George's portrait has been repainted as "GENERAL WASHINGTON". A busy crowd argues about "rights of citizens", elections, Congress, liberty and Bunker's Hill. When asked whether he is "Federal or Democrat", Rip says he is "a loyal subject of the King" and is almost mobbed as "A tory! a spy!" He asks for his friends: Nicholas Vedder has been dead eighteen years, Brom Dutcher went to war and died at Stony Point, and Van Brummel became a general and is now in Congress. Someone points out a lazy young man leaning against a tree: Rip's son, the image of his father, and Rip is so confused he says "I'm not myself". A young woman with a baby turns out to be his daughter Judith Gardenier; she says her mother died recently, bursting a blood vessel while yelling at a peddler. An old woman recognizes him ("where have you been these twenty long years?"). Old Peter Vanderdonk confirms the legend: Hendrick Hudson and the crew of the Halfmoon return to the mountains every twenty years to play ninepins. Rip moves in with Judith and her farmer husband, goes back to idling at the inn, and becomes a respected elder who tells his story to every stranger. Freed from "petticoat government", he is happy. The old Dutch villagers still say thunder in the Kaatskills is Hudson's crew at ninepins, and henpecked husbands wish for "a quieting draught out of Rip Van Winkle's flagon". In the closing Note, Knickerbocker insists the tale is true.
  • Character: Rip Van Winkle: Protagonist. Simple, good-natured, henpecked and lazy about his own work. Mostly static: he is the same man after twenty years. Importance: he stands for the old, easygoing colonial past sleeping through the Revolution.
  • Character: Dame Van Winkle: Rip's wife, called a "termagant" and "virago"; her tongue is "the only edged tool that grows keener with constant use". Importance: the domestic "tyranny" Rip escapes, paralleling the political tyranny (King George) the colonies escaped. Irving's language clearly takes Rip's side.
  • Character: Judith Gardenier: Rip's grown daughter, who takes him in. Importance: she confirms his identity and gives him a home in the new world.
  • Character: Rip Jr.: Rip's son, a ragged copy of his father. Importance: when old Rip sees him lounging by the tree, he doubts his own identity ("Does nobody here know Rip Van Winkle?").
  • Characters: Nicholas Vedder, Derrick Van Brummel, Brom Dutcher: The silent innkeeper who ran the idlers' club, the schoolmaster who read old newspapers, and Rip's friend. After twenty years Vedder is dead, Van Brummel is in Congress and Dutcher died in the war, which shows how much has changed.
  • Characters: Hendrick Hudson's crew: The ghostly ninepin players of the Halfmoon, said to return every twenty years. Importance: the supernatural (Romantic) cause of the sleep.
  • Point of view: Third person, told by a narrator inside a frame: Geoffrey Crayon presents the tale as found among the papers of Diedrich Knickerbocker. The frame and the closing Note (claiming the story is fact) add humor and irony.
  • Themes: Change vs. tradition; freedom vs. duty; the birth of an American identity (King George to George Washington); nostalgia for the past; Irving's gentle satire of village politics and marriage.
  • Other important factors: Romantic elements: wild mountains, the supernatural, nostalgia. Irving's tone is humorous and urbane. The Introduction calls it an orderly tale of linked incidents told in time order, the opposite of Poe's tale of single effect.

"The Eve of St. Agnes" (Keats, 1819)

A Romantic narrative poem of 42 Spenserian stanzas (nine lines, rhyme ABABBCBCC, last line longer). Written 1819, published 1820.
  • Intro: "St. Agnes' Eve - Ah, bitter chill it was! The owl, for all his feathers, was a-cold." On the freezing night of January 20, an old Beadsman (a man paid to pray for others) says his rosary in the castle chapel with numb fingers, his breath rising "like pious incense". He walks past cold stone statues of the dead, hears music from the feast, and goes back to sit in ashes and pray. The Baron's castle is full of noisy guests at a grand party. Among them is his daughter Madeline, who barely notices the dancing because she is thinking of the St. Agnes legend: if a young woman goes to bed without supper, doesn't look behind her, lies on her back and prays, she will dream of her future husband that night.
  • Middle: Porphyro, a young man from a family that hates Madeline's, has ridden across the moors and sneaks into the castle, where every guest would kill him (Hildebrand and old Lord Maurice are named as enemies). He finds old Angela, Madeline's nurse, who is terrified for him and tells him about Madeline's St. Agnes ritual. Porphyro gets an idea (a "stratagem"): let him hide in Madeline's room. Angela is shocked, but when he swears he means no harm and threatens to call out to his enemies if she refuses, she agrees, hides him in a closet in the room, and brings delicacies. Madeline comes up with her candle, which goes out. Moonlight shines through a stained-glass window onto her as she prays, undresses and falls asleep. Porphyro creeps out, sets a table with candied apple, quince, plum, jellies, dates and spices, and plays an old song on her lute, "La belle dame sans mercy".
  • End: Madeline wakes but still seems to be dreaming. She is upset because the real Porphyro looks pale and sad compared to the one in her dream. Porphyro says "Awake! arise! my love" and melts into her dream as the storm hits the window; he calls her his bride and promises to take her home with him across the moors. They slip down through the castle past drunken, sleeping guests and a porter; the bloodhound recognizes Madeline and lets them pass, and they flee "into the storm". The poem then says it all happened "ages long ago": that night the Baron and his warriors had nightmares, Angela died soon after, her face twisted, and the Beadsman died unnoticed among his cold ashes.
  • Character: Madeline: The Baron's daughter. Pure, dreamy, devoted to the ritual. Importance: the romance and the dream-vs-reality idea center on her.
  • Character: Porphyro: The young lover from the enemy family. Bold and romantic. Importance: his risky entry drives the plot.
  • Character: Angela: Madeline's old nurse. Fearful but helps Porphyro. Importance: the helper who makes the meeting possible; her death darkens the ending.
  • Character: the Beadsman: An old man paid to pray for others. Importance: frames the poem at both ends with cold, age and death, in contrast to the young lovers' warmth.
  • Point of view: Third-person narrator telling a story in verse.
  • Themes: Love vs. family hatred (like Romeo and Juliet); dream vs. reality; youth and warmth vs. age, cold and death; the holy vs. the sensual.
  • Other important factors: Rich sensory imagery, contrast (cold outside, warm inside), alliteration, personification and allusion. Compare it with Poe's Usher and "The Haunted Palace": romance, dreams and the fall of a great house.

"The Fall of the House of Usher" (Poe, 1839)

A Gothic short story built for Poe's "single effect": dread. The Introduction calls it Poe at his best.
  • Intro: On a dull, dark, soundless autumn day, the unnamed narrator rides alone to the House of Usher. At the sight of it he feels "an insufferable gloom": bleak walls, "vacant eye-like windows", rotting tree trunks, and fungus hanging from the stones. Looking at its upside-down reflection in the black tarn (small lake) beside it only makes his dread worse. He notices a thin crack (fissure) running zigzag from the roof down the front wall into the tarn. He has come because his boyhood friend Roderick Usher sent a desperate letter saying he is ill in body and mind and begging for company. A valet leads him through dark halls; he passes the family doctor on the stairs.
  • Middle: Roderick is shockingly changed: pale as a corpse, with huge shining eyes, thin lips and wild, silky hair. He is extremely nervous and says he suffers from a family illness: every sense is painfully sharp, so he can eat only bland food, wear only certain clothes, stand no flowers, bright light or sounds except stringed instruments. He says he is not afraid of danger but of FEAR itself, and believes the house itself (its stones, fungi and tarn) has a strange, living influence over him. His twin sister Madeline, his only relative, is dying of a mysterious illness that makes her waste away and go into trances. She passes through the room without seeing the narrator, and the next evening she collapses. For days the narrator tries to cheer Roderick with painting, reading and music. Roderick paints weird pictures, including a long, bright tunnel with no light source, and sings "The Haunted Palace", a poem about a fair palace ruled by "Thought" that is overrun by "evil things", which mirrors his own mind. One evening Roderick announces that Madeline is "no more". He decides to keep her body for two weeks in a vault inside the house before burial, saying it's because of her strange illness and the doctors' curiosity. The narrator helps carry her to a copper-lined vault deep under his own bedroom. He notices how alike the twins are, and that Madeline still has a faint blush on her face and a lingering smile. Over the next week Roderick gets worse: pacing, staring, listening to something no one else hears.
  • End: On the seventh or eighth night a wild storm hits, and the house glows with a strange light. Roderick comes to the narrator's room in hysteria, and to calm him the narrator reads aloud the "Mad Trist" by Sir Launcelot Canning. Each time something happens in the story, it is echoed by real sounds in the house: when the hero Ethelred smashes the hermit's door, a cracking sound; when he kills the dragon, a horrible shriek; when a brass shield falls, a metal clang. Roderick, rocking in his chair, confesses they buried Madeline alive: he has heard her moving in the coffin for days and was too afraid to say so. "Madman! I tell you that she now stands without the door!" The doors blow open and Madeline stands there in her white burial robes, bloody from struggling out of the vault. She falls on her brother, and both die, Roderick of the terror he expected. The narrator runs out into the storm. Looking back, he sees a blood-red moon shining through the crack, which widens until the walls fall apart and the tarn closes silently over the remains of the House of Usher.
  • Character: the narrator: Unnamed friend of Roderick. Rational at first, gradually infected by the house's gloom. Importance: the reader's eyes; his growing fear builds the effect.
  • Character: Roderick Usher: The last male Usher. Hypersensitive, artistic, terrified, decaying like his house. Importance: his mind and the house fall together; he entombs Madeline prematurely.
  • Character: Madeline Usher: Roderick's twin. Silent, ghostly, wasting away. Importance: her return from the tomb causes the climax and both deaths.
  • Character: the house: Almost a character itself (personification): its "eye-like" windows and crack mirror the family's decline. "House" means both the building and the Usher family line.
  • Point of view: First person, told by the narrator, so we only know what he sees and fears.
  • Themes: Madness and fear; decay of a family and a mind; isolation; the link between a place and the people in it.
  • Other important factors: Inserted texts: "The Haunted Palace" (a fair palace ruled by Thought taken over by "evil things", an allegory of Roderick's mind) and the "Mad Trist" (foreshadowing that blurs fiction and reality). Heavy imagery, elevated diction and a controlled mood of dread from start to finish.

"Baker's Bluejay Yarn" (Twain, 1880)

A humorous yarn (tall tale) by Mark Twain, from A Tramp Abroad (1880), anthology pp. 136-141. A miner tells how a bluejay tried to fill a whole house with acorns through a knot-hole.
  • Intro (pp. 136-138): The frame narrator opens: "Animals talk to each other, of course. There can be no question about that; but I suppose there are very few people who can understand them." He knew only one man who could, and knew it "because he told me so himself": Jim Baker, "a middle-aged, simple-hearted miner" who lived for years "in a lonely corner of California, among the woods and mountains", studying "his only neighbors, the beasts and the birds" until he believed he could translate anything they said. Baker says some animals have "only a limited education" and simple words, while others have a big vocabulary, love to talk and enjoy "showing off". After long observation he decided bluejays are the best talkers of all birds and beasts.
  • Baker's case that jays are human (pp. 137-138): "There's more to a bluejay than any other creature." A jay has "more moods, and more different kinds of feelings" and can put any feeling into language: not commonplace talk but "out-and-out book-talk—and bristling with metaphor"; words "just boil out of him". No creature "uses as good grammar as a bluejay". A cat claims good grammar, but get two cats fighting on a shed at night "and you'll hear grammar that will give you the lockjaw"; the noise isn't what's annoying, "it's the sickening grammar they use". When a jay does use bad grammar, he is "as ashamed as a human". A jay is a bird only because "he's got feathers on him, and don't belong to no church"; otherwise he is "just as much a human as you be". "A jay hasn't got any more principle than a Congressman. A jay will lie, a jay will steal, a jay will deceive, a jay will betray; and four times out of five, a jay will go back on his solemnest promise." A jay "can outswear any gentleman in the mines". "A jay can cry, a jay can laugh, a jay can feel shame, a jay can reason and plan and discuss, a jay likes gossip and scandal, a jay has got a sense of humor, a jay knows when he is an ass just as well as you do—maybe better. If a jay ain't human, he better take in his sign, that's all." Then: "Now I'm going to tell you a perfectly true fact about some bluejays."
  • Middle, part 1: the hole (p. 138): Seven years ago "the last man in this region but me moved away", leaving his house empty: "a log house, with a plank roof—just one big room, and no more; no ceiling—nothing between the rafters and the floor." One Sunday morning Baker sits in front of his cabin with his cat, "taking the sun", looking at the blue hills, listening to the lonely leaves and thinking of his home back in the states, which he "hadn't heard from in thirteen years". A bluejay lands on the empty house with an acorn in his mouth and says, "Hello, I reckon I've struck something." The acorn falls out and rolls off the roof, but he doesn't care: he's found a knot-hole in the roof. He cocks his head, shuts one eye and looks in "like a 'possum looking down a jug", winks his wings ("which signifies gratification") and says, "It looks like a hole, it's located like a hole,—blamed if I don't believe it is a hole!" He looks again, "perfectly joyful", and says, "Oh, no, this ain't no fat thing, I reckon! If I ain't in luck!—why it's a perfectly elegant hole!"
  • Middle, part 2: the jay gets obsessed (pp. 138-140): He fetches the acorn, drops it in, and is tilting back "with the heavenliest smile on his face" when he freezes in a listening position; the smile fades "like breath off'n a razor". "Why, I didn't hear it fall!" He looks from every side, walks "round and round the hole", scratches his head with his right foot, and decides it must be "a mighty long hole" but he has business to tend to: "chance it, anyway." He drops a second acorn and tries to get his eye to the hole fast enough to see it land, but he's "too late". A third: "Well, I never struck no such a hole as this before; I'm of the opinion it's a totally new kind of a hole." Now he gets mad: he paces the roof ridge muttering, then "broke loose and cussed himself black in the face". He vows: "Well, you're a long hole, and a deep hole, and a mighty singular hole altogether—but I've started in to fill you, and I'm d—d if I don't fill you, if it takes a hundred years!" For about two and a half hours he hurls acorns in without stopping to look, "one of the most exciting and astonishing spectacles" Baker ever saw, until he can hardly flap his wings and is "sweating like an ice-pitcher". He drops one more ("Now I guess I've got the bulge on you by this time!"), looks, and comes up "pale with rage": "I've shoveled acorns enough in there to keep the family thirty years, and if I can see a sign of one of 'em I wish I may land in a museum with a belly full of sawdust in two minutes!" He crawls up to lean against the chimney and "free his mind", and Baker realizes the swearing he'd heard in the mines "was only just the rudiments".
  • End (pp. 140-141): Another jay passing by hears him "doing his devotions" and asks what's up. The "sufferer" tells the whole story: "yonder's the hole, and if you don't believe me, go and look for yourself." The second jay looks and asks how many he put in. "Not any less than two tons." The second jay can't figure it out either and yells; three more jays come, examine it, make the sufferer tell it again, and give "as many leather-headed opinions about it as an average crowd of humans could have done". They call more and more jays until the region has "a blue flush about it": about five thousand of them, "jawing and disputing and ripping and cussing", each putting his eye to the hole and giving a "more chuckle-headed opinion" than the last. They examine the whole house. The door is half open, and finally "one old jay happened to go and light on it and look in". That knocks the mystery "galley-west in a second": acorns are scattered all over the floor. He whoops: "Come here, everybody; hang'd if this fool hasn't been trying to fill up a house with acorns!" They swoop down "like a blue cloud", and as each one looks in, "the whole absurdity of the contract that that first jay had tackled hit him home and he fell over backwards suffocating with laughter". They roost on the rooftop and trees for an hour and "guffawed over that thing like human beings". Baker: "It ain't any use to tell me a bluejay hasn't got a sense of humor, because I know better. And memory, too." For three summers jays came from all over the United States to look down the hole, and other birds too. They could all see the point "except an owl that come from Nova Scotia to visit the Yo Semite"; he said he couldn't see anything funny in it. "But then he was a good deal disappointed about Yo Semite, too."
  • Order of events (chronology): 1) Narrator introduces Baker. 2) Baker ranks animal talkers and praises jays. 3) Baker's list of human traits. 4) Sunday morning, jay lands with an acorn. 5) Finds the knot-hole; the first acorn rolls off the roof. 6) Drops an acorn in; doesn't hear it fall. 7) Second and third acorns; decides it's a "totally new kind of a hole". 8) Swears and vows to fill it "if it takes a hundred years". 9) Two and a half hours of work; "thirty years" of acorns. 10) Second jay arrives; "two tons". 11) Three more jays, then about 5,000. 12) Old jay looks through the door and solves it. 13) The laughing, an hour of guffawing. 14) Three summers of visitors; the owl.
  • Character: Jim Baker: The storyteller. Middle-aged, "simple-hearted", lonely (no one else in the region; hasn't heard from home in thirteen years), observant, sincere, and prone to exaggeration. He tells an absurd story with complete seriousness and swears it is a "perfectly true fact". Twain characterizes him indirectly through his speech (dialect, grammar mistakes like "I never see a bird take on so", "begun", "come"). Importance: his deadpan telling is where the humor comes from, and his loneliness explains why the jays are his "neighbors".
  • Character: the first jay ("the sufferer"): Proud, stubborn, excitable and foolish. Goes from delight ("perfectly elegant hole") to confusion to rage to exhaustion. Refuses to give up or to investigate the obvious. Importance: the main joke and a mirror of human stubbornness: he works hardest on a job that can never be finished.
  • Character: the second jay and the crowd: The passer-by who asks "How many did you say you put in there?", then three more, then about 5,000. They gossip, argue and give "leather-headed" and "chuckle-headed" opinions without solving anything. Importance: satire of human crowds and experts who talk a lot but don't look at the obvious.
  • Character: the old jay: The one who lands on the half-open door and simply looks inside. Importance: common sense solves in a second what the crowd argued about; age and experience win.
  • Character: the owl from Nova Scotia: The only bird who doesn't find it funny; he was also "disappointed about Yo Semite". Importance: the final punchline, poking fun at a humorless, hard-to-impress outsider.
  • Point of view: Frame narrative. The outer frame is first person, told by Twain's narrator, who introduces Baker with dry irony ("because he told me so himself"). The inner story is Baker's first-person account in Western miner's dialect, reporting the jays' dialogue as if he understood every word. Because Baker is the only witness, he is an unreliable narrator, which is part of the joke.
  • Setting: A lonely, deserted mining region in the woods and mountains of California, near Yosemite; a Sunday morning; Baker's cabin and the empty neighbor's log house (one room, plank roof, no ceiling, door half open).
  • Themes: Human foolishness and stubbornness (refusing to quit a pointless task); pride; the crowd's noisy, useless opinions vs. one sensible look; humor and laughter as part of being "human"; loneliness of the frontier; satire of human behavior (Congressmen with no principle, miners' swearing, gossip).
  • Devices: Anthropomorphism: the jays talk, swear, reason, feel shame, laugh and gossip. Frame story. Dialect and colloquial diction ("ain't", "reckon", "blamed", "struck"). Hyperbole ("two tons", "thirty years", "five thousand", "a hundred years"). Similes ("like a 'possum looking down a jug", "like breath off'n a razor", "sweating like an ice-pitcher", "like a blue cloud"). Verbal irony and satire ("more principle than a Congressman"). Juxtaposition of Baker's serious tone with a ridiculous story. Situational irony (the harder he works, the less he gets done). Characterization through speech (STEAL).
  • Interpretation: The jay and the knot-hole can be read as a metaphor for human life: a creature wastes enormous effort on something it doesn't understand, and others laugh when they see the whole picture. By making the jays act exactly like people, Twain turns the joke on the reader: if the jays are this human, humans are this foolish. The story also shows Twain's rhetorical skill: the humor, dialect and deadpan delivery make Baker (and Twain) likable and believable, even while telling an impossible story.
  • Other important factors: From A Tramp Abroad (1880); Mark Twain, 1835-1910. A yarn is a long, exaggerated tale told as true; a tall tale and beast fable. The anthology's Introduction says Twain mastered the frontier humorous sketch and the fable rather than the short story, and calls "Bluejay Yarn" superb of its kind, linked to the Uncle Remus stories and later Thurber's fables. It bridges Romanticism and Regionalism / local color (setting-specific dialect and customs).

"Young Goodman Brown" (Hawthorne, 1835)

A dark Romantic allegory set in Puritan Salem Village. Hawthorne's own ancestors took part in the Salem witch trials.
  • Intro: At sunset in Salem Village, Puritan Massachusetts, young Goodman Brown is leaving on a one-night "errand". His wife of three months, Faith, with pink ribbons in her cap, begs him to stay: "A lone woman is troubled with such dreams". He refuses and asks if she already doubts him. As he walks off he feels guilty ("what a wretch am I to leave her on such an errand!") and hints that the trip is evil, but promises himself that after this night he will cling to her skirts and follow her to heaven.
  • Middle: Deep in the dark forest he meets a traveler of about fifty, simply dressed and looking a lot like Brown, who says "You are late, Goodman Brown" ("Faith kept me back a while"). The man's staff looks like a great black snake that seems to twist like a living serpent. Brown wants to turn back, saying his family were honest Christians, but the traveler says he knew them well: he helped Brown's grandfather, a constable, whip a Quaker woman through Salem's streets, and gave Brown's father a torch to burn an Indian village in King Philip's War. He adds that deacons, selectmen and the governor's court are his friends. They see Goody Cloyse, the pious old woman who taught Brown his catechism. Brown hides, and she greets the traveler as "your worship", talks of witch ointments and a flying broomstick, and says a young man is to join their meeting tonight; the traveler lends her his staff and she vanishes. Brown sits down and refuses to go further, and the traveler leaves him the staff and disappears. Then Brown hears the minister and Deacon Gookin riding past, talking eagerly about the meeting and a young woman to be taken into communion. Brown looks up to heaven, but a dark cloud passes overhead carrying voices of townspeople, including Faith's, and a pink ribbon flutters down onto a branch. "My Faith is gone!" he cries. "There is no good on earth; and sin is but a name. Come, devil! for to thee is this world given." Laughing madly, he grabs the staff and races through the howling forest to the meeting: a rock altar in a clearing, lit by four burning pines, where the town's most respected people sit beside criminals and Indian priests. A dark figure calls the converts forward. Goody Cloyse and Martha Carrier lead in a veiled woman: Faith. The figure says "Welcome, my children, to the communion of your race" and "Evil is the nature of mankind", promising to reveal everyone's secret sins, and prepares to baptize them from a basin of what looks like blood.
  • End: Brown cries "Faith! Faith! look up to heaven, and resist the wicked one!" Instantly he is alone in the calm, silent forest, the rock cold and a twig dripping cold dew on his cheek. The next morning he walks into Salem Village. The minister blesses him, Deacon Gookin is praying, and Goody Cloyse is teaching a little girl her catechism, which Brown snatches the child away from. Faith runs out joyfully to meet him, pink ribbons and all, but he looks at her sternly and passes without a word. Hawthorne asks whether Brown only fell asleep and "dreamed a wild dream of a witch-meeting", and answers "Be it so if you will". Either way, Brown becomes "a stern, a sad, a darkly meditative, a distrustful, if not a desperate man". He can't stand to hear hymns or sermons, shrinks from Faith at night, and sees hypocrisy everywhere. When he dies an old man, followed to the grave by Faith, children, grandchildren and neighbors, they carve no hopeful verse on his tombstone, "for his dying hour was gloom".
  • Character: Goodman Brown: Protagonist. Young, naive, curious about evil, then bitter. Dynamic: he returns changed forever. Importance: every man who loses his faith in others.
  • Character: Faith: Brown's wife, with her pink ribbons. Importance: both a person and a symbol of his religious faith; "My Faith is gone!" works both ways.
  • Character: the traveler / the devil: Older man resembling Brown, with a serpent staff. Importance: the tempter; his claims make Brown doubt his family and church.
  • Characters: Goody Cloyse, the minister, Deacon Gookin: Respected Puritan leaders seen serving the devil. Importance: they expose the town's hypocrisy (Goody Cloyse and Martha Carrier are real Salem-trial names).
  • Point of view: Third person, limited mostly to Brown, so the reader shares his uncertainty about what is real.
  • Themes: Loss of innocence and faith; hypocrisy of the Puritans; the idea that evil lives in everyone; doubt that poisons a whole life.
  • Other important factors: Allegory and symbols (Faith; the pink ribbons for innocence; the forest for the moral wilderness; the serpent staff for evil); Biblical allusion (Eden, baptism and communion parodies); foreshadowing; deliberate ambiguity. The Introduction says Hawthorne added moral "specific gravity" and describes the story as a journey from light into darkness and back.

"The Custom House" (Hawthorne, 1850)

Hawthorne's long autobiographical essay that introduces The Scarlet Letter. It is nonfiction blended with one big literary trick: Hawthorne claims the novel that follows is a true story he found, not one he invented.
  • Intro: Hawthorne describes his job as Surveyor of the Custom House in Salem, Massachusetts, where he inspects incoming ships' cargo and collects duties for the federal government. He is openly unimpressed with his co-workers: a crew of old, half-asleep political appointees who got their jobs through favoritism and do almost no real work. The most dignified is the elderly "Collector," a Revolutionary War veteran (based on General James Miller) who naps through most of the day but still commands respect.
  • Middle: Poking around in the building's dusty upper story, Hawthorne says he discovers a small bundle left by a former Surveyor named Jonathan Pue: old documents and a faded, moth-eaten scrap of scarlet cloth shaped like a capital letter "A," with gold embroidery. Pue's papers tell the story of a woman named Hester Prynne, who lived generations earlier. When Hawthorne holds the letter to his chest, he claims to feel it burn, "as if the letter were not of red cloth, but red-hot iron." He frames The Scarlet Letter as simply his retelling of Pue's manuscript, not his own invention. He also reflects at length on his Puritan ancestors, especially a stern early judge who persecuted Quakers and a witch-trial judge, and says he feels a kind of inherited guilt that his writing might be paying off.
  • End: Hawthorne loses his customs job when a change in presidential administration brings in a new political appointee to replace him — he jokes that he has been "guillotined," though, unlike a real beheaded victim, he survives it and can write about it afterward. Being pushed out of the practical, unimaginative world of the Custom House is what frees him to finally become a writer and finish the novel.
  • Character: Hawthorne (the narrator): The essay's first-person voice, blending the real Nathaniel Hawthorne with a partly invented persona. Importance: he sets up why the reader should trust the story that follows, and explains his own conflicted identity as both a Custom House officer and a writer.
  • Character: the Collector (based on General James Miller): An elderly, dignified Revolutionary War veteran who nominally runs the Custom House but naps through most of his duties. Importance: represents a fading, honorable past next to Hawthorne's more cynical, satirical view of the other officers.
  • Character: Jonathan Pue: A former Surveyor of the Custom House, long dead, whose old manuscript and the scarlet letter "A" Hawthorne claims to discover. Importance: the whole "found manuscript" conceit depends on him; he is Hawthorne's fictional source for Hester Prynne's story.
  • Character: Hawthorne's Puritan ancestors: Unnamed but based on real ancestors, including a judge who persecuted Quakers and a judge from the Salem witch trials. Importance: Hawthorne says he feels their harshness as a kind of family curse, and sees his writing as a way of answering for it.
  • Point of view: First person. Hawthorne writes as himself, an autobiographical essayist, though he mixes in the invented discovery of Pue's manuscript as if it were also true.
  • Themes: The tension between practical, commercial life (the Custom House) and the imaginative life of an artist; the weight of inherited guilt from Puritan ancestors; using a "found document" to make fiction feel authentic; gentle satire of government patronage jobs.
  • Other important factors: Genre: nonfiction essay, but built around an invented frame device (the discovered manuscript), a technique used to make an invented story feel like a rediscovered true one. Extended metaphor: losing his job as a political "guillotining." The scarlet letter "A" is introduced here as already strange and almost magical (it burns him) before the novel proper even begins. Hawthorne also describes his parlor at night, moonlight mixed with firelight, as the ideal condition for his imagination -- the mindset he needed to finally write the novel.
  • Important lines — Key passages from the essay, in order, for close-reading and presentation prep.
  • Lines 5-25 "It is a little… or his own.": Opening paragraph -- Hawthorne explains why he's writing another autobiographical sketch (he did one before, in "The Old Manse") and sets the ground rules for how personal an author may get without "violating either the reader's rights or his own."
  • Lines 26-55 "It will be…barbed arrows": Explains this sketch's real purpose (to explain how he got the manuscript and prove it's authentic), then describes the actual Custom-House building in Salem, including the American eagle over the door -- federal symbol, warns everyone off, "has no great tenderness."
  • Lines 56-86 "The pavement… Custom-House officers": Describes the mostly-empty, grass-grown Custom-House, the occasional busy morning when ships arrive, and introduces the row of old, half-asleep officers sitting around -- "Custom-House officers."
  • Lines 87-123 "Furthermore…to know": Describes his own office and desk, notes he's since been swept out of the job by political "reform," and begins reflecting on his strange, deep attachment to plain, unglamorous Salem.
  • Lines 124-153 "But the sentiment… with mine": Introduces his Puritan ancestor (persecuted Quakers) and that ancestor's son (a Salem witch-trial judge) -- his sense of inherited family guilt, and his ancestors' imagined scorn at him being "a writer of story-books."
  • Lines 154-181 "Planted… earth": Traces his family's generations in Salem (mostly sea captains) and how deeply the family became rooted -- literally buried -- in the town's soil.
  • Lines 182-214 "On emerging… to Paradise": Explains how he actually got the Surveyor job (a political appointment, "the President's commission"), meets his new staff of very old officers, and jokes that neither door of the Custom-House "opens on the road to Paradise."
  • Lines 215-248 "The greater part…any remedy": Most of his officers are Whigs, but he -- not a political operator -- doesn't fire them; describes their comic incompetence (missing real smuggling, over-fussing small things) with dry irony.
  • Lines 249-302 "Unless people… of the two": He grows genuinely fond of the old officers despite everything, then introduces the ancient, cheerful permanent Inspector -- pure appetite, "no soul, no heart, no mind" beyond his stomach.
  • Lines 303-333 "I used to… axe and handsaw": Continues the Inspector's portrait: he remembers old dinners in vivid detail but nothing else; the "chief tragic event" of his life was a famously tough goose he once tried to carve.
  • Lines 334-364 "But it is… alien weeds": Moves on to the Collector (General Miller) -- a once-great, now-frail soldier, described as hard to read as the ruined fortress of Ticonderoga, "overgrown... with grass and alien weeds."
  • Lines 365-413 "Nevertheless… desk": Gives the Collector real dignity -- a quiet, massive inner strength ("a deep, red glow, as of iron in a furnace") -- and pictures him as an old sword out of place among the office's inkstands and paper-folders.
  • Lines 414-458 "There was one… the change": Gives the Collector's famous battle motto, "I'll try, sir!," then introduces a totally different figure: the sharp, efficient "man of business" clerk who actually keeps the whole Custom-House running.
  • Lines 459-500 "Literature.. now writing": Hawthorne says he stopped caring about literature entirely while working there, valued only as "a tolerably good Surveyor of the Customs," and realizes how little his writing means to the people around him.
  • Lines 501-550 "In the second… skull itself": The key discovery scene: his own name is now famous only as a customs stamp on cargo; poking through attic barrels he finds an old parchment package -- Surveyor Jonathan Pue's actual royal commission and papers (Pue's exhumed remains, he notes, were basically just a skeleton and a well-preserved wig).
  • Lines 551-586 "They were documents… upon the floor": Describes Pue's private antiquarian notes, then the discovery of the scarlet cloth letter "A" itself -- and the famous moment Hawthorne holds it to his chest, feels it burn "as if the letter were not of red cloth, but red-hot iron," and drops it in shock.
  • Lines 587-609 "In the absorbing… the outline": He opens the roll of paper wrapped around the letter and finds Pue's manuscript telling Hester Prynne's story -- the passage where Hawthorne explicitly claims the novel is "authenticated" by a real document, while admitting he invented plenty of the details himself.
  • Lines 610-625 "This incident… I will": The whimsical ghost-of-Surveyor-Pue scene -- Hawthorne imagines Pue's ghost formally commissioning him to write up Hester Prynne's story. "Do this," says the ghost, "and the profit shall be all your own!" Hawthorne answers: "I will!"
  • Lines 626-654 "On Hester… many-hued description": Hawthorne obsessively paces the Custom-House halls thinking about Hester Prynne's story, annoying the old Inspector -- but his imagination stays dead. Says if he'd stayed in the job "through ten Presidencies," the novel never would have been written.
  • Lines 655-685 "If the imaginative… from me": The famous moonlight passage -- moonlight in a familiar room is the ideal condition for a writer's imagination, turning ordinary objects into "things of intellect." None of it worked on him: "An entire class of susceptibilities... was gone from me."
  • Lines 686-716 "It is my belief… of mankind": He reflects he might have written something better by capturing the old sea-captain Inspector's storytelling gift instead. Ends with his verdict on Custom-House work: it doesn't let a man "share in the united effort of mankind."
  • Lines 717-740 "An effect… manly character": The passage on leaning on "the mighty arm of the Republic" -- his theory that public office slowly destroys a man's self-reliance, comparing a government salary to "the Devil's wages."
  • Lines 741-777 "Here was… struck off": His fear of becoming like the old Inspector, then the political guillotine section -- Taylor's election, the Whigs sweeping Democrats from office. "My head was the first that fell!"
  • Lines 778-804 "In short… that fell / The moment… friendly one": Losing his job actually suits him -- he compares it to a man who wanted to commit suicide and got the "good hap to be murdered" instead.
  • Lines 806-828 "Meanwhile… literary man / Now it was… of quiet": Newspapers mock him as Irving's Headless Horseman; freed from the job, he becomes a writer again. Pue's old papers "came into play" -- this is where he actually starts writing the novel. Ends: "For I am in the realm of quiet!"
  • Lines 829-849 "The life of… without me / It may be… town pump": Final farewell to the Custom-House and Salem, naming the real merchants he worked among and saying the town won't miss him. Closes on the ironic Town Pump joke as his one hoped-for legacy.

Background and reference

Everything around the six stories: devices and terms, the anthology intro, Romanticism, Emerson, the American Dream, how to answer analysis questions, the authors cheat sheet, handouts and the periods timeline. Each block starts with its own bold heading.
  • Words and tools (devices, terms and settings) — Every literary device and term for the unit in one place, followed by each story's setting.
  • Anthropomorphism: giving animals or living things human traits (the bluejays).
  • Personification: giving nonliving objects human traits (the House of Usher's "eye-like" windows).
  • Imagery: language that appeals to the senses.
  • Mood / Tone: mood is the feeling created for the reader; tone is the author's attitude toward the subject.
  • Setting: time and place.
  • Characterization: how the author reveals a character. Direct: the narrator tells you. Indirect (STEAL): Speech, Thoughts, Effect on others, Actions, Looks.
  • Round / flat, static / dynamic: round characters are complex, flat have one or two traits; static characters don't change, dynamic ones do (Goodman Brown).
  • Point of view: who narrates: first person, third limited, omniscient. A frame narrative is a story that holds another story.
  • Allusion: an indirect reference to a well-known person, place, text or event (the serpent staff and Eden).
  • Foreshadowing: hints about what comes next (the Mad Trist sounds).
  • Symbolism / Allegory: a symbol stands for a bigger idea; an allegory is a whole story whose characters and events stand for moral or political meanings.
  • Irony: verbal (saying the opposite of what you mean), situational (the opposite of what is expected), dramatic (the reader knows more than the character).
  • Juxtaposition: putting things side by side for effect (Baker's serious tone vs. an absurd story).
  • Metaphor / Simile / Hyperbole: direct comparison; comparison with like or as; exaggeration ("two tons" of acorns).
  • Diction / Syntax: word choice; sentence structure.
  • Dialect: speech showing a region or class (Jim Baker).
  • Ambiguity: an ending or event left open to more than one reading (Brown's dream).
  • Tall tale / Yarn / Fable: exaggerated story told as true; a beast fable uses animals to teach a point.
  • Gothic: horror plus romance in gloomy old buildings (Poe).
  • Spenserian stanza: nine lines, ABABBCBCC, last line longer (Keats).
  • Rhetorical appeals: ethos (credibility), logos (logic), pathos (emotion).
  • SOAPSTone: Speaker, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Subject, Tone.
  • Setting: "Rip Van Winkle": a Dutch village and the Kaatskill (Catskill) Mountains on the Hudson, before and after the American Revolution (about 1770s to 1790s).
  • Setting: "The Eve of St. Agnes": a medieval castle on a freezing St. Agnes' Eve, January 20, during a loud feast; the lovers escape into a storm.
  • Setting: "The Fall of the House of Usher": the decaying Usher mansion beside a black tarn on a dull, soundless autumn day, ending on a stormy night.
  • Setting: "Baker's Bluejay Yarn": a lonely, empty log cabin in the woods of California (near Yosemite), told by a miner.
  • Setting: "Young Goodman Brown": Puritan Salem Village, Massachusetts, in the late 1600s; the dark forest at night, then the village next morning.
  • Introduction to Great American Short Stories (Stegner) — Written by Wallace and Mary Stegner (the anthology is dated 1957); know its main claims and connect them to each story.
  • Poe published "Metzengerstein" in the Philadelphia Saturday Courier on January 14, 1832, using for the first time the technique of the single effect on which the modern short story is built. The short story is called an American invention and specialty; Poe and Hawthorne "together created the short story as a form".
  • Irving vs. Poe: "Rip Van Winkle" is an example of the orderly tale of linked incidents chronologically treated, graceful, humorous and urbane. Poe substituted the concentrated tale of effect: one preconceived impression, action focused on the climactic moment, mood controlled from first to last, improbabilities made plausible by concreteness.
  • The editors call Poe's terror "not of Germany but of the soul" (Poe's own phrase) yet judge his effects closer to "gooseflesh", and say his "madness" is more literary than observed. They name "The Fall of the House of Usher" Poe at his best, with its doomed mansion and the "deadly inverted image of the fateful house reflected in the tarn". Poe also fathered the tale of ratiocination, the parent of the detective story.
  • Hawthorne added moral or intellectual weight, Henry Seidel Canby's "specific gravity". The typical American story asks to be taken seriously as a reflection of life, manners, morals or national character. Hawthorne's contribution is the story that probes a situation rather than linking incidents or contriving effects.
  • Usher and "Young Goodman Brown" are both built on a journey and a return, moving from light into darkness and back to light. Poe's darkness is the theatrical dark of the horror tale; Hawthorne's is the darkness of the soul, the forest of sin. Brown's initiation shows "conviction that virtue is a mask and all men are guilty".
  • Twain: the Introduction says he never wrote a good short story; his mastered forms were the humorous frontier sketch (his essay "How to Tell a Story") and the fable. "Bluejay Yarn" is not a short story by Matthews' definition but superb in its kind, linked to Harris's Uncle Remus stories and, later, Thurber's fables. The beast story is among the oldest forms of fiction.
  • Irving's "Rip Van Winkle" and "The Legend of Sleepy Hollow" are named as his best and best known. The editors' criteria for selection were the excellence of each story in itself and its representativeness in the story's development in the United States.
  • Later sections trace local color (Bret Harte's "The Luck of Roaring Camp", 1868), Henry James, Stephen Crane, Sherwood Anderson, Hemingway and Faulkner. Only the material connected to your six texts is likely to be tested, but the local-color and beast-fable paragraphs support Twain.
  • Romanticism and Washington Irving — Romanticism is the frame for the whole unit. Irving's packet content is on the exam, so know the movement's vocabulary as well as the man.
  • Romanticism (late 18th to mid 19th century, per the Britannica handout) rejects the order, calm, harmony, balance and rationality of Classicism and late-18th-century Neoclassicism, and reacts against the Enlightenment, rationalism and physical materialism.
  • Romanticism emphasizes the individual, the subjective, the irrational, the imaginative, the emotional and the transcendental; it loves nature, exalts emotion over reason and the senses over intellect, and has a predilection for the exotic, mysterious, occult, monstrous and even satanic.
  • Classicism and Neoclassicism prized order, reason and restraint; Rationalism trusted reason above feeling. Romantic writers push back with intuition, mystery and the sublime.
  • Washington Irving (1783-1859) is often called the first American author to win international fame. "Rip Van Winkle" appeared in The Sketch Book (1819-20) under the pen name Geoffrey Crayon.
  • Irving frames the tale as papers left by the fictional historian Diedrich Knickerbocker, a playful fake-history frame (historiography: how history gets written).
  • Romantic markers to spot in the stories: dreamlike or supernatural events, wild nature, nostalgia for the past, a lone individual against society, and strong emotion.
  • Packet vocabulary to know: Classicism, Neoclassicism, Enlightenment, Rationalism, Transcendental, exaltation, predilection, occult, satanic, historiography.
  • English Romanticism began in the 1790s with Wordsworth and Coleridge's Lyrical Ballads; Wordsworth's 1800 Preface called poetry "the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings". Blake was a third early poet; Keats, Byron and Shelley are the later high point; Scott is credited with the historical novel.
  • Gothic fiction combines horror and romance. Its origin is credited to Horace Walpole's The Castle of Otranto (1764, subtitled "A Gothic Story"); later examples include Mary Shelley's Frankenstein, Poe, and Stoker's Dracula. "Gothic" refers to the pseudo-medieval buildings where many of the stories take place.
  • Emerson and Transcendentalism — The Emerson handout (Lesson #5) is an excerpt from "The Transcendentalist". Bullet-point summary of the argument is on your notes.
  • Ralph Waldo Emerson (1803-1882) was the leading voice of American Transcendentalism, a Romantic movement that trusts intuition, individual conscience and nature over institutions and tradition.
  • From the excerpt: the materialist "takes his departure from the external world" and esteems a man as one product of it, respecting society, government, social art, luxury and "every mass". The idealist "takes his departure from his consciousness, and reckons the world an appearance".
  • The idealist's measure is metaphysical: the rank things take in his consciousness, not their size or appearance. "Mind is the only reality, of which men and all other natures are better or worse reflectors." Nature, literature and history are "subjective phenomena".
  • The idealist does not respect government, church, charities, property or the arts for themselves, only as symbols of the laws of being, and sees facts flowing outward "from an invisible, unsounded centre in himself". His thought "is the Universe".
  • Use the handout: quote how Emerson defines each side, then say what he means and how you know from his diction.
  • Connect to the unit: Rip resists social duties (individual vs. society); Goodman Brown loses faith in his community; Poe's Roderick lives inside his own mind; Keats prizes imagination.
  • Emerson is nonfiction (an essay/lecture), so you can also discuss purpose, audience, tone and ethos, logos and pathos in it.
  • The American Dream (James Truslow Adams) — How can we critically interpret the American Dream? Start with James Truslow Adams' definition.
  • James Truslow Adams popularized the phrase in The Epic of America (1931): a dream of a land where life is better, richer and fuller for everyone, with opportunity for each person according to ability or achievement.
  • Adams stresses that it is not just cars and high wages but a social order where each person can reach their fullest stature. Notice how diction like "better and richer and fuller" makes it about more than money.
  • Handout details: Adams wrote it in The Epic of America (1931, pp. 214-215); the dream is "for the European upper classes" hard to interpret, many Americans had "grown weary and mistrustful of it", and each person should be "recognized by others for what they are, regardless of the fortuitous circumstances of birth or position".
  • The handout also quotes the Declaration of Independence (life, Liberty and the Pursuit of Happiness), asks whether homesteaders, immigrants and World War II veterans pursued the same dream, and notes critics who say it became material prosperity or is out of reach for the working poor. Thomas Wolfe is quoted on "his shining, golden opportunity".
  • Ask of each story: whose version of the dream is shown, and who is left out or disillusioned?
  • Rip Van Winkle: freedom from duty and the birth of a new nation. Goodman Brown: the Puritan ideal of a godly community, undone by hypocrisy. Baker's yarn: folksy Western tall tale that pokes fun at human effort.
  • Definition is a moving target: consider how the dream has evolved and what it is not.

  • Irving's life and impact (Lesson #1 handout) — The Irving handout is a Wikipedia biography with discussion questions. Expect accuracy-of-information and historical-context questions from it.
  • Washington Irving (April 3, 1783 - November 28, 1859) was born in Manhattan the same week New Yorkers learned of the British ceasefire that ended the Revolution. His mother named him for George Washington, whom he met at age six.
  • Boyhood trips up the Hudson, including the Catskill region (the setting of "Rip Van Winkle"), shaped him: he wrote that the Kaatskill Mountains had "the most witching effect on my boyish imagination". Tarrytown gave him Sleepy Hollow's Dutch customs and ghost stories.
  • Early writing: letters to the Morning Chronicle (1802) as Jonathan Oldstyle; the magazine Salmagundi (1807), which nicknamed New York "Gotham"; then A History of New-York by Diedrich Knickerbocker (1809), promoted with a hoax about the missing historian.
  • "Rip Van Winkle" is in The Sketch Book of Geoffrey Crayon, Gent. Irving wrote it overnight in Birmingham, England, staying with his sister Sarah and her husband Henry van Wart. He lived abroad for 17 years (1815-1832).
  • He served as U.S. Minister to Spain (1842-1846), lived at Sunnyside, and is buried in Sleepy Hollow cemetery (Dec 1, 1859). He encouraged writers such as Hawthorne, Melville, Longfellow and Poe, though Poe called him "much over-rated".
  • Legacy: called the first American man of letters and the first to earn a living by his pen; he advocated copyright protection, popularized "Gotham", is credited with the phrase "the almighty dollar", and helped shape the American image of St. Nicholas and Christmas. His Columbus biography also spread the flat-Earth myth.
  • Literary periods timeline (Literary Devices packet) — The last page of your Literary Terms packet gives a timeline and movement definitions. Historical-context questions can ask you to place a work in its period.
  • Puritanism 1620-1770s (plain style; obedience to God; journals, sermons, poems). Neoclassic 1770s to early 1800s (rationalism, the Age of Reason).
  • Romanticism early 1800s-1870s: a revolt against Rationalism. Realism 1850s-early 1900s. Regionalism 1884-early 1900s. Naturalism late 1800s-mid 1900s. Modernism 1920s-1945. Post-Modernism 1945 on.
  • Transcendentalism: a 19th-century movement in the Romantic tradition holding that every individual can reach ultimate truths through spiritual intuition, which transcends reason and sensory experience.
  • Rationalism: truth reached by reason instead of the authority of the past, the Church or an institution (also called Neoclassicism or the Age of Reason).

Unit 1: The Rhetorical Situation

SOAPSTone
A framework for analyzing a text's rhetorical situation: Speaker, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Subject, Tone.
Exigence
The specific problem, need, or urgency that makes a piece of writing necessary at that moment.
Purpose
What the writer wants the audience to think, feel, or do — always phrased as 'to ___.'
Constraints
Limits on a writer, such as word count, audience expectations, or what's appropriate for the occasion.
Kairos
The opportune moment: fitting the argument to the right time, audience, and circumstances.
Rhetorical triangle
The relationship among speaker (ethos), audience (pathos), and subject/message (logos).
Purpose (phrasing)
Always a verb phrase: 'to convince [specific audience] that/to ___.'
Audience analysis
Identifying the specific readers' values, assumptions, and knowledge so appeals can be aimed at them.

Unit 2: Rhetorical Appeals & Logical Fallacies

Ethos
An appeal to credibility and character — why the audience should trust the speaker.
Pathos
An appeal to emotion, designed to make the audience feel something that moves them toward the writer's position.
Logos
An appeal to logic and reason, using facts, statistics, and clear chains of reasoning.
Ad hominem
A logical fallacy that attacks the person making an argument instead of the argument itself.
Straw man
A logical fallacy that misrepresents an opponent's argument as weaker than it really is, then attacks that weak version.
False dilemma
A logical fallacy presenting only two options when more actually exist.
Ethos / pathos / logos
Appeals to the speaker's credibility, the audience's emotions, and logical reasoning, respectively.
False dilemma (either/or)
Presenting only two options when more exist.
Post hoc, ergo propter hoc
Assuming that because one event followed another, the first caused the second.
Hasty generalization
Drawing a broad conclusion from too little or unrepresentative evidence.
Appeal to authority
Citing an authority as support; a fallacy when the authority is irrelevant or offered as the sole proof.
Deductive vs. inductive reasoning
Deductive: general principle to specific conclusion. Inductive: specific cases to a general (provisional) claim.
Persona
The character a writer projects through language, tone, and stance - not the same as the real author.

Unit 3: Rhetorical Devices & Figurative Language

Anaphora
Repetition of a word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses or sentences.
Antithesis
Placing two contrasting ideas in a parallel grammatical structure.
Juxtaposition
Putting two things side by side for effect, such as Baker's earnest tone against a ridiculous tale.
Metaphor
A direct comparison between two unlike things, without using 'like' or 'as.'
Rhetorical question
A question asked for effect rather than to get a literal answer, leading the reader to a conclusion.
Epistrophe
Repetition of a word or phrase at the end of successive clauses.
Chiasmus
A reversal of grammatical structure in successive phrases ('ask not what... what you can do...').
Asyndeton
Omitting conjunctions between items in a list, speeding the pace ('I came, I saw, I conquered').
Polysyndeton
Repeating conjunctions between items ('and... and... and'), slowing the pace and adding weight.
Metaphor vs. simile
A metaphor states a comparison directly; a simile uses 'like' or 'as.'
Extended metaphor
A single comparison developed across multiple sentences or an entire passage.
Allusion
A brief, indirect reference to a person, place, text or idea of historical, cultural, literary or political significance; relies on the reader's knowledge.
Litotes
Understatement by negating the opposite ('not a small problem'), often for dry emphasis.
Verbal irony
Saying the opposite of what is meant, expecting the audience to catch the gap; sarcasm is a pointed form.

Unit 4: Style: Diction, Syntax & Tone

Denotation
A word's literal, dictionary definition.
Connotation
The emotional association attached to a word, beyond its literal meaning.
Syntax
How a writer arranges words and constructs sentences — a rhetorical choice in itself.
Tone
The writer's attitude toward the subject, revealed through diction and syntax; it can shift within a passage.
Diction
A writer's word choice, including the connotations those words carry.
Denotation vs. connotation
Denotation is the literal meaning; connotation is the emotional or cultural association.
Tonal shift
A change in attitude within a text, often marked by 'but,' a paragraph break, or a change in sentence length; it signals a turn in the argument.
Periodic sentence
Withholds the main clause until the end, building suspense and emphasis.
Cumulative (loose) sentence
States the main clause first, then adds modifying phrases and detail.
Selection of detail
What a writer chooses to include or omit; itself a form of argument.
Imagery
Language that appeals to the senses, making an abstraction vivid and felt.

Unit 5: Claims, Evidence & Commentary

Claim of value
An argument that something is good, bad, right, or wrong.
Commentary
Analysis that explains WHY evidence matters and HOW it supports the argument — different from summary.
Line of reasoning
The logical thread connecting a thesis through each piece of evidence to a coherent overall argument.
Claim types
Fact (is it true?), value (is it good?), and policy (what should we do?).
Summary vs. commentary
Summary restates what the evidence says; commentary explains HOW it supports the claim.
Qualifying a claim
Narrowing a claim with words like 'often,' 'in most cases,' or a condition, so it fits the evidence.
Relevance vs. sufficiency
Relevant evidence bears on the exact claim; sufficient evidence is enough, and varied enough, to be credible.

Unit 6: Organization, Structure & Counterargument

Thesis
A specific, arguable claim that someone could reasonably disagree with — the core of an essay's argument.
Concession
Acknowledging a valid point on the opposing side of an argument before pivoting back to your own position.
Rebuttal
Directly refuting an opposing point by explaining why it's flawed or less compelling.
Effective thesis
Specific and arguable; it previews a line of reasoning rather than stating an obvious truth.
Given-new principle
Begin a sentence with familiar information and end with the new point, to create flow.
Concede-then-counter
Grant an opposing point, then explain why it does not defeat the thesis ('While X is true, it does not follow that Y').
Transitions
Words that must name the logical relationship between ideas (however, therefore, for instance), not just decorate.
Modes of arrangement
Chronological, cause-effect, problem-solution, comparison-contrast, and climactic (order of importance).
Coherence
The quality of prose that guides the reader from idea to idea without gaps; aided by repeated key terms and clear referents.

Unit 7: The Synthesis Essay

Synthesis essay
An essay that develops the writer's own argument using at least three provided sources as support.
Source-by-source trap
Organizing a synthesis essay by summarizing each source in turn instead of by the writer's own claims — a major weakness.
Synthesis essay requirement
Build an argument using at least three of the provided sources as evidence, cited clearly.
The summary trap
Walking through sources one by one instead of organizing body paragraphs around your own claims.
Signal phrase vs. parenthetical citation
A signal phrase names the source in the sentence ('Source B argues'); a parenthetical puts it at the end ((Source B)).
Defensible thesis
A clear, arguable, often qualified position that the sources can be used to support.
Using disagreeing sources
Use one source to complicate or answer another within a paragraph built on your claim.

Unit 8: The Rhetorical Analysis Essay

Rhetorical analysis essay
An essay explaining HOW a writer builds their argument and its effect on the audience, not whether the reader agrees.
Device-to-effect move
The core rhetorical analysis skill: identify a specific choice, then explain its effect on the audience.
Rhetorical analysis focus
Explain HOW a writer argues and to what effect - not whether you agree with the argument.
Weak vs. strong analysis thesis
Weak: lists devices ('uses ethos, pathos, logos'). Strong: names what the writer does and to what end.
Device-dropping
Naming a strategy and quoting a line without explaining its effect on the audience and purpose.
Depth over breadth
On a timed essay, analyze 2-3 strategies thoroughly rather than listing many.
Claim-evidence-analysis
The core structure of a body paragraph: identify a choice, quote the text, explain the effect.

Unit 9: The Argument Essay

Argument essay
An essay defending the writer's own position on an issue using the writer's own knowledge, with no provided sources.
Sophistication
Demonstrating nuance, qualification, or broader implications in an argument — beyond a simple thesis-evidence structure.
Refutation vs. concession

Rhetorical Situation

Purpose is a verb, not a noun
Never write "the purpose is technology" — write "to warn readers about..." If it's not phrased as "to ___," it isn't purpose yet.
Subject ≠ Purpose
Subject is WHAT it's about. Purpose is what the writer wants to happen because of it. Two totally different questions, always answer both.

Appeals & Fallacies

E-P-L: Ethics, Passion, Logic
Ethos=credibility (think "ethics"). Pathos=emotion (think "passion" or "sympathy"). Logos=logic (think "logical").
Name it, then explain it
"This is pathos" earns nothing alone. Always finish the sentence: "...which makes the audience feel ___, moving them to ___."
Attack the argument, not the arguer
If the criticism is about the PERSON instead of their actual claim, it's probably ad hominem, not real logos.

Devices

Anaphora starts, epistrophe ends
Anaphora repeats at the BEGINNING of clauses. Epistrophe repeats at the END. Both start with vowels near the front of the alphabet — 'a' comes before 'e,' just like 'anaphora' comes at the start.
Simile has the 'like/as' sibling
'Simile' sounds like 'similar' — it's a comparison, and it always keeps the word 'like' or 'as' as a visible sibling.

Style

Short = sharp, long = layered
Short sentences hit hard and fast. Long sentences build, accumulate, and layer ideas on top of each other.
Ban vague tone words
If your tone word could describe a hundred different passages ('good,' 'bad,' 'interesting'), it's too vague — get specific.

Argument Building

"So what?" after every quote
Every time you cite evidence, silently ask yourself "so what?" — the answer to that question IS your commentary.
Concede, then pivot
"Sure, X has a point, BUT..." is the whole shape of a concession-rebuttal — acknowledge fairly, then turn back to your claim.

The Three Essays

Synthesis = your argument + their sources
Never summarize sources in order. Build YOUR claims first, then drop sources in as support — like citations in a research paper, not a book report.
Rhetorical analysis = HOW, not IF
You are never arguing whether the writer is right. You are only explaining how they built their case and why it works.
Argument = your evidence, no packet
No sources are given — dig into history, current events, or your own experience for real, specific proof.

Grammar

A comma alone can't join two sentences
Two complete sentences need a period, a semicolon, or a comma PLUS a conjunction (and/but/so) — a lone comma creates a splice.
Match the list's grammar
If one item in a series ends in '-ing,' they all should. Mismatched forms in a list is a parallel structure error.
