Unit 1: Writer's Toolkit & Habits

Freewriting
Writing continuously without stopping to edit or judge, used to bypass the inner critic and generate raw material.
Show, don't tell
A craft principle favoring concrete sensory detail and action over abstract statements of emotion or meaning, so readers infer feeling themselves.
In medias res
Latin for 'into the middle of things'; beginning a narrative amid action rather than at chronological start, then filling in context later.
Clustering (mind-mapping)
An idea-generation technique that starts with a core word or image and radiates outward with associations to find unexpected connections.
Morning pages
Julia Cameron's technique of writing three unedited pages each morning to clear mental clutter before other creative work.
Shitty first draft
Anne Lamott's term (from 'Bird by Bird') for the permission to write a rough, imperfect first draft, since polishing happens in revision.
Sensory detail
Concrete description engaging sight, sound, smell, touch, or taste that grounds abstract ideas and emotions in physical experience.
Throat-clearing opening
An opening that delays the real story with unnecessary setup, such as backstory dumps or weather description, before any tension begins.
Writer's notebook
A running collection of fragments, overheard dialogue, and striking images gathered for use in future writing.
Constraint-based generation
An idea-generation exercise that imposes a limitation (like one setting or dialogue-only) to force inventive creative solutions.

Unit 2: Story Elements Deep Dive

Freytag's Pyramid
A model of dramatic structure mapping a story's tension through exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and resolution.
Inciting incident
The event that disrupts a story's status quo and sets the central conflict and plot in motion.
Story vs. plot (Forster)
E.M. Forster's distinction: story is a chronological sequence of events, while plot links events through cause and effect.
Denouement
The final resolution of a story's remaining plot threads after the climax and falling action.
Motif
A recurring image, phrase, or object that accumulates meaning through repetition across a text.
Symbol
An object, image, or action that represents something beyond its literal meaning, such as the green light in 'The Great Gatsby.'
Theme
The underlying idea or insight about life a story explores implicitly through action and consequence, not stated as a direct moral.
Scene vs. summary
Scene dramatizes action in near-real time; summary compresses time to cover longer spans quickly, controlling pacing.
Person vs. self conflict
An internal conflict type involving a character's competing desires or moral struggle.
Regionalism
A literary approach where a specific place (like Faulkner's Yoknapatawpha County) functions almost as a character, deeply shaping story and theme.

Unit 3: Character Craft

Want vs. need
Want is a character's conscious external goal; need is the deeper internal truth they must reach, often unrecognized at first. The gap drives the arc.
Direct characterization
Revealing a character's trait through explicit statement by the narrator or another character, rather than through action or inference.
Indirect characterization
Revealing character through speech, thoughts, actions, appearance, and others' reactions, letting readers infer traits (often abbreviated STEAL).
Round character
Forster's term for a complex, multidimensional character capable of surprising the reader in a believable way.
Flat character
Forster's term for a simple character built around a single trait or function, typically used for minor roles.
Dynamic character
A character who undergoes significant internal change over the course of a story.
Static character
A character who remains fundamentally unchanged throughout a story.
Character's ghost/wound
A past traumatic event that shapes a character's core misbelief, fear, or flaw at the story's outset.
Foil
A character whose contrasting traits highlight another character's qualities by comparison; not necessarily a villain.
Flat/testing arc
An arc in which a character's already-correct worldview is tested and ultimately confirmed, often changing the world or people around them instead.

Unit 4: Dialogue That Works

Subtext
The unspoken meaning beneath a line of dialogue — what a character truly wants, fears, or means, distinct from their literal words.
On-the-nose dialogue
Dialogue in which characters state exactly what they feel or mean, often resulting in flat, unrealistic exchanges.
Action beat
A physical action used instead of or alongside a dialogue tag to attribute speech while adding characterization and controlling pace.
Idiolect
An individual character's unique pattern of speech, including word choice, sentence length, and syntax, distinct from general dialect.
Iceberg theory
Hemingway's principle that the most powerful meaning in a text stays beneath the surface, largely unstated.
Talking heads syndrome
A weakness in dialogue scenes where exchanges have no physical action, setting, or stakes grounding them, feeling disembodied.
Code-switching
A character shifting speech patterns between different social contexts, often revealing social awareness or power dynamics.
Said-bookism
The overuse of ornate dialogue tags (exclaimed, expostulated) instead of the largely invisible 'said,' often considered weaker craft.
New speaker, new paragraph
The standard formatting convention that begins a new paragraph whenever the speaker in a dialogue exchange changes.
Register
The level of formality or casualness in a character's speech, which can shift to reveal relationship dynamics or social context.

Unit 5: Point of View Choices

First person narration
Narration through an 'I' who is a character in the story, limited to what that character personally perceives, thinks, or is told.
Second person narration
Narration that addresses the protagonist as 'you,' implicating the reader directly and creating an unusual closeness or distance.
Third person limited
Narration using 'he/she/they' restricted to one character's thoughts and perceptions per scene, like a camera on their shoulder.
Third person omniscient
A narrator with access to any character's thoughts and events across time and place, often able to comment from outside the story.
Free indirect discourse
A technique blending the narrator's voice with a character's inner thoughts, without quotation marks or 'he thought.'
Unreliable narrator
A narrator whose account cannot be fully trusted, due to bias, limited knowledge, mental state, or deliberate deception.
Head-hopping
An uncontrolled shift between multiple characters' thoughts within a single scene or paragraph, generally considered a POV error.
Objective (camera-eye) POV
A third-person variant that reports only external action and dialogue, withholding access to any character's interior thoughts.
Narrative distance
How close or far the narration sits from a character's immediate perception — close narration stays inside the moment; distant narration summarizes.
Dramatic irony (via POV)
A gap between what the reader knows and what a character knows, created and controlled through the writer's choice of point of view.

Unit 6: Poetry Forms & Imagery

Sonnet
A 14-line poem, traditionally in iambic pentameter, structured to set up and resolve a single idea via a volta, or turn.
Volta
The turn or pivot point in a sonnet where the argument, tone, or perspective shifts — before the couplet in Shakespearean form, between octave and sestet in Petrarchan.
Iambic pentameter
A meter of five iambic feet (unstressed-stressed) per line, roughly ten syllables, common in traditional sonnets.
Haiku
A short Japanese-origin poem, traditionally three lines of 5-7-5 syllables, capturing a single sensory moment without explicit commentary.
Kigo
A season word in a traditional haiku that anchors the poem in a specific time of year.
Free verse
Poetry without fixed meter or rhyme scheme that uses line breaks, rhythm, and white space as deliberate structural choices.
Enjambment
A sentence or phrase that runs past a line break without punctuation, creating momentum and temporary ambiguity.
Imagery
Language that appeals to the senses — sight, sound, touch, taste, smell — to make abstract ideas or emotions concrete.
Simile
A figurative comparison that signals itself explicitly using 'like' or 'as.'
Metaphor
A figurative comparison that asserts one thing directly is another, without 'like' or 'as.'

Unit 7: Revision as Rewriting

Revision
The big-picture stage of improving a draft — structure, plot logic, character arcs, pacing, and theme — literally 're-seeing' the work.
Editing
The paragraph- and sentence-level stage of improving a draft: clarity, word choice, sentence rhythm, and consistency.
Proofreading
The final pass for surface errors — spelling, grammar, punctuation, and typos — done after revision and editing.
Kill your darlings
The principle of cutting a beloved line or scene that serves the writer's ego more than the story.
Structural edit
A large-scale revision addressing scene order, pacing, plot logic, and whether the story's shape serves its meaning.
Line edit
A sentence- and paragraph-level revision refining clarity, rhythm, word choice, and voice once structure is sound.
Reverse outlining
Summarizing an existing draft's scenes after the fact to reveal structural problems invisible when reading line by line.
Reading aloud (revision technique)
Reading a draft out loud to surface awkward rhythm, unnatural dialogue, and run-on sentences the eye tends to skim past.
Distance (in revision)
The psychological gap gained by setting a draft aside before revising, letting a writer see it more like a reader than its author.
Sagging middle
A pacing problem where a story's middle section loses momentum, typically diagnosed and fixed at the structural level.

Unit 8: Workshop: Giving & Taking Feedback

Feedback sandwich
A framework that opens with genuine strengths, moves to specific concerns, and closes with encouragement or a forward-looking note.
Stop, start, continue
A feedback framework organizing comments into what isn't working, what's missing, and what's working well.
Descriptive feedback
Feedback that reports a reader's experience of the text (e.g. 'I got confused here') without dictating a specific fix.
Prescriptive feedback
Feedback that tells the writer exactly what change to make, rather than describing the reader's experience.
The writer doesn't speak (rule)
A common workshop norm where the writer listens silently during critique rather than defending or explaining the work.
Defensiveness (in critique)
A writer's impulse to explain or justify choices during feedback, which can prevent hearing what a reader actually experienced.
Pattern across readers
Feedback that recurs across multiple readers, a more reliable signal of a real issue than one reader's isolated reaction.
Workshop confidentiality
The norm that unpublished, vulnerable workshop drafts shouldn't be shared or discussed outside the group.
Textually grounded feedback
Feedback that points to specific lines, moments, or passages rather than making unsupported global claims about a piece.
Writer's final authority
The principle that the writer, not the workshop group, decides which feedback to act on and how to revise.

Unit 1: Writer's Toolkit & Habits

Freewriting and Generative Drafting
Freewriting means writing continuously without stopping to edit, judge, or plan, in order to bypass the inner critic and generate raw material.
Idea Generation Techniques
Writers use deliberate techniques to move from a blank page to a workable idea rather than waiting for inspiration.
Show, Don't Tell
'Show, don't tell' means rendering experience through concrete sensory detail and action so readers infer emotion and meaning themselves, rather than being told what to feel.
Sensory Detail and Concrete Imagery
Concrete, specific sensory details ground abstract ideas and emotions in a reader's experience, making prose vivid and credible.
Writing Routines and Revision Mindset
Professional writers rely on consistent habits — a regular schedule, low-stakes drafting, and a willingness to revise extensively — more than on waiting for inspiration.
Point of Entry: Starting a Piece
How and where a piece begins shapes reader engagement immediately, so writers choose an opening strategy deliberately rather than starting wherever chronology happens to begin.
Key fact
Freewriting generates raw material by silencing the internal editor; revision is a separate, later stage
Key fact
'Show, don't tell' works through specific sensory and concrete detail, not through piling on adjectives
Key fact
In medias res openings start amid action and delay exposition to hook the reader immediately
Key fact
Consistent writing habits (regular time blocks, low-stakes drafts) produce more finished work than waiting for inspiration

Unit 2: Story Elements Deep Dive

Plot Structure: Freytag's Pyramid and Beyond
Plot is the sequence of causally related events; classical models like Freytag's Pyramid describe a rising and falling arc of tension.
Conflict: Types and Function
Conflict is the engine of narrative tension, arising from opposing forces that a character must confront.
Setting as Active Element
Setting is more than backdrop; skilled writers use place and time to shape mood, reveal character, and drive plot.
Theme and Thematic Statement
Theme is the underlying idea or insight about life that a story explores, usually emerging through action and consequence rather than direct statement.
Motif and Symbol
A motif is a recurring element (image, phrase, or object) that develops meaning through repetition; a symbol is an object or image that represents something beyond its literal meaning.
Pacing and Scene vs. Summary
Pacing controls how quickly or slowly a reader experiences story time, achieved by alternating dramatized scenes with summarized narration.
Key fact
Freytag's Pyramid maps a story's tension as exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and resolution
Key fact
The seven core conflict types include person vs. person, self, nature, society, fate, technology, and the supernatural
Key fact
Theme is inferred through action, symbol, and consequence, not stated as an explicit moral
Key fact
Pacing is controlled by alternating dramatized scene (real-time detail) with summary (compressed time)

Unit 3: Character Craft

Want vs. Need: Character Motivation
Compelling characters pursue a conscious external 'want' while often lacking awareness of a deeper internal 'need,' and the gap between the two drives character arc.
Characterization: Direct and Indirect
Characterization is the set of techniques a writer uses to reveal who a character is, ranging from direct authorial statement to indirect inference through action.
Round vs. Flat, Dynamic vs. Static
E.M. Forster's terms distinguish characters by complexity (round vs. flat) while dynamic vs. static describes whether a character changes over the story.
The Character Arc
A character arc is the internal transformation a character undergoes across a story, typically moving from a flawed worldview or wound toward change (positive, negative, or flat).
Backstory and Its Deployment
Backstory is a character's history prior to the story's start; it must inform present behavior without overwhelming the narrative.
Antagonists and Foils
An antagonist opposes the protagonist's goals, while a foil is a character whose contrasting traits highlight another character's qualities by comparison.
Key fact
The gap between a character's conscious 'want' and unconscious 'need' generates the engine of a character arc
Key fact
Indirect characterization (through action, speech, and thought) is generally more sophisticated than direct authorial statement
Key fact
Forster's round/flat axis measures complexity; dynamic/static measures whether a character changes
Key fact
The strongest antagonists have understandable motivations and often mirror the protagonist's own flaw

Unit 4: Dialogue That Works

Dialogue vs. Real Speech
Good fictional dialogue is a stylized, compressed version of real speech, not a verbatim transcript — it cuts filler while preserving the illusion of natural talk.
Dialogue Tags and Beats
Dialogue tags (he said, she asked) attribute speech to a speaker, while action beats use physical action instead of or alongside tags to ground a line in the scene.
Subtext in Dialogue
Subtext is the unspoken meaning beneath a line of dialogue — what a character truly wants, fears, or means, which differs from their literal words.
Dialect, Voice, and Idiolect
Idiolect is an individual's unique pattern of speech; writers use vocabulary, syntax, and rhythm — more than phonetic spelling — to render distinct character voices.
Dialogue as Conflict Engine
The strongest dialogue scenes contain some form of conflict or disagreement, even subtle, rather than two characters simply agreeing pleasantly.
Formatting and Punctuation Conventions
Standard manuscript conventions for dialogue (punctuation placement, paragraphing, and quotation style) keep exchanges clear and professional.
Key fact
'Said' is the least distracting dialogue tag and is generally preferred over ornate alternatives
Key fact
Subtext — what characters mean but don't say outright — creates the tension that keeps dialogue from feeling flat
Key fact
Distinct idiolect (word choice, rhythm, syntax) differentiates character voices more effectively than phonetic misspelling
Key fact
New speaker, new paragraph is the standard dialogue formatting convention in English-language prose

Unit 5: Point of View Choices

First Person Narration
First person narration tells the story through an 'I' who is a character inside the events, giving readers direct access to one mind but only one.
Second Person Narration
Second person narration addresses the protagonist as 'you,' implicating the reader directly in the action and creating an unusual, often disorienting closeness.
Third Person Limited
Third person limited narrates with 'he/she/they' but restricts access to a single character's thoughts and perceptions per scene, like a camera mounted on one person's shoulder.
Third Person Omniscient
Third person omniscient uses a narrator who can access any character's thoughts, move freely through time and place, and comment on events from outside the story.
Reliability and the Unreliable Narrator
An unreliable narrator's account of events cannot be fully trusted, whether from limited knowledge, self-deception, mental state, age, or deliberate deception.
Choosing and Committing to a POV
POV choice shapes what a story can reveal, when, and to whom — it should be a deliberate craft decision tied to the story's central tension, not a default.
Key fact
First person and third-limited restrict readers to one consciousness at a time; omniscient and objective can range freely across characters
Key fact
Free indirect discourse merges narrator and character voice without dialogue tags, a hallmark of close third person
Key fact
An unreliable narrator's gaps and contradictions are deliberate craft signals, not writing errors
Key fact
Head-hopping — uncontrolled shifts between characters' thoughts within a scene — differs from deliberate, signaled omniscience

Unit 6: Poetry Forms & Imagery

The Sonnet
A sonnet is a 14-line poem, traditionally in iambic pentameter, that uses a fixed structure to set up and resolve a single idea, image, or emotional turn.
The Haiku
A haiku is a short Japanese-origin poem, traditionally three lines of 5-7-5 syllables, that captures a single moment of observation, often from nature, without explicit commentary.
Free Verse
Free verse is poetry that does not follow a fixed meter or rhyme scheme, instead using line breaks, rhythm, and white space as deliberate structural choices.
Imagery
Imagery is language that appeals to the senses — sight, sound, touch, taste, smell — to make abstract ideas or emotions concrete and vivid for the reader.
Metaphor and Simile
Metaphor and simile are figurative comparisons that describe one thing in terms of another to reveal a resemblance, but simile signals the comparison explicitly while metaphor asserts it directly.
Sound Devices: Rhyme, Alliteration, and Rhythm
Poets use patterned sound — rhyme, alliteration, assonance, and rhythm — to create musicality, emphasis, and memorability independent of a poem's literal meaning.
Key fact
A sonnet's volta (turn) is the structural pivot where its argument or tone shifts, positioned differently in Shakespearean vs. Petrarchan form
Key fact
Haiku favor a single concrete, sensory image or juxtaposition over explicit statement or moral
Key fact
Free verse abandons fixed meter and rhyme but still relies on deliberate line breaks, enjambment, and white space as structure
Key fact
Simile signals comparison with 'like' or 'as'; metaphor asserts the comparison directly as identity

Unit 7: Revision as Rewriting

Revision vs. Editing vs. Proofreading
Revision, editing, and proofreading are distinct stages of improving a draft, each operating at a different scale and best done in that order — big structure before small polish.
Cutting vs. Adding
Revision often requires both cutting material that doesn't serve the story and adding material the story needs — knowing which a scene requires is a core revision skill.
Structural Edits
Structural edits address a story's large-scale architecture — order of scenes, pacing, plot logic, and whether the piece's shape serves its meaning.
Line Edits
Line edits work at the sentence and paragraph level, refining clarity, rhythm, word choice, and voice once the larger structure is sound.
Reading Aloud as Revision Technique
Reading a draft aloud surfaces problems the eye skims past — awkward rhythm, unnatural dialogue, run-on sentences, and repeated words become audible.
Getting Distance Before Revising
Setting a draft aside before revising creates psychological distance that lets a writer see the work more like a reader than its author.
Key fact
Revision (structure), editing (sentence-level), and proofreading (surface errors) are distinct stages, done in that order
Key fact
'Kill your darlings' means cutting material that serves the writer's ego rather than the story
Key fact
Reverse outlining an existing draft reveals structural problems invisible when reading line by line
Key fact
Reading a draft aloud exposes rhythm and dialogue problems the eye skims past

Unit 8: Workshop: Giving & Taking Feedback

The 'Sandwich' and Other Feedback Frameworks
Structured feedback frameworks, like the praise-critique-praise 'sandwich,' help givers balance honesty with encouragement so writers can actually hear and use the critique.
Descriptive vs. Prescriptive Feedback
Descriptive feedback reports the reader's experience of the text; prescriptive feedback tells the writer what to do instead — workshops generally favor the former.
Giving Effective Feedback
Effective workshop feedback is specific, textually grounded, and focused on the piece as it exists on the page rather than on the writer as a person.
Receiving Criticism
Receiving feedback well is itself a skill: it means listening fully, resisting the urge to defend, and treating critique as information rather than a verdict.
Peer Review Etiquette
Workshop etiquette sets shared norms — how feedback is delivered, how much air time each piece gets, and how participants treat each other's work and time.
Key fact
The feedback 'sandwich' pairs genuine praise with specific critique, but padding with insincere praise undermines its purpose
Key fact
Descriptive feedback reports a reader's experience; prescriptive feedback dictates a fix — workshops generally favor the descriptive
Key fact
The 'writer doesn't speak' rule during critique lets a writer listen fully instead of defending the work
Key fact
A pattern across multiple readers' feedback signals a real issue more reliably than one reader's isolated reaction

Unit 1: Writer's Toolkit & Habits

Watch out
Editing while freewriting, not after — stopping to fix grammar defeats the purpose of generative drafting
Watch out
Telling emotions with abstract labels, not showing them through concrete, specific action and sensory detail
Watch out
Opening with backstory or scene-setting, not with a hook that raises a question or launches action
Watch out
Waiting for inspiration to start writing, not building a routine that produces pages regardless of mood

Unit 2: Story Elements Deep Dive

Watch out
Confusing story (raw chronological events) with plot (events linked by causation), not recognizing plot requires cause and effect
Watch out
Stating theme directly through a character's speech, not letting theme emerge implicitly through action and consequence
Watch out
Treating setting as static backdrop, not using it actively to build mood, conflict, or symbolic meaning
Watch out
Overexplaining a symbol's meaning, not trusting readers to infer significance from repetition and context

Unit 3: Character Craft

Watch out
Giving a character a vague want ('wants to be happy'), not a specific, concrete, actionable goal
Watch out
Relying only on direct characterization ('she was kind'), not showing traits through action, speech, and reaction
Watch out
Front-loading backstory in exposition dumps, not deploying it in small doses tied to present action
Watch out
Writing a purely evil antagonist with no understandable motivation, not a character who believes they are right

Unit 4: Dialogue That Works

Watch out
Writing dialogue as a verbatim transcript of real speech, not a stylized, purposeful compression of it
Watch out
Relying on adverb-heavy tags ('she said sadly'), not action beats or word choice to convey emotion
Watch out
Making dialogue too 'on-the-nose' by having characters state exactly what they feel, not building in subtext
Watch out
Overusing heavy phonetic spelling for dialect, not suggesting voice through rhythm, syntax, and word choice

Unit 5: Point of View Choices

Watch out
Head-hopping within a scene, not a controlled shift at a scene or chapter break
Watch out
Treating second person as a gimmick rather than a choice that serves theme or subject matter
Watch out
Confusing 'unreliable narrator' with 'poorly written narrator' — unreliability must be a deliberate, detectable craft choice
Watch out
Assuming omniscient narration is 'easier' because it allows anything — it actually requires more control to avoid diffusing tension

Unit 6: Poetry Forms & Imagery

Watch out
Free verse means no fixed meter or rhyme, not no structure or craft at all
Watch out
Confusing simile ('like'/'as') with metaphor (direct assertion) — a common mix-up on identification questions
Watch out
Counting syllables rigidly for English haiku instead of recognizing the form's spirit of brevity and image
Watch out
Reaching for clichéd comparisons ('busy as a bee') instead of fresh, specific imagery that actually illuminates

Unit 7: Revision as Rewriting

Watch out
Editing sentences before fixing structure, then having to redo that polish after cutting or reordering scenes
Watch out
Confusing revision (re-seeing the whole) with proofreading (catching typos) — they operate at very different scales
Watch out
Revising immediately after finishing a draft, without the distance needed to see it as a reader would
Watch out
Refusing to cut a favorite scene or line because it's well-written, even when it doesn't serve the story

Unit 8: Workshop: Giving & Taking Feedback

Watch out
Confusing 'I didn't like this' with an objective flaw — most workshop feedback is a subjective reader reaction, not a verdict
Watch out
Being prescriptive ('change it to X') when descriptive feedback ('I felt confused here') would preserve the writer's ownership of the fix
Watch out
Defending or explaining a piece during critique instead of listening, which blocks hearing what a reader actually experienced
Watch out
Treating every single comment as mandatory — the writer, not the group, has final authority over revision choices
