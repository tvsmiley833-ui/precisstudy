Unit 1: Writer's Toolkit & Habits6 concepts · ~26 min▾
Freewriting and Generative Drafting
Freewriting means writing continuously without stopping to edit, judge, or plan, in order to bypass the inner critic and generate raw material.
  • Set a timer (5-15 minutes) and keep the pen or cursor moving even if you write 'I don't know what to write'
  • Peter Elbow popularized freewriting in 'Writing Without Teachers' as a way to separate generating from editing
  • Never delete during a freewrite; cross out lightly at most, then mine the draft afterward for usable lines
  • Use prompts (an object, a sound, a memory, a 'what if') to jump-start a stalled freewrite
  • Morning pages (Julia Cameron's term) are a daily three-page freewrite meant to clear mental clutter before real work
  • Freewriting builds fluency; it is not meant to produce a finished piece
Idea Generation Techniques
Writers use deliberate techniques to move from a blank page to a workable idea rather than waiting for inspiration.
  • Brainstorming/listing: generate quantity first, judge later
  • Clustering (mind-mapping): start with a core word, radiate associations outward to find unexpected connections
  • The 'what if' question: destabilize a familiar situation to find story potential
  • Journaling and observation logs: collect overheard dialogue, striking images, and small true details for later use
  • Constraint-based generation (e.g., write a scene using only dialogue, or set in one room) forces inventive solutions
  • Combining unrelated concepts (a fairy tale + a workplace) often produces more original premises than starting from scratch
Show, Don't Tell
'Show, don't tell' means rendering experience through concrete sensory detail and action so readers infer emotion and meaning themselves, rather than being told what to feel.
  • Telling states a conclusion ('She was nervous'); showing gives evidence ('Her pen tapped an uneven rhythm against the desk')
  • Use specific, sensory nouns and verbs instead of abstract adjectives
  • Telling is not always wrong — it can compress time and pace a story; use it deliberately for transitions
  • Chekhov's advice, often paraphrased as 'don't tell me the moon is shining, show me the glint of light on broken glass,' captures the principle
  • Overuse of showing can bloat a scene; balance vivid detail with narrative summary
  • Test: if you can cut the abstract label and the image still conveys the feeling, the showing is working
Sensory Detail and Concrete Imagery
Concrete, specific sensory details ground abstract ideas and emotions in a reader's experience, making prose vivid and credible.
  • Engage more than sight: sound, smell, touch, and taste are underused but highly evocative
  • Specificity beats generality: 'a rusted Schwinn' is more vivid than 'an old bike'
  • Select details that do double duty — revealing character or theme, not just decorating the scene
  • Avoid detail overload; choose the two or three details that matter most rather than cataloguing a room
  • Concrete imagery supports figurative language: metaphors and similes work best when built from precise, sensory nouns
  • Grounding abstractions (love, grief, ambition) in physical action and object keeps prose from feeling vague
Writing Routines and Revision Mindset
Professional writers rely on consistent habits — a regular schedule, low-stakes drafting, and a willingness to revise extensively — more than on waiting for inspiration.
  • Anne Lamott's 'shitty first drafts' (from 'Bird by Bird') gives permission to write badly on the first pass
  • Separate drafting and editing into different sessions or mindsets to avoid stalling on every sentence
  • Set small, consistent goals (a word count or time block) rather than relying on motivation
  • Keep a writer's notebook for fragments, overheard lines, and images to return to later
  • Revision is not punishment for a bad draft; it is where craft decisions actually get made
  • Reading widely and analytically (noticing how other writers solve problems) is itself a writing habit
Point of Entry: Starting a Piece
How and where a piece begins shapes reader engagement immediately, so writers choose an opening strategy deliberately rather than starting wherever chronology happens to begin.
  • In medias res: starting in the middle of action to hook readers before backfilling context
  • A striking image or line can establish voice and tone before plot begins
  • Avoid throat-clearing openings — weather reports, waking-up scenes, or long backstory dumps that delay the real story
  • An opening should raise a question the reader wants answered, whether about plot, character, or voice
  • The first line sets a contract with the reader about genre, tone, and pace
  • Drafts often find their 'true' opening later; writers frequently cut their original first page in revision
Editing while freewriting, not after — stopping to fix grammar defeats the purpose of generative drafting
Telling emotions with abstract labels, not showing them through concrete, specific action and sensory detail
Opening with backstory or scene-setting, not with a hook that raises a question or launches action
Waiting for inspiration to start writing, not building a routine that produces pages regardless of mood
Unit 2: Story Elements Deep Dive6 concepts · ~26 min▾
Plot Structure: Freytag's Pyramid and Beyond
Plot is the sequence of causally related events; classical models like Freytag's Pyramid describe a rising and falling arc of tension.
  • Freytag's Pyramid: exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution/denouement
  • The inciting incident disrupts the status quo and launches the central conflict
  • Rising action should escalate stakes through a series of complications, not just repeat the same obstacle
  • The climax is the story's peak of tension, where the central conflict is decided
  • Not all stories follow a linear arc — many contemporary and literary works use circular, fragmented, or in medias res structures
  • Plot differs from story: story is 'the king died and then the queen died'; plot is 'the king died, and then the queen died of grief' (E.M. Forster)
Conflict: Types and Function
Conflict is the engine of narrative tension, arising from opposing forces that a character must confront.
  • Person vs. person: direct interpersonal opposition (protagonist vs. antagonist)
  • Person vs. self: internal conflict, competing desires or moral struggle
  • Person vs. nature: survival against natural or environmental forces
  • Person vs. society: conflict with social norms, institutions, or systems
  • Person vs. fate/technology/the supernatural: additional external forces beyond human control
  • Effective conflict is specific and escalating, tied directly to what the protagonist wants and fears
Setting as Active Element
Setting is more than backdrop; skilled writers use place and time to shape mood, reveal character, and drive plot.
  • Setting establishes atmosphere and mood through sensory and concrete detail, not exposition dumps
  • A well-chosen setting can create or intensify conflict (a blizzard trapping characters together)
  • Setting can function symbolically, mirroring or contrasting a character's internal state
  • Historical and cultural setting details must be integrated organically, not lectured to the reader
  • Setting includes time period, era, and even time of day/season, all of which affect tone
  • Regionalism in fiction (e.g., Faulkner's Yoknapatawpha County) shows setting operating almost as a character itself
Theme and Thematic Statement
Theme is the underlying idea or insight about life that a story explores, usually emerging through action and consequence rather than direct statement.
  • Theme is not a moral lesson bluntly stated; it is an implicit exploration a reader infers
  • A story can hold multiple, even contradictory, themes simultaneously
  • Symbols, motifs, and repeated images reinforce theme without stating it outright
  • Theme often emerges from what a character loses, learns, or refuses to learn by the story's end
  • Avoid 'moralizing' — having a character or narrator explicitly state the lesson undercuts subtlety
  • Genre conventions can carry thematic weight (e.g., horror often explores fear of the unknown or loss of control)
Motif and Symbol
A motif is a recurring element (image, phrase, or object) that develops meaning through repetition; a symbol is an object or image that represents something beyond its literal meaning.
  • Symbols can be universal (a dove for peace) or contextual, gaining meaning specific to the story
  • Motifs accumulate significance across a text through repetition with variation
  • Overexplaining a symbol ('the broken clock represented time running out') destroys its power — trust the reader
  • The green light in 'The Great Gatsby' is a classic literary symbol of unreachable longing and the American Dream
  • Effective symbols arise naturally from the story's world rather than being imposed from outside
  • Distinguish symbol (representational) from motif (recurring pattern) from theme (the idea both serve)
Pacing and Scene vs. Summary
Pacing controls how quickly or slowly a reader experiences story time, achieved by alternating dramatized scenes with summarized narration.
  • Scene renders moment-by-moment action and dialogue in near-real time, slowing the reader's experience
  • Summary compresses time, covering days, months, or years in a few sentences
  • Fast pacing (short sentences, more white space, quick cuts) suits action and tension
  • Slow pacing (longer sentences, more interiority and description) suits reflection and emotional weight
  • Overuse of summary makes a story feel distant; overuse of scene can bloat pacing and bury the plot
  • Chapter and section breaks are pacing tools, controlling where tension resets or builds
Confusing story (raw chronological events) with plot (events linked by causation), not recognizing plot requires cause and effect
Stating theme directly through a character's speech, not letting theme emerge implicitly through action and consequence
Treating setting as static backdrop, not using it actively to build mood, conflict, or symbolic meaning
Overexplaining a symbol's meaning, not trusting readers to infer significance from repetition and context
Unit 3: Character Craft6 concepts · ~26 min▾
Want vs. Need: Character Motivation
Compelling characters pursue a conscious external 'want' while often lacking awareness of a deeper internal 'need,' and the gap between the two drives character arc.
  • Want is the tangible goal a character consciously pursues (win the race, get the promotion)
  • Need is the internal truth or growth the character must reach, often unrecognized at the story's start
  • The tension between want and need creates dramatic irony and propels transformation
  • A character may achieve their want but fail their need (or vice versa) for a bittersweet or tragic ending
  • Motivation should be specific and rooted in backstory, not generic ('wants to be happy')
  • Clear want gives a story forward momentum; unclear want often causes flat, meandering plots
Characterization: Direct and Indirect
Characterization is the set of techniques a writer uses to reveal who a character is, ranging from direct authorial statement to indirect inference through action.
  • Direct characterization: the narrator or another character states a trait outright ('She was stubborn')
  • Indirect characterization: traits are revealed through speech, actions, thoughts, appearance, and how others react (sometimes called STEAL)
  • Indirect characterization is generally considered more sophisticated because it mirrors how we learn about real people
  • Consistent, telling details (a nervous habit, a verbal tic) build a coherent character across a text
  • Contradiction and complexity — a kind character who lies, a cruel character who loves animals — create dimensionality
  • Physical description should reveal character or story-relevant information, not just catalog appearance
Round vs. Flat, Dynamic vs. Static
E.M. Forster's terms distinguish characters by complexity (round vs. flat) while dynamic vs. static describes whether a character changes over the story.
  • Round characters are complex, multidimensional, and capable of surprising the reader believably
  • Flat characters are simple, built around a single trait or function, useful for minor roles
  • Dynamic characters undergo significant internal change by the story's end
  • Static characters remain fundamentally the same, which can be intentional (a fixed moral compass) or a flaw if unintended
  • Protagonists are usually round and dynamic; a flat, static protagonist often signals underdeveloped craft
  • Even flat characters benefit from one vivid, specific detail to avoid feeling like cardboard
The Character Arc
A character arc is the internal transformation a character undergoes across a story, typically moving from a flawed worldview or wound toward change (positive, negative, or flat).
  • Positive/change arc: the character overcomes a flaw or 'lie they believe' to reach growth
  • Negative/fall arc: the character succumbs further to their flaw, ending worse off (common in tragedy)
  • Flat/testing arc: the character's correct worldview is tested and ultimately confirmed, changing the world around them instead
  • The 'ghost' or wound: a past event that created the character's core misbelief or fear
  • Arc should be demonstrated through choices and action at the climax, not simply announced
  • A well-built arc connects external plot events to internal psychological change
Backstory and Its Deployment
Backstory is a character's history prior to the story's start; it must inform present behavior without overwhelming the narrative.
  • Reveal backstory in small, purposeful doses tied to present action, not front-loaded exposition dumps
  • Backstory should explain motivation and behavior, answering 'why does this character act this way now?'
  • Flashback is one tool for backstory but should be used sparingly and only when necessary information can't be conveyed otherwise
  • Withholding backstory strategically can create mystery and reader investment
  • Not all backstory needs to appear on the page — writers often know more about a character than readers see
  • Overuse of backstory early in a story slows pacing and delays reader engagement with present conflict
Antagonists and Foils
An antagonist opposes the protagonist's goals, while a foil is a character whose contrasting traits highlight another character's qualities by comparison.
  • The strongest antagonists believe they are right; villainy framed only as 'evil for evil's sake' is often less compelling
  • An antagonist's goal should directly conflict with the protagonist's want, creating unavoidable confrontation
  • A foil need not be a villain — it can be a friend, sibling, or rival whose different choices illuminate the protagonist's traits
  • Antagonistic force need not be a person; it can be society, nature, or an internal flaw
  • A well-drawn antagonist often mirrors the protagonist's own flaw, taken to a different or extreme conclusion
  • Foils clarify characterization through contrast rather than direct exposition
Giving a character a vague want ('wants to be happy'), not a specific, concrete, actionable goal
Relying only on direct characterization ('she was kind'), not showing traits through action, speech, and reaction
Front-loading backstory in exposition dumps, not deploying it in small doses tied to present action
Writing a purely evil antagonist with no understandable motivation, not a character who believes they are right
Unit 4: Dialogue That Works6 concepts · ~26 min▾
Dialogue vs. Real Speech
Good fictional dialogue is a stylized, compressed version of real speech, not a verbatim transcript — it cuts filler while preserving the illusion of natural talk.
  • Real speech is full of ums, false starts, repetition, and small talk that would bore a reader if transcribed exactly
  • Fiction dialogue trims to the essential exchange while keeping rhythm and voice that feel authentic
  • Every line of dialogue should do at least one job: reveal character, advance plot, or create tension — ideally more than one
  • Subtext matters more than surface content — characters often don't say exactly what they mean
  • Distinct diction, sentence length, and rhythm differentiate one character's voice from another's
  • Reading dialogue aloud is a standard revision technique to test whether it sounds natural
Dialogue Tags and Beats
Dialogue tags (he said, she asked) attribute speech to a speaker, while action beats use physical action instead of or alongside tags to ground a line in the scene.
  • 'Said' is largely invisible to readers and is generally preferred over fancier tags (exclaimed, expostulated)
  • Adverb-heavy tags ('she said angrily') often signal telling rather than showing the emotion
  • Action beats ('He set down his fork.' 'I'm leaving.') attribute dialogue while adding characterization and pacing
  • Overtagging every line is unnecessary once readers track who's speaking in a two-person exchange
  • New speaker, new paragraph is the standard formatting convention for dialogue
  • Beats can replace tags to avoid monotony and to control the pace of an exchange
Subtext in Dialogue
Subtext is the unspoken meaning beneath a line of dialogue — what a character truly wants, fears, or means, which differs from their literal words.
  • Characters often talk around a subject rather than stating their true feelings directly, especially in tense scenes
  • Subtext is created through evasion, deflection, changing the subject, or saying the opposite of what's felt
  • The 'iceberg theory' (Hemingway) suggests the most powerful meaning stays beneath the surface of the text
  • On-the-nose dialogue — characters stating exactly what they feel — often feels flat and unrealistic
  • Tension rises when the reader senses a gap between what's said and what's meant
  • Context, tone, and body language (via action beats) help readers detect subtext without explicit narration
Dialect, Voice, and Idiolect
Idiolect is an individual's unique pattern of speech; writers use vocabulary, syntax, and rhythm — more than phonetic spelling — to render distinct character voices.
  • Idiolect includes word choice, sentence length, favorite phrases, and syntax patterns unique to a character
  • Heavy phonetic spelling ('Ah cain't rightly say') can become distracting or feel like caricature; used sparingly if at all
  • Suggest dialect through rhythm, word choice, and grammar rather than misspelling every word
  • Distinct character voices help readers track who's speaking even without tags
  • Code-switching (a character speaking differently in different contexts) can reveal social awareness or power dynamics
  • Formal vs. casual register shifts convey relationship dynamics and social context between speakers
Dialogue as Conflict Engine
The strongest dialogue scenes contain some form of conflict or disagreement, even subtle, rather than two characters simply agreeing pleasantly.
  • Give characters conflicting goals within a single scene so dialogue naturally generates friction
  • Interruption, contradiction, and evasion create dynamic back-and-forth rather than static Q&A
  • Avoid 'talking heads' syndrome — dialogue with no physical action, setting, or stakes grounding it
  • A scene where characters simply exchange pleasant information rarely earns its place; look for the underlying tension
  • Power dynamics (who interrupts whom, who asks questions vs. answers) can be conveyed entirely through dialogue patterns
  • Silence and what a character refuses to say can be as dramatically potent as what they do say
Formatting and Punctuation Conventions
Standard manuscript conventions for dialogue (punctuation placement, paragraphing, and quotation style) keep exchanges clear and professional.
  • Commas and periods go inside closing quotation marks in American English convention ('I know,' she said.)
  • A new paragraph begins with each new speaker to keep exchanges easy to follow
  • Interrupted dialogue uses an em dash inside the quotes ('I was just—') while trailing off uses an ellipsis ('I don't know...')
  • Action beats attributing a different character's action than the one speaking should generally start a new paragraph
  • Long speeches spanning multiple paragraphs omit the closing quotation mark at the end of each paragraph except the last
  • Overuse of exclamation points in dialogue tags or lines can undercut tension rather than heighten it
Writing dialogue as a verbatim transcript of real speech, not a stylized, purposeful compression of it
Relying on adverb-heavy tags ('she said sadly'), not action beats or word choice to convey emotion

Unit 5: Point of View Choices6 concepts · ~26 min▾
First Person Narration
First person narration tells the story through an 'I' who is a character inside the events, giving readers direct access to one mind but only one.
  • The narrator can only report what they personally see, hear, think, or are told — everything else is speculation or hearsay
  • Voice becomes central: diction, humor, and blind spots all characterize the narrator as they narrate
  • First person creates intimacy and immediacy but limits scope — the reader never knows more than the narrator does
  • First person plural ('we') is a rarer choice used to render a collective voice, like a town or group
  • A first-person narrator can misjudge their own story, creating dramatic irony readers see but the narrator doesn't
  • Present-tense first person heightens immediacy; past-tense implies the narrator survived to tell the story
Second Person Narration
Second person narration addresses the protagonist as 'you,' implicating the reader directly in the action and creating an unusual, often disorienting closeness.
  • 'You' can mean the protagonist, the reader themselves, or both at once, depending on how the writer handles it
  • It's rare in long fiction because the novelty can wear thin, but it suits short pieces, instructional-style narratives, and trauma or dissociation
  • Second person can mimic the emotional distancing of talking to oneself, as in Jay McInerney's Bright Lights, Big City
  • It works well for choose-your-own-adventure structures and interactive or game-like narratives
  • Overuse risks feeling gimmicky if the 'you' address doesn't serve a clear thematic purpose
  • Second person often pairs with present tense to intensify the sense of being inside the moment
Third Person Limited
Third person limited narrates with 'he/she/they' but restricts access to a single character's thoughts and perceptions per scene, like a camera mounted on one person's shoulder.
  • The narrator can report only what the viewpoint character could plausibly know, see, or infer
  • This is the most common mode in contemporary fiction because it balances intimacy with narrative flexibility
  • Multiple third-limited viewpoints can appear in a novel, but conventionally one viewpoint per scene or chapter avoids confusing head-hopping
  • Free indirect discourse blends the narrator's voice with the character's inner thoughts without quotation marks or 'he thought'
  • Third limited lets a writer withhold information other characters know, building suspense or dramatic irony
  • Switching viewpoint characters between chapters is a common structural device in multi-POV novels
Third Person Omniscient
Third person omniscient uses a narrator who can access any character's thoughts, move freely through time and place, and comment on events from outside the story.
  • The omniscient narrator can reveal what multiple characters are thinking within the same scene, unlike limited POV
  • Classic omniscient narrators (Austen, Tolstoy, Eliot) often editorialize, offering judgment or irony about characters and events
  • Omniscience allows dramatic irony on a large scale — readers can know what no single character knows
  • The risk is diffusing emotional intensity: readers may feel less attached to any one character than in limited POV
  • A 'camera-eye' or objective variant reports only external action and dialogue, withholding any character's interior thoughts
  • Head-hopping — jumping between characters' thoughts within a single paragraph without control — is a common beginner error, distinct from deliberate omniscience
Reliability and the Unreliable Narrator
An unreliable narrator's account of events cannot be fully trusted, whether from limited knowledge, self-deception, mental state, age, or deliberate deception.
  • Unreliability can stem from naivety (a child narrator), bias, memory failure, madness, intoxication, or a hidden agenda to mislead the reader
  • Writers signal unreliability through contradictions, gaps, other characters' reactions, or a disconnect between the narrator's claims and depicted events
  • Classic examples include Holden Caulfield's skewed self-perception and the narrator's dawning madness in Poe's 'The Tell-Tale Heart'
  • The reader's job becomes reading against the narrator — inferring the truth the narrator can't or won't state directly
  • Unreliable narration works in first person most naturally, but a close third-limited narrator can also filter events through a biased consciousness
  • A twist reliant on unreliable narration should still play fair — clues should exist on rereading, not come from nowhere
Choosing and Committing to a POV
POV choice shapes what a story can reveal, when, and to whom — it should be a deliberate craft decision tied to the story's central tension, not a default.
  • Ask which character has the most at stake, the most interesting perception, or the most necessary blind spot for the story to work
  • Distance can be adjusted within a POV: close narration stays inside a character's immediate perception; distant narration steps back for summary and pacing
  • Changing POV mid-scene without a clear break (a scene or chapter division) confuses readers about whose consciousness they're in
  • POV determines dramatic irony: what the reader knows that a character doesn't, or vice versa, drives suspense
  • A consistent POV rule, once set for a scene or chapter, should be followed until a deliberate transition signals a shift
  • Testing a scene in more than one POV during drafting can reveal which vantage best serves the story's tension

Unit 6: Poetry Forms & Imagery6 concepts · ~26 min▾
The Sonnet
A sonnet is a 14-line poem, traditionally in iambic pentameter, that uses a fixed structure to set up and resolve a single idea, image, or emotional turn.
  • The Shakespearean (English) sonnet uses three quatrains (ABAB CDCD EFEF) and a closing couplet (GG) that often delivers a twist or summary
  • The Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet splits into an octave (ABBAABBA) posing a problem and a sestet (varied rhyme, e.g. CDECDE) resolving it
  • The 'volta,' or turn, is the pivot point where the poem's argument, tone, or perspective shifts — before the couplet in Shakespearean form, between octave and sestet in Petrarchan
  • Iambic pentameter is five iambic feet (unstressed-stressed) per line, roughly ten syllables, echoing natural English speech rhythm
  • Modern sonnets often keep the 14-line structure and volta while abandoning strict meter or rhyme — a 'loose' or 'contemporary' sonnet
  • The compressed length forces economy: every line must earn its place in developing or resolving the poem's central idea
The Haiku
A haiku is a short Japanese-origin poem, traditionally three lines of 5-7-5 syllables, that captures a single moment of observation, often from nature, without explicit commentary.
  • Traditional haiku include a kigo (season word) that anchors the poem in a specific time of year
  • A kireji (cutting word) creates a pause or juxtaposition between two images or ideas, roughly like a colon or dash in English versions
  • English-language haiku often relax the strict 5-7-5 syllable count in favor of brevity and the form's spirit, since Japanese sound units don't map directly onto English syllables
  • Haiku favor concrete, sensory images over abstract statement or explicit emotion — showing a moment rather than explaining its meaning
  • The juxtaposition of two images (often across the line break) invites the reader to find the connection themselves
  • Haiku resist neat conclusions or morals; the power comes from a precise, suggestive image left open
Free Verse
Free verse is poetry that does not follow a fixed meter or rhyme scheme, instead using line breaks, rhythm, and white space as deliberate structural choices.
  • 'Free' doesn't mean 'unstructured' — free verse poets still make deliberate choices about line length, breaks, and pacing
  • A line break can create emphasis, suspense, or double meaning by controlling where the reader's eye and breath pause
  • Enjambment (a sentence or phrase running past a line break without punctuation) creates momentum and can generate temporary ambiguity resolved on the next line
  • End-stopped lines (with punctuation at the line's end) create a pause or full stop, controlling pacing more deliberately
  • Repetition, anaphora (repeated opening words/phrases), and white space substitute for meter and rhyme as organizing devices
  • Walt Whitman and later poets like William Carlos Williams pioneered free verse as a deliberate departure from formal constraint, not a lack of craft
Imagery
Imagery is language that appeals to the senses — sight, sound, touch, taste, smell — to make abstract ideas or emotions concrete and vivid for the reader.
  • Concrete, specific imagery ('a rusted bicycle wheel') is more evocative than abstract or generic language ('an old object')
  • Imagery doesn't need to be only visual — sound (auditory), touch (tactile), taste (gustatory), and smell (olfactory) imagery all deepen sensory immersion
  • 'Show, don't tell' in poetry often means rendering an emotion through a precise image rather than naming the emotion directly
  • A controlling or extended image can recur and develop across a whole poem, unifying its structure
  • Imagery works best when specific and sensory rather than vague ('flowers') — precision ('a wilting daffodil') sharpens the picture
  • Juxtaposing unexpected images can generate meaning or emotion the individual images don't carry alone
Metaphor and Simile
Metaphor and simile are figurative comparisons that describe one thing in terms of another to reveal a resemblance, but simile signals the comparison explicitly while metaphor asserts it directly.
  • A simile compares using 'like' or 'as' ('her voice was like gravel'); a metaphor asserts identity ('her voice was gravel')
  • An extended metaphor develops a single comparison across multiple lines or an entire poem, deepening its implications
  • A mixed metaphor combines incompatible comparisons in a way that confuses or unintentionally amuses ('we'll cross that bridge when we burn it')
  • Personification, a specific type of metaphor, gives human qualities to nonhuman things or abstractions
  • A dead metaphor ('the leg of a table') has become so common it's no longer perceived as figurative language
  • Fresh, specific comparisons are generally more effective than clichéd ones ('busy as a bee') because clichés no longer surprise or illuminate
Sound Devices: Rhyme, Alliteration, and Rhythm
Poets use patterned sound — rhyme, alliteration, assonance, and rhythm — to create musicality, emphasis, and memorability independent of a poem's literal meaning.
  • Alliteration repeats initial consonant sounds ('wild and windy'); assonance repeats vowel sounds within words ('the light of the fire')
  • Consonance repeats consonant sounds anywhere in nearby words, not just at the start ('pitter patter')
  • Rhyme scheme is the pattern of end rhymes in a poem, labeled with letters (ABAB, AABB) to track which lines rhyme
  • Slant (or near) rhyme uses sounds that are close but not identical ('worm'/'swarm'), offering subtler musicality than perfect rhyme
  • Meter is a poem's rhythmic pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables; scanning a line means marking its stress pattern
  • Sound devices should reinforce meaning or mood — harsh consonants can underscore conflict, while soft, flowing sounds can underscore calm
Free verse means no fixed meter or rhyme, not no structure or craft at all

Unit 7: Revision as Rewriting6 concepts · ~26 min▾
Revision vs. Editing vs. Proofreading
Revision, editing, and proofreading are distinct stages of improving a draft, each operating at a different scale and best done in that order — big structure before small polish.
  • Revision (literally 're-seeing') addresses big-picture issues: structure, plot logic, character arcs, pacing, and theme
  • Editing works at the paragraph and sentence level: clarity, word choice, sentence rhythm, and consistency
  • Proofreading is the final pass for surface errors: spelling, grammar, punctuation, and typos
  • Doing these out of order wastes effort — polishing sentences that later get cut in a structural revision is time lost
  • Most experienced writers cycle through multiple rounds of revision before editing and proofreading become worthwhile
  • 'Revision' is often confused with 'editing' by beginners, leading them to fix commas while ignoring a broken plot
Cutting vs. Adding
Revision often requires both cutting material that doesn't serve the story and adding material the story needs — knowing which a scene requires is a core revision skill.
  • Cutting removes redundancy, scenes that don't advance plot or character, over-explanation, and throat-clearing openings
  • A common diagnostic: if a scene or paragraph could be deleted without the reader missing anything, it's a cutting candidate
  • Adding fills gaps: missing motivation, underdeveloped stakes, insufficient setup for a later payoff, or thin sensory detail
  • 'Kill your darlings' means cutting a beloved line or scene that serves the writer's ego more than the story
  • Writers often need to add earlier in a draft to set up something they only discovered while writing the ending
  • A useful rule: cut what a reader doesn't need to know yet; add what a reader needs to believe a later moment
Structural Edits
Structural edits address a story's large-scale architecture — order of scenes, pacing, plot logic, and whether the piece's shape serves its meaning.
  • Structural revision may involve reordering scenes, moving a flashback, or changing where the story begins ('start later, end earlier')
  • Pacing problems (a sagging middle, a rushed climax) are structural issues, not fixed by better sentences
  • Plot holes and inconsistent character motivation are diagnosed and fixed at the structural level before finer editing
  • Outlining or summarizing existing scenes ('reverse outlining') after a draft helps reveal structural problems invisible line by line
  • A structural edit can mean cutting an entire subplot or character if it doesn't serve the story's core throughline
  • Structural problems are usually the most labor-intensive to fix and should be addressed before line-level polish
Line Edits
Line edits work at the sentence and paragraph level, refining clarity, rhythm, word choice, and voice once the larger structure is sound.
  • Line edits target wordiness, vague language, repeated words, and awkward or unclear phrasing
  • Varying sentence length and structure prevents monotony and controls pacing at the sentence level
  • Strong verbs and specific nouns reduce reliance on adverbs and adjectives to do descriptive work
  • Consistency checks (tense, POV, character details, timeline) are typically caught at the line-edit stage
  • Line edits should preserve the writer's voice, not sand every sentence into generic 'correct' prose
  • Reading a line edit in isolation from the whole work can miss how sentences function in context — check both levels
Reading Aloud as Revision Technique
Reading a draft aloud surfaces problems the eye skims past — awkward rhythm, unnatural dialogue, run-on sentences, and repeated words become audible.
  • The ear catches clunky rhythm and run-ons that the eye, used to skimming familiar text, glosses over
  • Stumbling over a sentence while reading aloud is a reliable signal that it needs restructuring
  • Dialogue especially benefits from being read aloud (or performed) to test whether it sounds like real speech
  • Reading aloud slows the writer down, forcing attention to each word rather than skimming for overall sense
  • Having someone else read the draft aloud to the writer can reveal different problems than reading it oneself
  • Text-to-speech tools offer a similar benefit by rendering prose in an unfamiliar 'voice' that exposes awkward phrasing
Getting Distance Before Revising
Setting a draft aside before revising creates psychological distance that lets a writer see the work more like a reader than its author.
  • Immediately after finishing a draft, a writer is too close to see plot holes or weak scenes objectively
  • Even a short break (a day, a week) measurably improves a writer's ability to spot problems on rereading
  • Distance helps writers evaluate whether an intended effect actually lands on the page, not just in their head
  • Printing a draft or changing its font/format can create a similar 'fresh eyes' effect by making it look unfamiliar
  • Revising too soon after drafting risks defending choices rather than honestly evaluating them
  • Professional writers typically build deliberate downtime into their process between drafting and revising

Unit 8: Workshop: Giving & Taking Feedback5 concepts · ~23 min▾
The 'Sandwich' and Other Feedback Frameworks
Structured feedback frameworks, like the praise-critique-praise 'sandwich,' help givers balance honesty with encouragement so writers can actually hear and use the critique.
  • The feedback sandwich opens with genuine strengths, moves to specific concerns, and closes with encouragement or a forward-looking note
  • The 'stop, start, continue' framework organizes feedback into what isn't working, what's missing, and what's working well
  • Effective frameworks force specificity: 'I liked it' or 'this is confusing' are too vague to act on without examples
  • Praise in a feedback framework should be genuine and specific, not padding inserted just to soften criticism
  • Frameworks work best paired with the writer's own stated goals for the piece — feedback should answer what the writer actually wants to know
  • Over-reliance on a rigid formula can feel mechanical; the goal is honest, structured feedback, not scripted phrases
Descriptive vs. Prescriptive Feedback
Descriptive feedback reports the reader's experience of the text; prescriptive feedback tells the writer what to do instead — workshops generally favor the former.
  • Descriptive feedback: 'I got confused about who was speaking here' reports an effect without dictating a fix
  • Prescriptive feedback: 'Add a dialogue tag here' tells the writer exactly what change to make
  • Descriptive feedback respects the writer's ownership of solutions — the writer, not the reader, decides how to fix a diagnosed problem
  • Too much prescriptive feedback can push a piece toward the critic's vision rather than the writer's own
  • Good workshop practice usually favors describing effects and reactions, letting the writer generate solutions
  • Prescriptive fixes are sometimes appropriate for narrow, technical issues (a grammar error) but risky for larger creative choices
Giving Effective Feedback
Effective workshop feedback is specific, textually grounded, and focused on the piece as it exists on the page rather than on the writer as a person.
  • Point to specific lines, moments, or passages rather than making global, unsupported claims about the whole piece
  • Distinguish 'this didn't work for me' (a subjective reader reaction) from 'this is objectively wrong' (rarely true in creative work)
  • Address craft choices and their effects, not the writer's talent, intelligence, or character
  • Prioritize the most significant issues rather than listing every minor quibble, which can overwhelm and obscure what matters most
  • Ask questions ('What if the scene started here instead?') to prompt the writer's own thinking rather than dictate answers
  • Comment on what's working, not only what isn't — writers need to know which choices to keep as they revise
Receiving Criticism
Receiving feedback well is itself a skill: it means listening fully, resisting the urge to defend, and treating critique as information rather than a verdict.
  • The common workshop rule 'the writer doesn't speak' during critique lets the writer listen without needing to defend or explain
  • Defensiveness (explaining what you 'really meant') often prevents a writer from hearing what a reader actually experienced
  • Not every piece of feedback needs to be acted on — the writer retains final authority over their own work
  • Look for patterns across multiple readers' feedback; a single reader's confusion may be idiosyncratic, but a pattern signals a real issue
  • Separating a reaction to feedback ('that stings') from an evaluation of its usefulness helps a writer use criticism productively
  • Taking notes during critique, rather than only listening, preserves detail for later when emotion has settled
Peer Review Etiquette
Workshop etiquette sets shared norms — how feedback is delivered, how much air time each piece gets, and how participants treat each other's work and time.
  • Read or review the piece fully and carefully before the workshop session, rather than skimming or arriving unprepared
  • Respect time limits so every writer's work gets fair attention rather than one piece dominating the session
  • Avoid hijacking a workshop discussion to rewrite the piece into what the reviewer would have written instead
  • Confidentiality matters: workshop drafts are typically unpublished, vulnerable work and shouldn't be shared or discussed outside the group
  • Calibrate tone to the writer's experience level and the piece's stage (a rough first draft needs different feedback than a polished revision)
  • Model the response you'd want to receive — etiquette in a workshop is reciprocal, not one-directional

Unit 6: Poetry Forms & Imagery

Watch out
Free verse means no fixed meter or rhyme, not no structure or craft at all
Watch out
Confusing simile ('like'/'as') with metaphor (direct assertion) — a common mix-up on identification questions
Watch out
Counting syllables rigidly for English haiku instead of recognizing the form's spirit of brevity and image
Watch out
Reaching for clichéd comparisons ('busy as a bee') instead of fresh, specific imagery that actually illuminates

Unit 7: Revision as Rewriting

Watch out
Editing sentences before fixing structure, then having to redo that polish after cutting or reordering scenes
Watch out
Confusing revision (re-seeing the whole) with proofreading (catching typos) — they operate at very different scales
Watch out
Revising immediately after finishing a draft, without the distance needed to see it as a reader would
Watch out
Refusing to cut a favorite scene or line because it's well-written, even when it doesn't serve the story

Unit 8: Workshop: Giving & Taking Feedback

Watch out
Confusing 'I didn't like this' with an objective flaw — most workshop feedback is a subjective reader reaction, not a verdict
Watch out
Being prescriptive ('change it to X') when descriptive feedback ('I felt confused here') would preserve the writer's ownership of the fix
Watch out
Defending or explaining a piece during critique instead of listening, which blocks hearing what a reader actually experienced
Watch out
Treating every single comment as mandatory — the writer, not the group, has final authority over revision choices
