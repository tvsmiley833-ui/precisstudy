Unit 1: Information & Ideas6 concepts · ~26 min

▾

Central Ideas and Main Purpose

Every SAT Reading passage (usually 25-150 words) has one controlling idea that everything else supports. The central-idea question asks what the passage as a whole is mainly about or trying to accomplish, not what any single sentence says.
  • Read the whole passage first; don't answer from the first sentence alone.
  • Ask 'what is this passage DOING' (arguing, explaining, describing, comparing) as well as what it's about.
  • The correct answer must be supported by the ENTIRE passage, not just part of it.
  • Eliminate answers that are too narrow (true of only one detail) or too broad (bring in outside claims).
  • Topic sentences and concluding sentences often signal the main idea, but short SAT passages can bury it mid-passage.
  • Restate the passage in your own one-sentence summary before looking at answer choices.

Supporting Details

Detail questions ask you to locate specific information the passage explicitly states, often phrased 'According to the text' or 'Based on the passage.'
  • These are the most literal questions on the test — the answer is stated, not implied.
  • Locate the exact line/phrase before choosing; do not rely on memory of the passage.
  • Watch for answer choices that use synonyms of the passage's wording (correct) vs. choices that reverse or distort a detail (wrong).
  • Beware 'half-right' answers that get one clause correct and the other clause wrong.
  • If two answers seem textually supported, pick the one that answers the exact question asked.

Making Inferences

Inference questions ask what the passage suggests or implies without stating directly. The correct inference is a small, logical step from the text — never a leap.
  • The right answer must be provable using only what's in the passage — no outside knowledge, no speculation.
  • Look for qualifying language in the passage (may, suggests, some researchers) — inferences often hinge on it.
  • Reject answer choices that are extreme ('always,' 'proves,' 'the only') when the passage is tentative.
  • A safe inference restates cause-and-effect or comparison relationships already implied by the passage's structure.
  • If an inference requires you to assume something not mentioned, it's wrong, however plausible it sounds.

Author's Purpose and Perspective

Purpose questions ask WHY the author included a sentence, detail, or the whole passage — to inform, persuade, illustrate, qualify, or contrast.
  • Identify the verb in the answer choice (illustrate, undermine, emphasize, concede) and check it matches the passage's function, not just its topic.
  • A quotation or example is usually included to support the point made in the surrounding sentences — trace it back to that claim.
  • Distinguish the author's own viewpoint from a viewpoint the author is describing or quoting (common trap in paired-perspective passages).
  • Tone words (skeptical, admiring, cautious, matter-of-fact) come from word choice, not the topic itself.

Summarizing and Paraphrasing

Some questions ask which choice best restates a sentence or passage's content in different words — testing whether you tracked meaning, not just vocabulary.
  • A correct paraphrase preserves the logical relationship (cause, contrast, condition) of the original, not just its topic words.
  • Wrong paraphrases often flip a relationship — turning a cause into an effect, or a similarity into a contrast.
  • Strip out figurative or technical language mentally and restate the sentence in plain terms before comparing to choices.
  • Watch for choices that summarize accurately but add unsupported extra claims.

Reading Data with Text (Informational Graphics)

Some Information & Ideas passages pair a short text with a table or graph; you must connect what the text claims to what the data actually shows.
  • Read the graph's axes, units, and labels before reading the surrounding text's claim about it.
  • Check whether the data supports, contradicts, or is irrelevant to the textual claim — all three appear as trap patterns.
  • Note the exact data range or group described in the text; graphs often contain more categories/years than the claim covers.
  • Answers that require interpolating beyond the graph's shown range are unsupported — reject them.
Choosing an answer that is true in the real world but not stated in the passage, not stated in the passage.
Picking an answer that restates one detail instead of the passage's overall main idea, overall main idea.
Selecting an extreme-language choice ('proves,' 'always,' 'completely') when the passage only hints or qualifies, only hints or qualifies.
Confusing the author's own opinion with a view the author is merely summarizing or quoting, merely summarizing or quoting.

Unit 2: Command of Evidence5 concepts · ~23 min

▾

Textual Evidence Questions

These paired-format questions ask which quotation or line from the passage best supports the answer to a previous question, or best supports a given claim.
  • Answer the claim/question first in your own words, THEN find the line that proves it — don't hunt for evidence before you know what you're proving.
  • The best evidence directly states the claim; it doesn't just relate to the same topic.
  • Eliminate quotations that support a DIFFERENT, related claim — a classic wrong-answer pattern.
  • If a quotation only partially supports the claim, keep checking — a fuller match is usually available.
  • On the digital SAT, evidence questions are single questions (not two-part) — the quotation is baked into the answer choices as bracketed text.

Quantitative Evidence (Graphs & Tables)

Command of Evidence also includes questions where you must pick the data point, from a table or graph, that best supports or completes an argument in the passage.
  • Identify exactly what variable and unit the claim is about before scanning the data.
  • Check every row/column carefully — answers often swap two categories or two years that look similar.
  • A correct data answer must be consistent with the direction of the claim (increase vs. decrease, higher vs. lower).
  • Reject data points that are true but don't address the specific comparison the claim makes.
  • For 'which finding would most strengthen/weaken' questions, decide strengthen or weaken conceptually first, then look for matching data.

Supporting vs. Undermining a Claim

Some questions give a hypothesis or claim and ask which fact, if true, would most support it or most weaken it.
  • A weakening fact usually introduces an alternative explanation, an exception, or a missing condition.
  • A supporting fact usually shows the predicted result actually occurred, or rules out an alternative cause.
  • Irrelevant-but-true facts are the most common wrong answer — they don't logically bear on the claim at all.
  • Watch for facts that support the OPPOSITE of what's asked (a classic reversed-logic trap).

Selecting the Best Evidence Among Similar Options

When two or more answer choices seem to relate to the correct claim, the SAT rewards precision — the tightest logical fit wins.
  • Compare each candidate quotation against the EXACT wording of the claim, not just its general subject.
  • A quotation that provides an example is stronger evidence than one that merely mentions the topic in passing.
  • General background information is rarely 'the best evidence' — specific findings or direct statements usually are.
  • Double-check quantifiers ('some,' 'most,' 'all') in the quotation match the quantifiers in the claim.

Common Data-Reasoning Traps

The digital SAT tests whether you can reason carefully about data, not just read a chart.
  • Correlation shown in data does not by itself prove causation — reject answers that claim one variable 'causes' another unless the passage states it.
  • A single data point is not a trend — don't accept an answer that generalizes from one year/sample to 'always' or 'increasingly.'
  • Sample size and scope matter: a claim about 'all teenagers' isn't supported by data from one school.
  • Percentage change and absolute change are different — misreading one for the other is a frequent trap.
Picking a quotation that's on-topic but proves a different point than the one asked about, than the one asked about.
Treating correlation in a graph as proof of causation, as proof of causation.
Choosing evidence that is true but too general to actually support the specific claim, to actually support the specific claim.
Misreading which direction (increase vs. decrease) the data or claim is going, the data or claim is going.

Unit 3: Words in Context5 concepts · ~23 min

▾

The Cover-and-Predict Strategy

Words-in-Context questions ask you to choose the word/phrase that best completes a sentence with a blank, or to determine the meaning of a word as used in the passage.
  • Cover the answer choices, read the sentence, and predict your own word for the blank before looking at options.
  • Match your prediction's MEANING to a choice — the correct answer is often a less common synonym, not the obvious word.
  • Context clues live in the same sentence or nearby sentences: look for contrast words (but, although, yet) and continuation words (moreover, similarly).
  • The right answer must fit both the meaning AND the grammar/register of the sentence (formal essay vs. casual quote).
  • Don't pick a word just because it's a word you know well — SAT often uses uncommon synonyms of common ideas.

Precise Word Choice and Connotation

Many wrong choices are near-synonyms with the wrong connotation (positive/negative/neutral) or wrong intensity for the context.
  • Determine whether the sentence's tone is positive, negative, or neutral before matching connotation.
  • 'Reluctant' and 'refusing' both mean unwilling, but differ in degree — match the intensity the context implies.
  • Watch for words that sound sophisticated but subtly shift meaning (e.g., 'notorious' vs. 'famous').
  • Eliminate any choice that would be a true statement but wrong tone (too harsh or too mild) for the passage's context.

Transitions as Context Clues

Logical transition words inside or around the blank sentence often ARE the context clue that determines the correct word.
  • 'However/but/yet' signals the blank should contrast with a nearby idea.
  • 'Moreover/similarly/likewise' signals the blank should continue or reinforce a nearby idea.
  • 'Because/since/therefore' signals a cause-effect relationship the word must fit.
  • If the sentence structure is 'not X but rather Y,' the blank's meaning is opposite of X.

Multiple-Meaning Words

Some tested words are common but have a less-common secondary meaning that fits the specific passage context.
  • Words like 'novel' (new vs. a book), 'sound' (healthy/valid vs. noise), 'grave' (serious vs. burial site) can trip you up if you jump to the first meaning you think of.
  • Always re-derive the meaning from the sentence's own context — never from the word's most common definition.
  • If your first-instinct answer isn't offered, reread the sentence for a less common sense of the word.

Tone and Register

Words-in-Context also tests whether a word fits the formality and attitude of the passage as a whole (academic, journalistic, personal narrative).
  • Academic/scientific passages favor precise, neutral, technical word choices.
  • A quoted speaker's own words may be more casual than the surrounding academic narration — match register to the exact spot in the sentence.
  • Overly emotional or colloquial choices are usually wrong in formal expository passages.
Picking the most familiar meaning of a word instead of the meaning that fits this specific context, that fits this specific context.
Choosing a synonym with the wrong connotation or intensity for the sentence's tone, for the sentence's tone.
Ignoring a contrast or continuation transition word that determines the blank's required meaning, that determines the blank's required meaning.
Selecting a word that matches the topic but not the grammatical fit of the sentence, but not the grammatical fit of the sentence.

Unit 4: Expression of Ideas5 concepts · ~23 min

▾

Rhetorical Synthesis

You're given bulleted notes on a topic and a stated goal (e.g., 'to compare two findings'), then asked which sentence best accomplishes that goal using only the given information.
  • Identify the exact goal verb: compare, contrast, introduce, explain a reason, emphasize a similarity/difference.
  • Use ONLY the facts given in the bullets — never add outside knowledge, even if true.
  • Eliminate choices that state facts accurately but don't accomplish the specific stated goal.
  • Eliminate choices that use only one bullet when the goal requires combining two.
  • A correct synthesis sentence is grammatically smooth and logically connects the chosen facts with an appropriate transition.

Transitions (Logical Relationships)

Transition questions ask you to pick the word/phrase that correctly signals the logical relationship between two sentences or clauses.
  • First determine the actual relationship: contrast, cause-effect, addition, example, sequence, or concession.
  • Contrast: however, nevertheless, on the other hand, in contrast, yet.
  • Cause-effect: therefore, thus, consequently, as a result.
  • Addition/continuation: moreover, furthermore, in addition, similarly.
  • Example: for instance, for example, specifically.
  • Read both surrounding sentences fully before choosing — the relationship is often not what the first sentence alone suggests.

Concision and Redundancy

The SAT rewards clear, economical writing — when all else is equal, the most concise grammatically correct option is best.
  • Cut answer choices that repeat an idea already stated elsewhere in the sentence (e.g., 'return back,' 'each individual person').
  • Concise is only correct if it's also grammatically complete and doesn't lose necessary meaning.
  • Wordy phrases like 'due to the fact that' can usually be replaced by 'because' with no meaning loss.
  • Don't confuse 'shorter' with 'correct' — a short choice with a grammar error is still wrong.

Adding, Revising, or Deleting a Sentence

These questions ask whether a proposed sentence should be added (or is effectively added) based on whether it fulfills a stated purpose.
  • Always check the STATED purpose in the question stem — 'yes' answers must actually achieve that purpose, not just be true information.
  • A sentence can be accurate and on-topic yet still 'No' because it doesn't fulfill the specific purpose asked about.
  • Consider whether the addition disrupts the paragraph's flow or duplicates a point already made.
  • For deletion questions, check whether removing the sentence loses a detail that a later sentence depends on.

Sentence Combining and Structure

Some questions ask you to combine two short sentences effectively, testing subordination, coordination, and modifier placement.
  • Subordinating conjunctions (although, because, while, since) show one idea depends on/relates to another.
  • Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) join two independent, equally weighted clauses.
  • Combine using a participial phrase (e.g., 'Having finished the report, she left') only when the modifier clearly refers to the subject that follows.
  • Avoid choices that create a misplaced modifier, a comma splice, or a sentence fragment when combining.
Choosing a transition that fits the topic but signals the wrong logical relationship, the wrong logical relationship.
Picking a rhetorical synthesis sentence that's accurate but doesn't fulfill the specific stated goal, the specific stated goal.
Selecting the shortest option even though it contains a grammar error, contains a grammar error.
Adding outside knowledge not present in the given bullet points during synthesis questions, in the given bullet points during synthesis questions.

Unit 5: Standard English Conventions6 concepts · ~26 min

▾

Subject-Verb Agreement

The verb must agree in number with its TRUE subject, even when other words or phrases sit between them.
  • Find the true subject by mentally deleting prepositional phrases and interrupters: 'The list of items (is/are) long' → subject is 'list' → 'is.'
  • Collective nouns (team, committee, group) are usually singular in American English: 'The team is winning.'
  • Compound subjects joined by 'and' are plural; joined by 'or/nor' the verb agrees with the nearer subject.
  • Indefinite pronouns like 'each,' 'everyone,' 'neither' are singular: 'Each of the students has a book.'
  • A subject following 'there is/there are' still determines the verb: 'There are three reasons.'

Commas, Semicolons, and Colons

Punctuation questions test whether you know exactly what structure each mark requires.
  • Comma: separates items in a list, joins two independent clauses only WITH a FANBOYS conjunction, sets off nonessential (nonrestrictive) clauses/phrases.
  • Semicolon: joins two independent clauses with no conjunction, or separates items in a complex list that already contains commas.
  • Colon: follows a complete independent clause and introduces a list, explanation, or elaboration — never follows an incomplete phrase.
  • Essential (restrictive) clauses ('that,' defining who/which one) take NO commas; nonessential ('which,' extra info) DO take commas.
  • Dashes can substitute for commas or colons to set off nonessential info or introduce elaboration, often more emphatically.

Comma Splices, Run-Ons, and Fragments

One of the most heavily tested conventions rules: recognizing when two clauses are improperly joined or a group of words isn't a complete sentence.
  • A comma splice = two independent clauses joined by only a comma: 'It rained, we stayed inside.' — WRONG.
  • Fix a splice with: a period, a semicolon, a comma + FANBOYS conjunction, or a subordinating conjunction.
  • A run-on has two independent clauses with NO punctuation at all between them — same fixes apply.
  • A fragment is missing a subject, a verb, or is a subordinate clause standing alone: 'Because it rained.' is a fragment.
  • Test independence: can the clause stand alone as a full sentence? If yes, it's independent; if no, it's dependent/subordinate.

Parallel Structure

Items in a list, comparison, or joined by a conjunction must share the same grammatical form.
  • In a list: 'She likes hiking, swimming, and to bike' is WRONG — should be 'hiking, swimming, and biking.'
  • With correlative conjunctions (either/or, neither/nor, not only/but also), the grammatical structure after each half must match.
  • In comparisons ('more X than Y'), X and Y must be the same type of grammatical element.
  • Parallel structure also applies to verb tense consistency across a list of actions.

Pronoun Agreement and Case

Pronouns must agree in number and gender with their antecedent, and must use the correct case (subject/object/possessive).
  • A pronoun's antecedent must be clear and unambiguous — if a sentence could refer to two different nouns, it's an error.
  • Singular antecedents (everyone, each, a person) take singular pronouns; in modern usage 'they' is accepted as a singular gender-neutral pronoun on some style guides, but SAT favors clear singular/plural matching.
  • Subject pronouns (I, he, she, they, who) function as the sentence's subject; object pronouns (me, him, her, them, whom) function as objects.
  • 'Who' refers to the subject of a clause; 'whom' refers to the object; test by substituting he/him.
  • Possessive pronouns (its, their, whose) never take an apostrophe; 'it's' = 'it is,' 'who's' = 'who is.'

Verb Tense and Mood Consistency

Verbs across a sentence or passage should stay in a logically consistent tense unless the meaning requires a shift.
  • Don't shift from past to present tense mid-sentence without a reason tied to the timeline of events.
  • The passage's established time frame (signaled by dates or context) tells you which tense is correct throughout.
  • The subjunctive mood is used for hypotheticals/wishes contrary to fact: 'If I were you' (not 'was').
  • Perfect tenses (has/have/had + past participle) show an action completed relative to another point in time — used to show sequence of past events.
Matching a verb to a noun in an interrupting phrase instead of to the true subject, instead of to the true subject.
Joining two independent clauses with just a comma (comma splice) instead of a semicolon or comma+conjunction, instead of a semicolon or comma+conjunction.
Adding a comma before an essential 'that' clause that should have no comma at all, that should have no comma at all.

Unit 6: Science Passages5 concepts · ~23 min

▾

Hypothesis-Method-Findings Structure

Science passages typically follow a predictable structure: background/problem, hypothesis or research question, method, findings, and interpretation/implications.
  • Identify which structural stage each sentence belongs to as you read — this helps you locate answers fast.
  • The hypothesis is a prediction; the findings are what actually happened; don't confuse the two in answer choices.
  • Interpretation/implication sentences often use hedged language ('suggests,' 'may indicate') — treat these as less certain than stated findings.
  • A passage may describe a surprising or unexpected result — the 'why it matters' sentence usually follows immediately after.

Experimental Design Reasoning

Some questions test whether you understand what a study's design can and cannot prove, including variables and controls.
  • Identify the independent variable (what's manipulated) and dependent variable (what's measured) in the described experiment.
  • A control group isolates the effect of the independent variable by holding other conditions constant.
  • A correlational (observational) study cannot establish causation the way a controlled experiment can — watch for this distinction in answer choices.
  • Confounding variables are alternate explanations a weak experimental design fails to rule out.

Interpreting Data (Graphs, Tables) in Science Passages

Science passages are the most likely to pair text with quantitative data — reading the graph correctly is as important as reading the prose.
  • Check axis labels and units first; a common trap answer misreads the unit (e.g., confusing percentage with raw count).
  • Identify the trend (increasing, decreasing, plateauing, cyclical) before matching to a textual claim about it.
  • Outliers or exceptions in the data are frequently the basis of an 'except' or 'best supports' question.
  • When a question asks which data point would strengthen/weaken a hypothesis, check if the point is consistent with the DIRECTION the hypothesis predicts.

Technical Vocabulary in Context

Science passages often define an unfamiliar or technical term within the passage itself — you're tested on using that in-context definition, not outside knowledge.
  • The passage usually defines or illustrates a technical term nearby (an appositive, a following sentence, or an example) — use that instead of prior knowledge.
  • Don't let real-world science knowledge override what the specific passage states; the SAT tests reading comprehension, not science facts.
  • Words like 'significant' have a technical statistical meaning (unlikely due to chance) that differs from everyday 'important' — watch for this in science contexts.

Cause, Effect, and Mechanism Language

Science writing frequently explains WHY something happens (mechanism) as distinct from simply stating THAT it happens (observation).
  • Distinguish a stated observation ('X increased') from a proposed explanation for it ('researchers believe this occurred because...').
  • Multiple competing explanations may be presented — track which one the author actually endorses versus merely reports.
  • Look for signal phrases: 'due to,' 'as a result of,' 'this is thought to occur because' introduce mechanism explanations.

Assuming a correlational study proves causation when the passage doesn't claim that, when the passage doesn't claim that.
Relying on outside science knowledge instead of the passage's own stated definition of a term, instead of the passage's own stated definition of a term.

Unit 7: History/Social Studies Passages5 concepts · ~23 min

▾

Reading Primary and Secondary Sources

History/Social Studies passages include excerpts from historical documents (primary sources) and modern scholarly commentary (secondary sources) — often with older or more formal diction.
  • Don't let unfamiliar older vocabulary or syntax stop you — restate dense sentences in plain modern language as you read.
  • Identify who is speaking (the historical author) and what their stated position or purpose is.
  • A secondary-source passage analyzing a primary source will state its own claim about that source — separate the source's content from the analyst's interpretation.
  • Historical context clues (dates, named events, institutions) help you infer the stakes of an argument even without outside knowledge.

Argumentative Structure and Rhetoric

Many history/social studies passages are persuasive essays or speeches — understanding their argumentative moves is key.
  • Identify the claim, the reasons given, and any counterargument the author addresses or concedes.
  • A concession ('while it is true that...') followed by 'but/however' shows the author acknowledges an opposing point before refuting it.
  • Rhetorical questions, repetition, and appeals to shared values are persuasive techniques the SAT may ask you to identify by function.
  • Distinguish evidence (facts, examples, data) from the claim it supports — evidence questions often follow argument passages.

Historical Context and Author's Stance

Understanding the social or political moment referenced helps determine an author's stance and purpose, even from a short excerpt.
  • Look for framing words revealing stance: reformer, critic, advocate, skeptic — often stated directly in an introductory note.
  • The SAT provides any necessary historical background in the passage itself or a brief intro line — you never need outside historical knowledge to answer.
  • An author writing 'in response to' or 'in defense of' something signals the passage is reactive/persuasive rather than purely descriptive.

Paired Passages and Multiple Perspectives

Some questions present two short passages or two viewpoints on the same historical/social topic and ask how they relate.
  • Identify each passage's central claim independently before comparing them.
  • Determine whether the passages agree, directly disagree, or address different aspects of the same issue (a common 'nuance' trap).
  • Passage-comparison questions often ask how the author of Passage 2 would likely respond to a claim in Passage 1 — base this ONLY on stated views, not assumption.
  • Watch for a shared premise the passages agree on even while disagreeing on the conclusion.

Tone, Bias, and Persuasive Word Choice

History/Social Studies passages are evaluated for how word choice reveals the author's attitude toward the subject.
  • Loaded words (regime vs. government, scheme vs. plan) reveal bias — identify their connotation.
  • An objective/neutral tone uses precise, unemotional language even when describing controversial topics.
  • A passage can present a claim it ultimately criticizes — track whose viewpoint a sentence expresses before judging author tone.
Assuming outside historical knowledge is needed when the passage/intro already supplies the needed context, already supplies the needed context.
Mixing up the historical speaker's viewpoint with the modern author's analytical viewpoint, with the modern author's analytical viewpoint.
Treating two paired passages as fully agreeing when they only share one narrow premise, when they only share one narrow premise.

Unit 8: Pacing & Strategy5 concepts · ~23 min

▾

Module Timing and Adaptive Structure

The digital SAT Reading & Writing section has two modules of 27 questions each in 32 minutes (about 71 seconds/question), and module 2's difficulty adapts based on module 1 performance.
  • Aim for roughly 60-75 seconds per question on average; some questions (grammar) go faster, leaving buffer for longer reading questions.
  • Module 1 performance determines whether Module 2 is the harder or easier variant — there's no way to 'skip ahead,' so treat every Module 1 question with full effort.
  • Because it's adaptive, you cannot go back to a previous MODULE once submitted, but you CAN move between questions within the current module.
  • Track time at the quarter-marks (roughly question 7, 14, 20) to catch yourself falling behind early rather than at the end.

Question Triage: Skip and Flag

Not all questions deserve equal time investment — recognizing when to flag and move on protects your overall score.
  • If a question is taking more than ~90 seconds with no progress, flag it and move on; return at the end if time allows.
  • Grammar/convention questions are often the fastest points on the test — do not lose time on them by overanalyzing.
  • Long or dense passages with a straightforward detail question can be answered by scanning for a keyword instead of rereading everything.
  • Never leave a question blank on the digital SAT — there's no penalty for guessing, so always select an answer before time runs out.

Process of Elimination Under Time Pressure

Eliminating clearly wrong answers quickly is often faster than trying to 'prove' the right answer directly, especially under time pressure.
  • Cross off any answer that contradicts explicit text, uses extreme language unsupported by the passage, or is off-topic.
  • For grammar questions, first check if there's an obvious agreement/punctuation error in three of the four choices.
  • When down to two choices, reread the specific line(s) in question rather than the whole passage again.
  • Trust textual evidence over 'this sounds right' instinct — SAT wrong answers are designed to sound plausible.

Managing Reading Fatigue and Focus

With dozens of short, dense passages back-to-back, maintaining focus across the whole module affects accuracy more than raw reading speed.
  • Take a 2-3 second mental reset between questions — treat each passage as a fresh start, not a continuation of the last one's topic.
  • If you catch yourself re-reading the same sentence three times, it usually means you skimmed too fast the first time — slow down deliberately.
  • Save your slowest, most careful reading for rhetorical synthesis and inference questions — they reward precision most.
  • Physical resets (sit up, breathe, unclench) between passages help sustain concentration through a ~32-minute module.

End-of-Module Review Strategy

Digital SAT allows reviewing and changing answers within the current module before time expires or you submit.
  • Use the on-screen review page to confirm every question has an answer selected before submitting the module.
  • Revisit flagged questions first with remaining time, using fresh eyes rather than immediately re-reading your first attempt's reasoning.
  • Don't second-guess grammar answers you were confident about on first pass — changing answers without new evidence often hurts more than helps.
  • If time is nearly out, guess strategically on remaining flagged items rather than leaving any blank.
Spending too long perfecting an early hard question and running out of time for easier later ones, and running out of time for easier later ones.
Leaving a question blank instead of guessing when time runs low, instead of guessing when time runs low.
Changing a confident grammar answer during review without any new textual reason, without any new textual reason.
Rereading an entire passage from scratch instead of scanning for the specific line a question references, instead of scanning for the specific line a question references.

Weaken a Claim
To provide a fact that introduces doubt, an exception, or an alternative explanation for the claim.
Correlation vs. Causation
Two variables changing together (correlation) does not by itself prove one causes the other (causation).
Irrelevant Evidence Trap
A true, on-topic fact that doesn't actually address or prove the specific claim being tested.
Direction of Data
Whether a data trend is increasing, decreasing, or stable — must match the direction claimed in the text.
Sample Size Issue
A data-reasoning trap where a claim about a large group is 'proven' using data from too small or narrow a sample.
Reversed Logic Trap
An answer choice that supports the opposite of what the question actually asks for.

Unit 3: Words in Context

Words in Context
Question type asking for the meaning of a word/phrase as used in a specific sentence, or the best word to fill a blank.
Cover-and-Predict
Strategy of covering answer choices and predicting your own word for the blank before comparing to options.
Connotation
The emotional or attitudinal association of a word (positive, negative, neutral) beyond its literal definition.
Context Clue
A word or phrase nearby in the sentence that reveals the meaning of the blank or targeted word.
Contrast Transition
Words like 'however,' 'yet,' 'although' that signal the blank should mean the opposite of a nearby idea.
Continuation Transition
Words like 'moreover,' 'similarly,' 'furthermore' that signal the blank should reinforce a nearby idea.
Multiple-Meaning Word
A word with more than one definition (e.g., 'sound,' 'novel') where context determines which meaning applies.
Register
The level of formality (academic, casual, technical) a word choice must match to fit the passage.
Precision Trap
A wrong choice that is a near-synonym but has the wrong degree of intensity for the sentence's context.
Tone Word
A word whose primary function is signaling the writer's or speaker's attitude, e.g., 'skeptical' or 'admiring.'

Unit 4: Expression of Ideas

Rhetorical Synthesis
Using given bullet-point notes to construct the sentence that best fulfills a specific stated rhetorical goal.
Transition Word
A word or phrase showing the logical relationship (contrast, cause, addition) between two ideas or sentences.
Concision
Writing that expresses an idea in the fewest necessary words without losing meaning or grammatical correctness.
Redundancy
Unnecessary repetition of an idea already stated, e.g., 'return back' or 'each individual person.'
Sentence Purpose
The specific function (e.g., 'to illustrate a contrast') a question asks a sentence to serve — the basis for add/delete questions.
Subordinating Conjunction
A word like 'although,' 'because,' or 'while' that joins a dependent clause to an independent clause.
Coordinating Conjunction (FANBOYS)
For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So — joins two independent, equally weighted clauses.
Misplaced Modifier
A descriptive phrase positioned so it appears to modify the wrong word in the sentence.
Cause-Effect Transition
Words like 'therefore,' 'thus,' or 'consequently' signaling one idea results from another.
Off-Goal Trap
An answer that is factually accurate but doesn't accomplish the specific goal stated in the question.

Unit 5: Standard English Conventions

Subject-Verb Agreement
The rule that a verb must match its true subject in number, ignoring intervening prepositional phrases.
Comma Splice
An error joining two independent clauses with only a comma, e.g., 'It rained, we left.'
Run-On Sentence
Two independent clauses joined with no punctuation or conjunction at all.
Fragment
A group of words missing a subject or verb, or a subordinate clause standing alone, that cannot stand as a complete sentence.
Essential (Restrictive) Clause
A 'that' clause defining which specific noun is meant; takes no surrounding commas.
Nonessential (Nonrestrictive) Clause
A 'which' clause adding extra, non-defining information; set off by commas.
Parallel Structure
The rule that items in a list, comparison, or joined by a conjunction must share the same grammatical form.
Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement
A pronoun must match its antecedent in number and gender, and refer unambiguously to one noun.
Subject vs. Object Pronoun Case
Subject pronouns (I, he, they, who) act as the subject; object pronouns (me, him, them, whom) act as the object.
Colon Rule
A colon must follow a complete independent clause and introduces a list, explanation, or elaboration.

Unit 6: Science Passages

Hypothesis
A researcher's testable prediction about an outcome, distinct from the actual findings of a study.
Independent Variable
The factor a researcher deliberately manipulates in an experiment.
Dependent Variable
The outcome or measurement that changes in response to the independent variable.
Control Group
A comparison group that does not receive the treatment, used to isolate the effect of the independent variable.
Correlational Study
Research observing a relationship between variables without manipulating them, which cannot establish causation alone.
Confounding Variable
An unaccounted-for factor that offers an alternative explanation for an observed result.
Statistical Significance
A technical term meaning a result is unlikely to be due to random chance, distinct from everyday 'important.'
Findings vs. Interpretation
Findings are what a study observed; interpretation is the researcher's explanation of why it happened.
Mechanism
The explanation for HOW or WHY an observed scientific effect occurs, often signaled by 'because' or 'due to.'
Outlier
A data point that doesn't fit the overall trend, often the focus of an 'except' or exception-based question.

Unit 7: History/Social Studies Passages

Primary Source
A firsthand historical document or account written by someone present at or involved in the events described.
Secondary Source
A modern or later analysis/commentary about a primary source or historical event.
Concession
An author's acknowledgment of a valid opposing point, often introduced with 'while it is true that...'
Counterargument
An opposing viewpoint an author raises, typically in order to refute or qualify it.
Loaded Language
Word choice carrying strong positive or negative connotation that reveals bias, e.g., 'regime' vs. 'government.'
Author's Stance
An author's overall position or attitude toward the subject, inferable from framing and word choice.
Paired Passages
Two related passages presenting different perspectives on the same topic, compared for agreement/disagreement.
Rhetorical Question
A question posed for persuasive effect, not to elicit an actual answer.
Shared Premise
A point of agreement between two passages that otherwise reach different conclusions.
Historical Context Clue
Dates, named events, or institutions in a passage that reveal the stakes of an argument without needing outside knowledge.

Digital SAT tool marking a question to revisit later within the same module before submitting.

Systematically ruling out unsupported, extreme, or off-topic choices rather than searching for a 'perfect' answer directly.

Deciding quickly which questions to answer immediately, flag, or guess-and-move-on based on difficulty and time.

The digital SAT's end-of-module screen showing which questions are answered, unanswered, or flagged.

A self-imposed pacing check (e.g., at question 7, 14, 20) to confirm you're on schedule within a module.

Unit 1: Information & Ideas

Central Ideas and Main Purpose
Every SAT Reading passage (usually 25-150 words) has one controlling idea that everything else supports. The central-idea question asks what the passage as a whole is mainly about or trying to accomplish, not what any single sentence says.
Supporting Details
Detail questions ask you to locate specific information the passage explicitly states, often phrased 'According to the text' or 'Based on the passage.'
Making Inferences
Inference questions ask what the passage suggests or implies without stating directly. The correct inference is a small, logical step from the text — never a leap.
Author's Purpose and Perspective
Purpose questions ask WHY the author included a sentence, detail, or the whole passage — to inform, persuade, illustrate, qualify, or contrast.
Summarizing and Paraphrasing
Some questions ask which choice best restates a sentence or passage's content in different words — testing whether you tracked meaning, not just vocabulary.
Reading Data with Text (Informational Graphics)
Some Information & Ideas passages pair a short text with a table or graph; you must connect what the text claims to what the data actually shows.
Key fact
Read the full short passage before touching the answer choices — SAT Reading passages are dense, and the first sentence rarely contains the whole main idea.
Key fact
Predict an answer in your own words before reading the choices; this prevents being talked into a trap answer.
Key fact
Every correct answer on Information & Ideas questions must be directly supported by the passage text — never by outside knowledge.
Key fact
Process of elimination beats searching for the 'perfect' answer — cross out anything unsupported, extreme, or off-topic first.

Unit 2: Command of Evidence

Textual Evidence Questions
These paired-format questions ask which quotation or line from the passage best supports the answer to a previous question, or best supports a given claim.
Quantitative Evidence (Graphs & Tables)
Command of Evidence also includes questions where you must pick the data point, from a table or graph, that best supports or completes an argument in the passage.
Supporting vs. Undermining a Claim
Some questions give a hypothesis or claim and ask which fact, if true, would most support it or most weaken it.
Selecting the Best Evidence Among Similar Options
When two or more answer choices seem to relate to the correct claim, the SAT rewards precision — the tightest logical fit wins.
Common Data-Reasoning Traps
The digital SAT tests whether you can reason carefully about data, not just read a chart.
Key fact
Always answer the underlying question first, then match it to evidence — never evidence-shop before you know your claim.
Key fact
The correct evidence choice proves the SPECIFIC claim asked about, not just a related idea from the same passage.
Key fact
For data questions, identify the exact variables and direction (more/less, before/after) the claim needs before scanning the graph.
Key fact
When strengthening/weakening a claim, decide the logical relationship first, then find the fact that matches it.

Unit 3: Words in Context

The Cover-and-Predict Strategy
Words-in-Context questions ask you to choose the word/phrase that best completes a sentence with a blank, or to determine the meaning of a word as used in the passage.
Precise Word Choice and Connotation
Many wrong choices are near-synonyms with the wrong connotation (positive/negative/neutral) or wrong intensity for the context.
Transitions as Context Clues
Logical transition words inside or around the blank sentence often ARE the context clue that determines the correct word.
Multiple-Meaning Words
Some tested words are common but have a less-common secondary meaning that fits the specific passage context.
Tone and Register
Words-in-Context also tests whether a word fits the formality and attitude of the passage as a whole (academic, journalistic, personal narrative).
Key fact
Always predict your own word before reading the answer choices — this prevents being misled by attractive-sounding wrong options.
Key fact
Context clues (contrast/continuation/cause-effect transitions) in the same or nearby sentence determine the correct meaning.
Key fact
Match connotation and intensity, not just literal meaning — many wrong choices are 'close but too strong/weak.'
Key fact
For multiple-meaning words, always derive meaning from the sentence's specific context, never from the word's most common definition.

Unit 4: Expression of Ideas

Rhetorical Synthesis
You're given bulleted notes on a topic and a stated goal (e.g., 'to compare two findings'), then asked which sentence best accomplishes that goal using only the given information.
Transitions (Logical Relationships)
Transition questions ask you to pick the word/phrase that correctly signals the logical relationship between two sentences or clauses.
Concision and Redundancy
The SAT rewards clear, economical writing — when all else is equal, the most concise grammatically correct option is best.
Adding, Revising, or Deleting a Sentence
These questions ask whether a proposed sentence should be added (or is effectively added) based on whether it fulfills a stated purpose.
Sentence Combining and Structure
Some questions ask you to combine two short sentences effectively, testing subordination, coordination, and modifier placement.
Key fact
For rhetorical synthesis, reread the stated goal verb before picking — many wrong answers are true but off-goal.
Key fact
Determine the logical relationship (contrast, cause, addition) between ideas before selecting a transition word.
Key fact
Prefer the most concise, grammatically correct option, but never sacrifice correctness for brevity.
Key fact
For add/delete/revise questions, judge choices against the stated purpose, not just factual accuracy.

Unit 5: Standard English Conventions

Subject-Verb Agreement
The verb must agree in number with its TRUE subject, even when other words or phrases sit between them.
Commas, Semicolons, and Colons
Punctuation questions test whether you know exactly what structure each mark requires.
Comma Splices, Run-Ons, and Fragments
One of the most heavily tested conventions rules: recognizing when two clauses are improperly joined or a group of words isn't a complete sentence.
Parallel Structure
Items in a list, comparison, or joined by a conjunction must share the same grammatical form.
Pronoun Agreement and Case
Pronouns must agree in number and gender with their antecedent, and must use the correct case (subject/object/possessive).
Verb Tense and Mood Consistency
Verbs across a sentence or passage should stay in a logically consistent tense unless the meaning requires a shift.
Key fact
Always find the TRUE subject of a verb by ignoring interrupting prepositional phrases before checking agreement.
Key fact
A comma alone can never join two independent clauses — that's a comma splice, one of the most common SAT errors.
Key fact
Essential (that/no commas) vs. nonessential (which/commas) clauses is a frequently tested distinction.
Key fact
Items joined in a list or by correlative conjunctions must share identical grammatical form (parallel structure).

Unit 6: Science Passages

Hypothesis-Method-Findings Structure
Science passages typically follow a predictable structure: background/problem, hypothesis or research question, method, findings, and interpretation/implications.
Experimental Design Reasoning
Some questions test whether you understand what a study's design can and cannot prove, including variables and controls.
Interpreting Data (Graphs, Tables) in Science Passages
Science passages are the most likely to pair text with quantitative data — reading the graph correctly is as important as reading the prose.
Technical Vocabulary in Context
Science passages often define an unfamiliar or technical term within the passage itself — you're tested on using that in-context definition, not outside knowledge.
Cause, Effect, and Mechanism Language
Science writing frequently explains WHY something happens (mechanism) as distinct from simply stating THAT it happens (observation).
Key fact
Science passages follow background → hypothesis → method → findings → interpretation; locate answers by structural stage.
Key fact
Never substitute outside science knowledge for the passage's own in-context definitions or findings.
Key fact
Correlational data alone cannot prove causation — a frequent basis for 'weakens the claim' answers.
Key fact
Always check graph axis units and labels before matching data to a textual claim.

Unit 7: History/Social Studies Passages

Reading Primary and Secondary Sources
History/Social Studies passages include excerpts from historical documents (primary sources) and modern scholarly commentary (secondary sources) — often with older or more formal diction.
Argumentative Structure and Rhetoric
Many history/social studies passages are persuasive essays or speeches — understanding their argumentative moves is key.
Historical Context and Author's Stance
Understanding the social or political moment referenced helps determine an author's stance and purpose, even from a short excerpt.
Paired Passages and Multiple Perspectives
Some questions present two short passages or two viewpoints on the same historical/social topic and ask how they relate.
Tone, Bias, and Persuasive Word Choice
History/Social Studies passages are evaluated for how word choice reveals the author's attitude toward the subject.
Key fact
You never need outside historical knowledge — all context necessary to answer is in the passage or its intro line.
Key fact
Separate what a quoted historical figure says from what the passage's own analysis says about them.
Key fact
In paired passages, determine each passage's claim independently before comparing agreement/disagreement.
Key fact
Identify claim → reason → counterargument/concession structure in persuasive history passages.

Unit 8: Pacing & Strategy

Module Timing and Adaptive Structure
The digital SAT Reading & Writing section has two modules of 27 questions each in 32 minutes (about 71 seconds/question), and module 2's difficulty adapts based on module 1 performance.
Question Triage: Skip and Flag
Not all questions deserve equal time investment — recognizing when to flag and move on protects your overall score.
Process of Elimination Under Time Pressure
Eliminating clearly wrong answers quickly is often faster than trying to 'prove' the right answer directly, especially under time pressure.
Managing Reading Fatigue and Focus
With dozens of short, dense passages back-to-back, maintaining focus across the whole module affects accuracy more than raw reading speed.
End-of-Module Review Strategy
Digital SAT allows reviewing and changing answers within the current module before time expires or you submit.
Key fact
Digital SAT R&W has 2 modules, 27 questions each, 32 minutes each — average pace is about 60-75 seconds per question.
Key fact
Module 2's difficulty is determined by Module 1 performance; you cannot revisit a module once it's submitted.
Key fact
There is no guessing penalty — never leave any question blank.
Key fact
Flag genuinely hard questions and return to them at the end rather than burning time on a single item early.
